Most people skip it. The little line of small print that sits above a psalm, before the psalm itself begins, the part that says something like "To the choirmaster" and then a phrase that makes no obvious sense. Our eyes slide past it to get to the good part, the actual song.

Do not slide past it this time. Because sitting up there, in the header of some of the most beloved songs in the Scriptures, are a handful of strange little phrases, and they are, in all likelihood, the ghosts of melodies. Tune names. The ancient equivalent of writing *"(to the tune of a song everybody already knows)"* at the top of a hymn.

And we have lost the tunes.

## Read the small print

Let me show you the actual phrases, because they are lovely even stripped of their music.

Over one psalm stands the phrase usually rendered *"The Doe of the Dawn"*, or the hind of the morning. Over others: *"Lilies."* Over several: *"Do Not Destroy."* There are more of these scattered through the collection, headings that speak of a *silent dove far away,* of instruments and settings with names like *Gittith* and *Sheminith* and *Mahalath,* and the recurring word for the one who led the music, the choirmaster or chief musician.

Now, I have to be a careful teacher here, and I want you to trust me precisely because I am careful.

**The headings themselves are in the text.** They are printed in the Hebrew manuscripts, above the psalms, and translators carry them across. That much is not in dispute.

But **what they mean is largely a matter of tradition and scholarship, not certainty.** When I tell you "The Doe of the Dawn" was probably a tune, I am telling you the best judgment of people who have spent their lives on this, not a fact delivered from the text itself. The most common and reasonable understanding is that phrases like these named popular melodies of the day: *sing this psalm to the tune called "The Doe of the Dawn," to the tune called "Lilies," to the tune called "Do Not Destroy."* But we are reading over the shoulders of a musical culture that did not write its melodies down the way we do, and so the tunes themselves are simply gone.

## What it means that a melody can die

Sit with that for a moment, because it is stranger and sadder than it first sounds.

Somewhere, long ago, there was a melody so well known that a poet could name it in a single phrase and every worshiper in Israel would immediately hear it in their head. *"The Doe of the Dawn"*, and they *knew* it. It was in the air the way a familiar song is in the air for us. The composer of the psalm did not need to supply the tune any more than I would need to supply the tune if I told you to sing new words to "Amazing Grace." Everyone had it already.

And then a generation passed who half-remembered it. And then a generation who had only heard of it. And then no one. The melody that once needed no explanation became a phrase that needs a footnote. The tune walked out of the world and left only its name behind, hovering over a song like the outline of something that used to be there.

> We kept every word of the psalm and lost the music it rode in on. The lyrics were carved into the memory of a people; the melody was written on the air.

That is the fragility of a tune. Words can be fixed in a text and preserved for three thousand years. But a melody in a culture that carries its songs by heart lives only as long as the last person who can hum it, and when that person is gone, the tune is gone, and no manuscript can bring it back.

## Why the loss is not only a loss

Here is where I want to lift your eyes, because I do not think this ends in mourning.

Notice *what* survived and what did not. The tunes vanished. The **words** did not. The melody of "The Doe of the Dawn" is lost beyond recovery, but every syllable of the psalm sung to it is still in your hands, exactly as it was. The Spirit-breathed words were kept with perfect care across the centuries; the human melody, precious as it was, was allowed to fade.

I find that ordering deeply reassuring. It tells me something about what Yahuah insisted on preserving and what He was willing to let go. The tune was the beautiful, local, temporary vessel, and it did its job for its own generation. The word was the treasure, and the treasure was kept.

And there is a second comfort. The very fact that these psalms were *set to tunes at all*, that they carried tune names, choirmaster's notes, instrument settings, tells us plainly that these songs were never meant to be read in silence off a page. They were living music, sung by real congregations to melodies everyone knew, led by a real choirmaster, accompanied by real strings. The headers are the fingerprints of a worshiping people who *sang* their Scriptures. We inherited the words. We were always meant to give them our own music.

## Sing them a new tune

So do not grieve the lost melodies too long. There is something we are quietly invited to do about them.

Every generation that has ever loved these psalms has done the same thing: taken the words whose original tunes were long gone, and set them to the music of its own day. The "Doe of the Dawn" melody is lost, but the psalm it carried has been sung to a thousand tunes since, in a thousand tongues, in cathedrals and cane fields and living rooms. The word kept finding new music. It always does.

That, I think, is the honest and hopeful truth hidden in that small print you were about to skip. We lost the ancient tunes because a melody is a fragile, mortal, beautiful thing that lives on the breath of the people who love it. But the words outlived every melody they were ever set to, and they are still here, still waiting, still asking the same thing they asked the choirmaster three thousand years ago.

Not to be read. To be sung. Give them your tune.
