If you sit down and read the book of Judges straight through, you will hit a moment that looks, at first, like an editing mistake. A great battle is described, a general summoned, an army mustered, an enemy commander routed, a war won. It is told clearly, in ordinary narrative prose: this happened, then that happened, and the land had rest. And then you turn the page and the whole thing happens again. The same battle, the same people, the same enemy swept away, but this time it is not a report. It is a song. Two full chapters, one event, told twice. First the facts. Then the melody.

That is the deliverance under Deborah. In the first telling, we learn the bare shape of it: Deborah, a prophetess who judged Israel from beneath a palm tree, sends for a man named Barak and gives him God's word to march. The enemy commander Sisera comes against them with iron chariots, and he is broken, and he flees on foot, and he dies in a tent at the hand of a woman named Jael, who does what no soldier managed. The oppression ends. The land has quiet for a generation. If all you wanted was the information, the story is now complete. You could close the book.

But Israel did not close the book. Israel wrote it again, and the second telling is one of the oldest pieces of poetry in the whole Bible, old enough that scholars who spend their lives on such things believe its Hebrew is nearly as ancient as anything preserved anywhere in Scripture. *Then Deborah and Barak sang, on that day.* And I want to ask the question the double telling forces on us: **why keep both?** Why, once you have the facts written down safely, would a people take the trouble to set the same events to music and preserve that too, side by side, forever?

## What the song remembers that the report cannot

Read the two versions next to each other and you feel the answer more than you can argue it. The prose gives you the skeleton, who, what, where, the sequence, the outcome. The song gives you the *body*: the fury, the trembling, the pride, the ache, everything a plain report has to leave on the floor.

The song remembers the sky. It says the very stars fought from their courses, that the ancient river Kishon swept the enemy away, as if the whole created order had risen up on Israel's side, which is a thing a battlefield dispatch can never say and a song can barely contain. The song remembers who showed up and who did not, naming the tribes that risked their lives on the heights and the tribes that stayed home among the sheepfolds and never came, calling them out by name centuries after the fact, because a song keeps its accounts. The song even remembers the enemy's mother, pictures her at her window, peering down the road, wondering why her son's chariot is so long in returning, counting out to her ladies the plunder she is sure he is dividing. It is an almost unbearable image of grief on the losing side, and no war record on earth would have thought to keep it. A song did. A song had room for the sorrow of the enemy's mother.

That is what melody does that a report cannot. It carries the weight of a thing. It keeps not only what happened but what it *felt like* to be alive when it happened, the terror before the deliverance and the wild relief after, the shame of the absent and the honor of the brave. Facts tell you that Israel was rescued. The song makes you stand in the field and feel your knees go weak and then your chest fill. And here is the deep Hebrew instinct behind the whole thing: this people understood that a nation forgets its records and remembers its songs. Ledgers get shelved. Songs get sung, at the fire, over the children, on the road, and by being sung, they refuse to die.

## The song that wakes the singer up

There is a line in Deborah's song where she turns and calls to herself. *Awake, awake,* she sings, *awake, awake, and sing.* She is a prophetess and a judge, a woman who has already carried the word of God and watched it come true, and still she has to summon herself to sing it. I find that enormously comforting, because it means even the great ones do not float effortlessly into praise. The song is a command she gives her own soul: wake up. Do not let this pass by unsung. Do not file this deliverance under *facts* and move on. Rouse yourself and put it to music, or you will lose the heart of it even while you keep the record of it.

We need that command more than we know. Something good happens, a real rescue, a bill paid we could not pay, a door that finally opened, a night we truly thought we would not survive and did, and our instinct is to note it and hurry on. We keep the fact. We lose the song. And a deliverance you only file is a deliverance that slowly goes cold in you, until one day you can no longer feel why it mattered. Deborah's answer to that danger is to shake herself awake and sing the thing while the feeling is still hot, and to make the song durable enough that her grandchildren will still feel the heat of it long after the details have blurred.

## Ending on the sunrise

Listen to where the whole thing lands. After all the chariots and the river and the tent peg and the weeping mother at the window, the song does not end on the wreckage of the enemy. It lifts its eyes and asks that all who love Yahuah would be like the sun when it rises in its strength, climbing, warming, unstoppable, a light that floods a whole valley and cannot be argued with. That is how a people who had lived under twenty years of oppression chose to close their memory of the day it broke: not with a gloat, but with a sunrise. The song reaches past the one battle and blesses everyone who will ever love God with the image of the morning coming up in full force.

That is worship remembering rightly. And it tells us what to do with our own deliverances. Do not only record them. Sing them. Take the night you survived and set it to a melody your children can carry. Keep the facts, yes, but do not let the facts be all you keep, because the facts will not warm anyone at two in the morning and the song will. The same God who split the sky over Deborah's field is still rescuing people who will not otherwise remember it, and He has given us the oldest and most durable memory-keeper there is. Wake up, the prophetess still sings across three thousand years. Wake up, and sing. And let the way you remember your rescue be the sun coming up in its strength over everyone still waiting for theirs.
