Of all the strange commands in the Torah, this may be the one that would get you the most confused looks if you tried to explain it to a stranger. Once a year, for seven days, Yahuah tells His people to move out of their good, solid, weatherproof houses, and to go live in a *hut.* A flimsy little shelter of branches, walls that shift in the wind, and a roof you are meant to leave loose enough that you can see the stars through it.

And here is the part that undoes the logic entirely: this is not a fast. It is not a mourning. It is called *the season of our joy.* It is the most exuberant feast on the whole calendar, singing, dancing, a full harvest brought in, tables loaded. And He houses all of that gladness in the one structure on the property that can barely hold itself up.

Why? Why would the God of the universe insist that His people's happiest week be spent in their weakest walls?

## The word is sukkah

The feast is **Sukkot**, the Feast of Booths, or Tabernacles. The hut has a name: a **sukkah** (plural, *sukkot*). It means exactly what it looks like, a temporary shelter, a booth, a thing thrown up quickly out of what the land gives you. Branches. Leaves. Nothing permanent. Nothing you'd trust in a real storm.

And Scripture gives the reason plainly. Yahuah tells Israel to dwell in booths for seven days so that every generation that comes after will *know*, will feel in their own bodies, that He made their ancestors dwell in booths when He brought them out of the land of Egypt. That is the whole design. It is a memory you move into. For one week you are not told *about* the wilderness years. You are made to *live* them.

Forty years, Israel dwelt in booths. Forty years, no house, no field of their own, no roof that stayed put. They pulled up stakes when the cloud moved and set them down when it stopped, and every night they slept under walls that could not defend them from anything. The sukkah is a small, deliberate return to that. A yearly week of remembered fragility.

> The permanent house is very good at one thing above all others: it helps you forget who kept you alive before you had it.

## What the solid walls quietly erase

Think about what a house does to your sense of things. Not a bad house, a *good* one. It is a mercy and a gift. But a good house is also a very effective forgetting machine.

Behind good walls, provision stops feeling like provision. The bread in the cupboard feels like *yours.* The warmth feels like a fact of the world rather than a gift renewed each day. Slowly, without any decision on your part, your dependence becomes invisible to you, not because it ended, but because the walls hid it. You are being carried every single moment, and the roof over your head is precisely the thing that keeps you from noticing.

So once a year, He takes the roof off. Not to frighten His people. To *free* them from the forgetting. Under a sukkah, with the branches loose over your head and the night air coming through and the first stars visible when you look up, you cannot pretend you are the reason you are safe. The walls will not let you. The whole structure is a confession you sleep inside: *I did not build the world that keeps me. I never did.*

And the astonishing thing, the thing that turns the whole feast on its head, is that this is where the joy lives. Not in spite of the fragile walls. *Because* of them. Joy that has to hold itself up is exhausting. Joy that rests in being held is the deepest joy there is. Israel was commanded to be glad in the sukkah because gladness is what honest dependence actually feels like once you stop fighting it.

## Fragility is not the enemy you think it is

I want to say this gently to whoever needs it, because I suspect someone reading this is living in a season that already feels like a sukkah they did not choose. The walls came off without your permission. The provision you counted on got thin. You can see too many stars through the roof lately, and it does not feel like a feast. It feels like exposure.

Hear the old command underneath the harder one. He does not put His people in booths to abandon them. He puts them there to be *known* by them. The wilderness was not the place Israel was most in danger. Read it again. It was the place they were most *carried.* Their clothes did not wear out. Bread fell in the morning. Water came out of stone. The desert was not the season of Yahuah's absence. It was the season His presence had nowhere to hide.

The solid years are a gift. But it is the fragile years that teach you the thing you will need forever: that you were never held up by your walls. You were held up by Him. And the sukkah exists so that a people who now live in houses will never, ever forget the God who kept them when they had none.

## The One who moved into a booth of His own

There is a reason this feast leans so far forward toward joy, and it is not only the harvest.

Centuries after the branches were first cut, John wrote of Yeshua that the Word became flesh and *dwelt* among us, and the word he reached for means He *tabernacled*, He pitched His tent, He set up His sukkah in the middle of us. The God who once made His people live in booths in the wilderness came and did it Himself. He took on walls that could be hurt, that could hunger, that could be struck down. He moved into fragility on purpose, right beside us, so that we would never again doubt that He knows exactly what it is to live under a roof with gaps in it.

And it was at this very feast, Scripture tells us, that Yeshua stood up in the middle of the celebration and cried out that anyone who was thirsty should come to Him and drink. The One who tabernacled among us is the water the whole wilderness was pointing at.

So when your roof feels thin this season, when you can see too much sky and not enough certainty, remember what the sukkah was always saying. The gaps are not the failure of the shelter. **The gaps are the window.** They are there so that when you look up from your weakest walls, the first thing you see is the sky He is keeping you under, and the God who moved in beside you rather than watch you dwell out here alone.

Sit down at the table anyway. Look up through the branches. This is not exposure. It is the season of your joy.
