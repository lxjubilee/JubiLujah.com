I want to tell you about a song you have probably never heard, that has been sung more times, by more people, over more centuries, than almost any melody on earth, and it has been sung mostly by children.

Not children in a choir. Children at a dinner table. Little ones with sleepy eyes and honey on their fingers, leaning against a grandfather, catching a tune before they can possibly understand the words. That is where this song lives. Not in a sanctuary, not in a concert hall. Around a table, on one particular night, in the mouths of a family.

Let me tell you what it is, because once you know, you will never picture Passover as a quiet meal again.

## The feast came with a score

Most of us, when we imagine the first Passover, imagine urgency and silence, blood on the doorposts, a meal eaten standing up, sandals on, the terror of the night going by outside. And that first night was exactly that.

But Passover was never meant to stay a one-night emergency. Yahuah told His people to *keep* it, year after year, forever, and over the generations the feast grew a shape, an order, a rhythm. And woven all the way through that rhythm was song. Not as decoration. As the very structure of the evening.

There is a cluster of psalms near the end of the songbook that begins and rings with the great shout *Hallelu-Yah*, "praise Yahuah." The tradition gathered them together and called them simply the *Hallel*, which means *praise*. And for as far back as the memory of the people reaches, this was the music of the Passover table. Part of it sung before the meal. The rest of it sung after. The feast of freedom, bracketed on both ends by praise.

Think about what that means. A Jewish family does not merely *remember* the rescue at Passover. They *sing* it. The night has a soundtrack, and every family knows it by heart, and they pass it down the way you pass down a face.

## A song older than any of the singers

Here is what undoes me about the Hallel at the seder.

When a family sings it, no one at that table wrote it. No one at that table is the first to sing it. The grandmother learned it from her grandmother, who learned it in another country, in another century, in another language of exile, and back and back, a chain of voices with no visible end. The child mumbling along tonight is holding a note that was struck long before he was born and will still be ringing long after he is gone.

That is a kind of belonging most of us ache for and rarely feel. To open your mouth and have three thousand years of your own people open their mouths *with* you. To sing something that is not yours and is entirely yours at the same time.

> You were not the first to sing it, and you will not be the last. That is not a smaller thing than an original song. It is a far greater one.

And the words they are singing are not small words. The Hallel remembers a God who lifts the poor out of the dust and seats them with princes. It calls on everyone who fears Yahuah to say, over and over, like a heartbeat that will not quit, that His steadfast love endures forever. It sings of a soul caught in the cords of death and distress, and then heard, and then rescued, and then returned to its rest. It asks what a person could possibly give back to God for all His goodness, and answers: lift the cup of salvation, and pay your vows.

A family sings all of that. Every year. Slaves' descendants, singing that the God who freed their fathers is still the God who frees. The song is a witness the empire could never confiscate, because you cannot burn a melody that lives in a child's chest.

## Why a feast must be sung and not only said

You could, in theory, simply *read* the story of the Exodus aloud and be done. But Yahuah did not build His feasts to be read. He built them to be tasted, and smelled, and, above all, sung.

Because a sung truth goes somewhere a spoken truth cannot reach.

I have watched it my whole life. A person can forget a sermon by the parking lot. But a song they learned at four years old will come back to them at eighty, whole, with every word intact, when their own name is beginning to slip. Melody is the safest place to hide something you never want a people to lose. And Yahuah, who knows exactly how He made us, hid the memory of the rescue not in a lecture but in a *song*, and then commanded the song be sung around a table where children could catch it before they could analyze it.

That is not a lesser way to teach. It is the deepest way there is. You *inherit* the tune long before you understand it, and then the understanding rises up to meet the tune you already carry. The song holds the theology in trust until the child is old enough to open it.

## The One who kept the feast, and sang

Now hold all of that, the ancient song, the table, the family, the praise bracketing the meal, and set one particular seder inside it.

On the last night before He was betrayed, Yeshua kept the Passover with His friends. He broke the bread and passed the cup. And the Gospels tell us, almost in passing, that when the supper was over, they sang a hymn, and went out. Anyone who knew that table knew what that hymn was. It was the last stretch of the very Hallel that every family had sung for generations. He kept the feast. He sang the songs of the feast.

But here is the thing that turns the whole tradition inside out.

For three thousand years, families have sung the Hallel *about* a rescue Yahuah once did, a strong hand, an outstretched arm, a sea that opened. And into the middle of that recurring song walked the One the whole feast had been quietly pointing at the entire time. The Lamb the doorposts remembered. The salvation the cup was lifted to. When those old praises sang of a stone the builders rejected becoming the cornerstone, of the day Yahuah has made, of the one who comes in His name. They were singing His face before anyone at the table could see it.

So the song your grandmother's grandmother sang was never only a memory. It was a hope with a melody. And the hope has a name now.

## Sing the part you were given

You do not have to write a new song to belong to this. That is the tenderest thing about the Hallel at the table. You were handed a part. Your job is simply to open your mouth and take up the note the ones before you carried, and to sing it clearly enough that the little one leaning on your arm can catch it too.

The feast of freedom comes with a songbook. It always has. And every voice that joins the old praise, freed slave, grafted stranger, sleepy child, you, is another verse in a rescue song that is still, right now, being sung around a table somewhere, waiting for morning.

Take up your part. Someone before you kept it warm for you. Someone after you is listening for you to start.
