The monks who walked the islands off the coast of Scotland and Ireland had a phrase for it that they never bothered to explain, because everyone who had felt it already knew.

They called them *thin places.* Moments and locations where the distance between heaven and the ordinary world seems to shrink to almost nothing, where, as the old saying goes, the veil grows so transparent you could almost put your hand through it. A hush after a service. A river in the rain. A headland at dawn. The air goes lucid, and you know, with your whole body and not just your mind, that you are not alone. They did not build a system out of it. They simply named the ground and said: *here is a doorway; listen for the sound.*

I have given my life to the wonder of how the holy draws near, and that little phrase reorganized everything for me. Because it means the barrier between us and God was never what we assumed. We picture a wall, thick, permanent, sealed. But the monks were describing something else entirely. Something that *thins.* Something that, in the right moment, you can feel *trembling.*

## A membrane, not a wall

Here is the image I could not escape, and it is the one the album is built on.

A wall does not move. But a membrane, a drum-skin, an eardrum, the thin surface of water, a membrane *trembles* when a sound passes through it. That is, in fact, the whole physics of hearing: a sound reaches you only because a wave crosses the air and sets a thin membrane vibrating. The barrier and the medium of contact are the *same surface.* The thing that separates is the exact thing that lets the sound through.

And I began to suspect that the veil between heaven and earth is far more like a membrane than a wall. In the song at the center of this record, "The Veil Trembling," I put it as plainly as I could, *the veil is not a curtain of impossible thick cloth. It is a thin trembling membrane that the Spirit can flow off.* Not a barricade. A drum-skin. Something built precisely so that Presence can pass through it and set the whole thing ringing.

This is not sentiment. It is what happened at the very center of the story. When **Yeshua** gave up His breath on the cross, *the curtain of the temple was torn in two from top to bottom* (Matthew 27:51), the thick veil that had walled off the Holy of Holies, torn open, top down, by a hand that was clearly not human. The letter to the Hebrews says He opened *a new and living way for us through the curtain* (Hebrews 10:19-20). The wall we thought was permanent turned out to be a thing that could be rent. He tore the cloth Himself, as I sing it, *that I might see His face.*

## The title is a map of where He shows up

I called the album **Thin Places** because I wanted to teach the ear where to listen, and because the answer surprised even me.

You would expect thin places to be dramatic. And some of them are. There is a song on the record called "Iona at Dawn," about standing on the shore of a small Hebridean island where fifteen hundred years of morning prayers have been sung, where *the sea is a chalice, the rocks are an altar, the sky is the roof of an open hall.* There is "The Cliff Above the Sea," four hundred feet of basalt with the Atlantic below, where the wind is so enormous it can carry every word you ever held that you should have let go, and I stand there and confess, straight out of Job, *You set the bounds of the ocean, You named the deep its place* (Job 38:8-11). Those are the postcard thin places. The grand ones.

But the album's whole argument is that the veil trembles just as thin in rooms with no view at all.

There is a song called "Holy Ground at the Kitchen Sink," and it is the one people write to me about most. Because I had asked God for a sign in a chapel, for a vision, a wonder, a burning bush, and He met me instead at the supper dishes, in the soap and the rind, in the soft yellow lamp above the saucers. Brother Lawrence, the old Carmelite who found God among the pots and pans, knew this. Moses knew it: he took his sandals off in a wilderness, because the bush that would not burn up had made an ordinary patch of dirt into holy ground (Exodus 3). *Every dish a kind of altar. Every soap-sud a small prayer.* The veil is as thin at the sink as on the cliff.

And there is a song I could barely record, "The Hospice Room Was a Thin Place", about a bedside, a communion cup, a father's breathing slowing like a tide, and the certainty that heaven was leaning into that small yellow room. If the veil is a membrane and not a wall, then it is thinnest exactly where we thought God was most absent.

## When bread becomes body

The wonder for me is that the thin place is not always a lightning strike. Often it is a *rhythm*, something the faithful step into on purpose.

That is what "When Bread Becomes Body" is about. Every Sunday of my whole life, the same small wonder takes hold of me at the communion rail, wheat that grew in one field, grape that swelled on a vine, gathered onto a table and, by ancient words, made into the nearness of God. *This is the regular thin place. This is the door we walk through.* You do not have to understand the mystery. He never asked us to dissect the gift, only to come and receive it.

> Jacob said it first at Bethel: *Surely the LORD is in this place, and I did not know it. How awesome is this place! This is none other than the house of God, and this is the gate of heaven* (Genesis 28:16-17).

That is the verse the whole album stands on. A man on the run, sleeping on a stone, wakes from a dream of a ladder between heaven and earth and realizes the doorway was there all along. He simply had not known it. *Heaven has a thousand doorways, and every one of them is blessed.*

## For the ones who feel the veil is stone

I made this for the person who has decided God is behind a wall.

The one for whom prayer feels like talking to sealed rock, who has concluded that if there was ever a doorway it has long since been bricked over. I want to tell you what the torn temple curtain says, what the trembling membrane says, what every thin place I have ever stood in says: the barrier is not what you think it is. It thins. It trembles. It was torn from the top by a Savior who wanted your face against His, and the Spirit still flows off it like sound off a drum-skin.

And the best news of the album is its closing song, "Carry the Thin Place Home." You do not have to stay on the island to belong. *The kingdom of God is portable*, as near as your breath, as thin in your kitchen as on the holy shore. Jacob carried Bethel with him all the long years of his life; every tent he pitched afterward was a doorway past the strife. You can carry yours too.

There is a day coming, the last song promises, when *heaven touches down* and the veil is not thin but gone, when every headland and every kitchen is lit with an unsetting sun and the whole earth is the thin place forever (Revelation 21). Every trembling doorway we have known was a whisper of that view.

Until then: listen for the sound. The wall you have been leaning against, exhausted, is thinner than you know. Put your hand out. It is trembling, because Someone on the other side is very near, and He has never once stopped leaning in.
