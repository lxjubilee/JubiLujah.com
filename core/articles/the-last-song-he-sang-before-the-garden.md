There is one sentence in the Gospels that I have never been able to read quickly.

It comes at the end of the last supper Yeshua shared with His friends. The bread has been broken. The cup has been poured and passed. He has told them plainly that one of them will hand Him over, and that before morning even the boldest of them will swear he never knew Him. The room is heavy with things they don't yet understand. And then, before they stand up and walk out into the night that will end at a cross, the record says something almost unbearably ordinary:

They sang.

"When they had sung a hymn, they went out to the Mount of Olives." That's it. No fanfare. Two of the Gospels give us the same small, staggering detail and then move on. On the last free night of His life, hours from betrayal, Yeshua of Nazareth sang a song with His friends, and then He walked toward His arrest.

I have to be careful here, because I want to tell you what song it was, and Scripture does not name it outright. So let me separate what the text says from what we can reasonably know, the way I always try to.

What the text says is simply that they sang a hymn.

But this was not any night. This was Passover, the night when the whole people of Israel remembered how Yahuah brought them out of Egypt with a strong hand, how the blood on the doorposts turned death away, how the sea opened and closed. And Passover was not a silent meal. It came with songs, in a set order, that Jewish families had sung for generations. There is a cluster of psalms near the end of the songbook, praise-songs that all begin or ring with the great shout *Hallelu-Yah*, "praise Yahuah", and this cluster had long been the music of the Passover table. The tradition calls it the *Hallel*, which simply means *praise*. Part of it was sung before the meal. The rest of it was sung after.

So when the Gospels say that after supper they sang a hymn and went out, the deep, well-founded understanding is that the hymn on their lips was the last movement of that Passover Hallel. Not a claim the text spells out, but the most natural reading in the world for anyone who knows what was sung at that table on that night. Yeshua, keeping the feast, would have sung the songs of the feast.

And that is where I have to sit down for a while. Because I know what those songs say.

Think about the man singing them. He knows. Unlike everyone else at that table, He is not guessing at what the next twelve hours hold. He knows about the garden, the kiss, the ropes, the false trial, the whip, the nails. He knows. And with all of that in front of Him, the songs the night handed Him to sing were songs of *deliverance and thanks*.

The Passover Hallel does not whisper. It remembers a God who lifts the poor out of the dust and seats them with princes. It calls on everyone who fears Yahuah to say that His steadfast love endures forever, over and over, a refrain that keeps circling back like a heartbeat. It sings of being caught in the cords of death, of distress and anguish, and then of being heard, of being rescued, of the soul returning to its rest. It sings of what a person can possibly give back to God for all His goodness, and answers that you lift the cup of salvation and pay your vows. It even says that the death of God's faithful ones is precious, weighty, costly in His sight.

He sang that. On the way to the garden.

And then there is the last stretch of the Hallel, and this is the part that undoes me every time. It sings of the stone the builders looked at and threw away, the reject, the one deemed useless, becoming the very cornerstone, the head of the corner, and it says *this is Yahuah's doing, and it is marvelous in our eyes.* It sings of a day the Lord has made, a day to rejoice in. It cries out for salvation, *hoshia-na*, save now, the very word a crowd would soon be shouting over Him. It blesses the one who comes in the name of Yahuah. And near the very end it speaks of binding the festival sacrifice to the horns of the altar.

Read that again and remember where His feet were carrying Him while His mouth was full of those words. The rejected stone was singing about the rejected stone. The festival sacrifice was singing about the sacrifice being bound. He walked out of that room into the dark on the last notes of a song about a builder's reject becoming the cornerstone, and He was the reject, and He knew it, and He sang it anyway, and He kept walking.

I do not think He sang because He felt no dread. The garden itself tells us otherwise; a little later that same night He would sweat and plead and ask, if it were possible, for the cup to pass. So this was not the singing of a man who was numb. It was the singing of a man who was afraid and faithful at the same time. He grieved and He praised in the same breath, on the same night, with the same voice. And He did not skip the song because His heart was breaking. He sang *through* the breaking.

That is the thing I most want you to take from this.

We have quietly taught ourselves that praise is for the good nights. That you sing when the meal is happy and the friends are loyal and the road ahead is clear. And so when the bad night comes, the diagnosis, the betrayal, the empty chair, the fear you cannot talk your way out of. We go silent, because it feels dishonest to sing when everything is caving in.

But look at Him. On the worst night in the history of the world, the Son of God kept the song. He did not wait for His circumstances to deserve praise. He let the ancient words carry Him into the dark, and He trusted that the God the songs remembered, the God who lifts the poor, who hears the one caught in the cords of death, who turns the rejected stone into the cornerstone, was still that God, even now, even here, even tonight.

You are allowed to sing on the bad night. You are allowed to be afraid and faithful in the same breath. You are allowed to let a song older than your fear carry truth into a room where your own words have run out.

He did it first. He stood up from a table where He had just been betrayed, and He sang about steadfast love that endures forever, and He walked out to prove it true.
