An hour before anyone else arrives, she is already there.

She unlocks the side door with a key worn smooth from her thumb. She turns on the lights in an order she could do blind. She sets out the chairs, the same chairs, the same rows, the folding ones for the overflow that has not overflowed in years, and she wipes down the table and starts the coffee that will be lukewarm by the time the first family walks in complaining about the parking. She has done this for twenty-two years. She will do it next week. And in all that time, as far as I can tell, no one has ever pulled her aside and asked how she is actually doing.

Every church has her. Sometimes it is the man at the soundboard who never once hears his own name from the stage, only "can you turn me up." Sometimes it is the woman who has run the children's room since before the current children were born, missing the sermon every Sunday of her life so that everyone else can hear it. Sometimes it is the volunteer who arrives early to run the words on the screen and stays late to shut the building down, and who worships, when she worships at all, with one eye on a laptop and one hand on a mouse.

These are the people who make the gathering possible. And they are, quietly, the least pastored people in the building.

I want to say something plainly to the ones who lead, and I include myself in the rebuke, because I have been slow to learn it. We have a word for these people, and the word is part of the problem. We call them "the team." And a team is something you deploy. You schedule it, you thank it in a general way from the front, you notice it mainly when it fails, when the sound cuts out, when the coffee runs dry, when the chairs are crooked. A team is a function. But the woman setting up the chairs is not a function. She is a sheep. She is part of the flock too, and she has been serving the flock so long that everyone, including her, has quietly forgotten she is also in it.

There is a moment in the old Scriptures I cannot stop returning to. Israel is in a battle in the wilderness, and the strange condition of the fight is this: as long as Moses holds his hands up over the valley, the people prevail, and when his arms sink, they begin to lose. So Moses stands on the hill with his arms raised. And of course his arms grow heavy, no one can hold their arms up all day, not even the man who parted the sea. He is failing. Not from unfaithfulness. From simple human exhaustion.

And here is the part that undoes me. Two men, Aaron and Hur, do something almost too ordinary to notice. They put a stone under him so he can sit. And then one stands on each side and holds his arms up for him. They do not take his place. They do not lead. They just get under the weight of the leader and carry it with him until the sun goes down. The victory in that valley, the Scripture is careful to tell us, was won by the ones whose names almost no one remembers, the two who held up the tired arms of the one everybody was watching.

That is who the woman with the chairs is. She is Aaron and Hur. She has spent twenty-two years holding up other people's arms so the battle could be won in a room she barely gets to sit down in.

So who holds up hers?

This is the question I think the church keeps failing, and I want to name it without dressing it up. We have built a whole idea of ministry around the ones who are seen, and we have quietly assumed that the ones who serve must not need much serving, that their faithfulness is a kind of self-sustaining engine that runs on its own. It is not. The volunteer who never misses is often the believer who is most alone. The one who is always fine is frequently the one no one has bothered to actually ask. And the person who makes worship possible for four hundred people is often the person who has not truly worshiped, herself, unhurried and unneeded, in a very long time.

Yeshua saw this exact thing and refused to let it stand. His friends had been serving the crowds so relentlessly that they had not even had time to eat. And He did not praise them for their stamina. He did not schedule them harder. He said, come away, by yourselves, to a quiet place, and rest a while. The Shepherd of the whole world looked at His most useful people and His instinct was not to use them more. It was to lead them beside still water and make them lie down in green pasture, because He knew that the ones who tend must themselves be tended, or they will dry up in the middle of their own faithfulness and no one will notice until they are gone.

I have watched them go. I have watched the woman with the chairs finally stop coming, not in anger, not in scandal, just emptied, quietly, after decades, wondering in her honest heart whether anyone would even notice the room did not set itself up. And the terrible answer, too often, is that we noticed the crooked chairs before we noticed the missing shepherd of them.

So this is a word to the leaders, and it is a simple, uncomfortable one. Go and find the person who serves in the shadows of your gathering, the soundboard, the coffee, the chairs, the screen, the nursery, and do not thank them for their service. Everyone thanks them for their service. Instead, sit down beside them in the empty room, before the crowd comes, and ask them how their soul is. Ask about the marriage, the diagnosis, the child who stopped calling. Let them be, for twenty minutes, not the team but the flock. Hold their arms up for once, the way they have held up everyone else's for years.

And if you are the one with the worn key and the smooth thumb, if you have read this far certain that it is about someone else, because you are the one who does the noticing and never the noticed, then let me be the one to say it to you now, plainly, as your pastor across whatever distance this reaches you: you are not staff. You are not a function. You are a sheep the Shepherd loves, and He did not put you in that early-morning room only to be useful. He put you there because He wanted to meet you in it, before anyone else arrives, in the quiet, with the coffee just starting and the light coming through the windows.

Turn around. He has been in the room this whole time. Sit down in one of your own chairs. He has been waiting to tend the one who tends everyone else.
