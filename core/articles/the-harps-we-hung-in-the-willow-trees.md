There is a psalm that begins with a river and a group of people who could not sing, and I have carried it in my chest across three languages and more homesick nights than I can count.

By the rivers of Babylon, they sat down and they wept. They were far from home, carried off, displaced, living in a country that was not theirs, under a people who had burned the city they loved. And the psalm tells us they took their harps, their lyres, the very instruments they had once played in the courts of Yahuah, and they hung them up in the willow trees by the water.

Not smashed them. *Hung* them. There is a whole grief in that word. You do not throw away the harp. You cannot, it is part of you, but you cannot bear to play it either. So you leave it in the branches, still yours, just silent.

And then comes the cruelest part. Their captors, the very people who had taken everything, turned to them and asked for a song. *Sing us one of your songs of Zion. Entertain us. Give us one of those famous worship songs from the temple you lost.*

And the exiles ask the question that has been rising in the throats of God's people ever since: *How can we sing the song of Yahuah in a foreign land?*

I need to talk about that question, because a great many of you are living inside it right now, and you think your silence means your faith broke.

## The silence that isn't rebellion

Let me say this clearly, prophet to worshiper: hanging your harp in the willow is not the same as walking away from God.

We have to stop treating every silent season as a spiritual failure. The exiles were not backslidden. They had not stopped loving Yahuah. You can feel in every line of that psalm how desperately they still loved Him and loved home. They were *grieving.* They were displaced. They had lost the place where their songs made sense, and in that dislocation the music went quiet, and they were honest enough to admit it instead of faking a joy they did not have.

I would rather see a believer honestly hang the harp than perform a worship they cannot feel.

Some of you have gone quiet. There was a season when you sang all the time, and then loss came, or exile came, a move, a diagnosis, a marriage ending, a church that wounded you, a country that was not yours, and the song just stopped. And you have been beating yourself up about it, certain that your silence is proof you have drifted. Hear me: the psalm that names your silence is *in the Bible.* Yahuah put the song of the people who could not sing right into His own book of songs. He was not ashamed of their quiet. He preserved it. He honored it enough to keep it forever.

Your silent season is not evidence that you left Him. Sometimes it is evidence of how much you loved what you lost.

## Why they would not sing on demand

Now look closely at *why* they would not sing, because this is where the exiles become teachers.

The captors did not want worship. They wanted a performance. They wanted the songs of Zion pulled out of their sacred context and played for entertainment, stripped of the God they were written for. And the exiles refused. They would rather hang the harp in a tree than turn the worship of Yahuah into a party trick for the people who had broken them.

There is a fierce holiness in that refusal, and we need it back.

Because the pressure on you is not usually to stop singing. The pressure is to sing the *wrong song*, to let the powers that displaced you tell you what your voice is for. To perform your faith as a product. To take the deepest, most sacred thing you carry and hand it over as amusement to a world that has no idea what it is asking for. And sometimes the most faithful thing a worshiper can do is close her mouth and say: *this song is not for that. This song belongs to Yahuah, and I will not sing it to entertain the empire that burned my home.*

Silence, in that moment, is not the absence of worship. It is the guarding of it.

## But the harp does not stay in the tree

And yet. I am an evangelist, and I cannot leave you weeping by the river, because the story of God's people was never that the harp stayed in the willow forever.

Here is the quiet miracle hidden inside that psalm. Even as they refused to perform the song of Zion for their captors, they were *writing a new one.* Psalm 137 is itself a song. Read it again. It has rhythm, it has form, it was meant to be sung. In the very act of saying *we cannot sing here,* they sang. Their grief became worship. Their silence, honestly named and offered to Yahuah, turned into one of the most enduring songs His people ever carried.

That is the thing about a heart that belongs to God. Even its silence is fertile. Even its lament composes. The harp comes down out of the willow not when the exile ends and you get home. It comes down the moment you are willing to sing the *true* song, the honest one, the one about how far away you feel and how much you miss His courts and how you refuse to stop being His even here. That is a song of Zion sung in a foreign land. You just did the thing you said could not be done.

I have sung the same hope in English and in Korean and in Romanian, into rooms where the language was not mine and the culture was not mine and I felt every inch of the distance from home. And I have learned that the song of Yahuah is not bound to a country. It is not bound to the building you lost or the season you left. It travels. It crosses borders and tongues and grief, and it will find its voice in a foreign land the moment you stop demanding that the land feel like home before you will open your mouth.

## Reaching for the branches

So let me speak to the one sitting by the river.

Maybe you are somewhere that is not yours, a life you did not choose, a place you did not want, a season of exile with no return date printed on it. And your harp is up in the tree, and you have been telling yourself the silence is your shame.

It is not your shame. It is your grief, and Yahuah is not offended by it. He wrote it into His songbook.

But you do not have to sing the song they are demanding, the performance, the fake joy, the worship-as-entertainment for the powers that displaced you. Refuse that one. Guard it.

And then reach up into the branches, and take your harp down, and sing the *true* song instead. The honest one. The one that tells Him exactly how far from home you feel, and then, right at the end, the way His grieving people always have, hangs on to Him anyway.

That is how you sing the song of Yahuah in a foreign land.

You will find, when you finally play it, that He was by the river the whole time.
