Imagine you had one page left. The whole book of Psalms behind you, a hundred and fifty songs of lament and rage and repentance and dizzying joy, everything a human heart can do in front of God, and now the very last page, the final word, the note the entire collection has been climbing toward.

You might expect a grand theological summary. A last commandment. A solemn warning to carry out the door.

Instead, the songbook ends by calling a roll of instruments.

The final psalm does almost nothing but list the band. It names them, one after another, and tells everything that has breath to join in, and I have come to believe that this list, so easy to skim, is one of the most generous and quietly astonishing things in all of Scripture.

## The roll call

Let me read you the roster, because the names themselves are worth knowing.

It opens with the *shofar*, the ram's horn, the raw and ancient blast that summoned armies and announced the new moon and shook the air at Sinai. Then the *nevel* and the *kinnor,* two stringed instruments; the *kinnor* is the lyre David carried, the small harp that soothed a tormented king, and the *nevel* its larger, deeper-voiced cousin. Then the *tof*, the hand-drum, the frame-drum, the timbrel that women lifted on the far shore of the sea when they danced, and it is named here alongside *machol,* the dance itself, the body moving. Then *minnim,* stringed things, and *ugav,* the pipe, breath through a reed. And finally, twice over, the *tziltzelim*, the cymbals, clashing and ringing.

Horn. Strings. Drum. Dance. More strings. Pipe. Cymbals.

Stop and notice what has just happened. Every family of instrument humans had invented was summoned by name into the last psalm. The things you *blow.* The things you *pluck.* The things you *strike.* Nothing was left out. Nothing was ruled too loud, too primitive, too rhythmic, too foreign.

> The Psalter does not end by narrowing worship down to one acceptable sound. It ends by throwing the doors open to every sound at once.

## The list that refuses to draw a line

I want you to feel how radical that is, because we have spent a great deal of our history trying to draw exactly the line this psalm refuses to draw.

In nearly every generation of the church, somebody has decided which instruments are holy and which are suspect. The organ is reverent but the guitar is worldly. Strings are fine but drums are dangerous. This style honors God; that style is mere emotion; that one over there is practically pagan. We keep trying to shrink the acceptable spectrum of praise down to the sounds we personally grew up trusting.

And then you reach the last page of the songbook and find the Spirit of God doing the exact opposite. Reaching for the loud horn *and* the delicate lyre. The pounding drum *and* the breathy pipe. The dance *and* the crashing cymbals. As if to say, at the very end, before the book closes: *all of it. Bring all of it. Every texture of sound a human being can make, I want it in the final crescendo.*

Notice too that this list spans the whole range of human feeling. The horn is fierce and the lyre is tender. The drum is the body and the pipe is the breath. There is a sound here for triumph and a sound here for aching sweetness, a sound for the disciplined musician and a sound for the one who can only clap and stamp. The last psalm was engineered to leave no one holding an instrument they were told they could not bring.

## Everything that has breath

And then comes the line that widens the invitation past instruments altogether.

After the whole orchestra is named, the psalm turns and says: let *everything that has breath* praise the Lord. Everything that breathes. That is the final expansion, and it is total. If the instrument list said *every kind of sound is welcome,* this last line says *every kind of person is welcome*, because the only qualification named is breath. You do not need training. You do not need a good voice. You do not need to have earned it or cleaned yourself up or learned the right words. You need to be breathing.

I love that the qualification is *breath,* of all things, because breath is the one instrument nobody had to buy and nobody can be too poor to own. The horn belonged to the one who could afford a ram. The lyre took years to master. But breath, breath is standard issue, handed to every human at the door of life, the same gift given to the king and the beggar and the child and the dying. When the Psalms want to name the widest possible circle of worshipers, they do not name the skilled or the worthy. They name *the breathing.* And that is you. That has always been you.

There is something almost circular and holy in it, too, that I cannot get over: the breath God first breathed into the dust is the very breath the last psalm asks us to breathe back out as praise. We return to Him, in worship, the exact thing He gave us to live. The whole story of a person, from that first inhale in the garden to this last exhaled *hallelujah*, is bookended by the same breath, only now it has learned what it is for.

## Why the songbook ends here

So why does the great book of prayer end not with a proposition but with an orchestra?

I think because the Psalms have spent a hundred and fifty songs teaching us that God can be met in *everything*, in the pit and on the mountain, in the tears and the dancing, in the honest rage and the speechless awe, and the only fitting way to end a book that big is with a sound big enough to hold it. One instrument could not do it. One texture, one volume, one mood could not do it. Only the whole orchestra, every family of sound and every breathing body, layered into a single closing crescendo, could gather up all that the songbook had carried and hand it back to God in one roar of joy.

And you are meant to hear the roll call as an invitation with your own name folded into it. Whatever you can bring, the polished instrument or the untrained clap, the strong voice or the whisper, the dance or the simple fact that your lungs are still moving, the last psalm has already made room for it. It drew no line you have to be on the right side of. It ended the entire book of prayer by insisting that praise is wide enough for every sound and every soul that draws breath.

So bring what you have. Bring the loud thing or the quiet thing, the practiced thing or the only thing you know how to do. The final page of the songbook is not a narrow door. It is a whole orchestra calling your name, a crescendo already building, and a single, breathtaking qualification for joining in, that you are, right now, breathing.
