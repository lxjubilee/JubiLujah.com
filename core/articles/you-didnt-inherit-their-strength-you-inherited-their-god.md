People keep telling me I am strong. Strong like my mother. Strong like her mother before her. And for a long time I wore that the way you wear a compliment you have not fully examined, gratefully, and without asking what was underneath it. Then I sat down to make an album about the women who raised me, and the whole thing nearly came apart in my hands, because I realized I was about to tell a lie.

The lie is that strength is something you inherit in the blood. It is not. I made *Ancestral Strength* to say the truer and far better thing.

## The Compliment That Was Almost an Idol

Here is the confession at the center of this record, in the song **Same God of My Grandmothers**:

> Grandma didn't have the strength. She had the God. Auntie didn't have the strength. She had the God.

I need you to feel how close that sits to something dangerous. In a culture that rightly honors its elders, it is the easiest thing in the world to slide from *honoring* the ancestors to *venerating* them, to start believing that the power to survive was in their genes, their grit, their bloodline, and that I am simply the next strong woman in a strong line. It flatters me. And it is false.

Because I watched these women up close. My grandmother knelt at a storefront-church altar rail until she wore a print into the wood. That was not a woman relying on her own reserves. That was a woman emptying herself before Someone stronger, every single morning, for sixty years. The strength people praise in me was never a family trait passing down the line. It was **Yahuah**, held onto by one generation and handed to the next, not as a bloodline, but as a faith.

## What the Bible Actually Puts in the Family Tree

Scripture is careful about this in a way we usually are not. When Paul writes to young Timothy, he does not say, "You come from strong stock." He says he is persuaded of *the unfeigned faith that is in thee, which dwelt first in thy grandmother Lois, and thy mother Eunice* (2 Timothy 1:5). Notice what got handed down. Not temperament. Not toughness. *Faith.* The inheritance was a way of trusting God, carried by real women with names.

That is the whole thesis of the song **Family Tree in the Bible**, the leather-bound King James on the parlor table with four generations of names written inside the cover, and the line that turns the whole thing right side up: *I'm not standing on the strength of bloodlines, I'm standing on the faith that they kept.* The names matter because of the God they trusted, not the other way around.

And this is why, when **Yahuah** wanted to remind His people who He was, He did not point them to their own pedigree. He said, *I am the God of thy father, the God of Abraham, the God of Isaac, and the God of Jacob* (Exodus 3:6). One God, across three generations of very flawed men. The constant in that sentence was never the patriarchs. It was the God who kept showing up to each of them.

## The Songs They Sang in Chains

You cannot make an honest album about African-diaspora heritage and skip the hardest ground, so I did not. **Cotton-Field Hallelujah** and **Wade in the Water Still** reach back to the spirituals, the songs the ancestors lifted where a master could not stop them, *Steal Away to Jesus* hummed low over a Mississippi row, *Go Down Moses* moving down the field.

I want to be precise as a teacher here, because this matters. The power in those songs was never that suffering is noble. Suffering is not noble; it is a wound. The power was that praise turned out to be the one thing chains could not confiscate. When Paul and Silas sat in a Roman prison at midnight, beaten and bound, *they prayed, and sang praises unto God* (Acts 16:25), and the foundations shook. The ancestors knew that secret in their bodies long before I read it in a book. The hallelujah was unchainable because the God it was aimed at was unchainable. Again: the strength was never in them. It was in the One they sang to.

## The Cloud Is Cheering, Not the Genes

There is a picture I built the finale around, **Cloud of Witnesses March**, and it comes straight from Hebrews. After a long chapter naming the heroes of faith, the writer says: *Wherefore seeing we also are compassed about with so great a cloud of witnesses... let us run with patience the race that is set before us, looking unto Jesus the author and finisher of our faith* (Hebrews 12:1–2).

Read that carefully. The cloud of witnesses is not there to be worshiped. They are in the stands. The eyes stay fixed on **Yeshua**, the *author* who started the faith in your family and the *finisher* who will complete it. The grandmothers are cheering. They are not the finish line. He is. And the same **Yeshua** who was *the same yesterday, and to day, and for ever* (Hebrews 13:8) for my grandmother at that altar rail is the same for me, which means I am not running on borrowed strength that will eventually run out. I am running to the same God who never has.

## Who I Made This For

I made *Ancestral Strength* for the one who is tired of being called strong. For the person carrying a family and a legacy and a quiet fear that when their own strength gives out, the whole line breaks with them. It will not. You were never the source. You were only ever a link, holding on to the same God the ones before you held.

And I made it for the opposite person too, the one estranged from their line, without a praying grandmother in the story, wondering if they missed the inheritance. Hear me. The inheritance was never the bloodline. It is the God. And He is not a family heirloom that only some people get. He is offered, fresh, to you, today, *the same God* who met every generation before you and is standing ready to be met by you.

So when someone tells you that you are strong like the women before you, you can smile and let it pass. Because you and **Yahuah** both know the real testimony. You did not inherit their strength. You inherited their God, and He is the only strength that was ever going to hold.
