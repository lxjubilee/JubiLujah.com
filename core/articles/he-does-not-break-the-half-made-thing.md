There is a folder on my phone I hope no one ever scrolls through.

It is where the rough takes live. Songs hummed into the dark at two in the morning, half awake, the melody arriving faster than I could find a pen, so I did the only thing I could and croaked it into a voice memo before it left. If you played those recordings back, you would hear a mess. You would hear me singing flat because I did not want to wake the house. You would hear a whole verse and then a mumble where the words had not come yet, a placeholder syllable held in place of a lyric I did not have. You would hear a cough. A dog. A refrigerator. And under all of it, if you were kind and patient, you might just make out the shape of a song that is not finished and may never be.

For a long time those recordings shamed me. They were evidence of how far my hands fall short of my ear. I would go back to write the song out properly and cringe at the raw version, and part of me wanted to delete the lot of them, to keep only the polished files, the finished masters, the songs I would let a stranger hear. Who wants to be caught mid-mumble? Who wants their unfinished, out-of-tune, coughing self on the record?

And then one morning I was reading the prophet Isaiah, in the passage about the servant Yahuah promises to send, and I came to a line that stopped me completely. It says that this one, when he comes, will not break a bruised reed, and will not snuff out a wick that is only smoking. And I sat there in my kitchen and thought: that is a promise about the rough take.

## What a reed actually is

You have to know what a reed is to feel the weight of it.

In that part of the world a reed was not a decoration. It was raw material. You cut a reed from the riverbank and you made things from it, a measuring rod, a pen, and, most tenderly, a pipe. A shepherd's flute. You hollowed it and cut the holes and put your breath through it, and it made music out on the hills where nobody was listening but the sheep. A reed was the cheapest instrument a poor person could own. It cost nothing but the cutting.

So a bruised reed, a reed that is cracked, bent, half-crushed underfoot, is a ruined instrument. It is the flute that has gone wrong. The obvious thing, the sensible thing, the thing any craftsman does, is to snap it and throw it in the river and cut a fresh one. There are a thousand reeds on the bank. Why fight to save the broken one? It will only play sour. It will only disappoint you.

And the promise Isaiah heard, the promise the Gospels say came true in Yeshua, is that this One does not do the sensible thing. He picks up the cracked flute. He does not break it across His knee. He keeps the ruined instrument, and, this is the part I cannot get over. He keeps it *as an instrument*. He does not throw it away and He does not stop expecting music from it. He bends down over the thing that barely makes a sound, and He does not put it out.

## The demo is not the failure

I want to say something to everyone who is carrying around a folder of half-made things.

Maybe yours is not a song. Maybe it is a marriage you have been quietly repairing that still plays a little sour. Maybe it is a faith that mumbles where the words should be, a prayer life that is mostly placeholder syllables held in the space where you cannot yet say what you mean. Maybe it is a version of yourself you would not let anyone hear, the rough take, the two-in-the-morning self, out of tune because you have been trying so hard not to wake anyone with how much this hurts.

Here is what I have come to believe, staking my whole working life on it. Yahuah is not embarrassed by the rough take. He is not waiting for the polished master before He starts loving the song. The finished version is not more precious to Him than the cracked reed croaked into the dark. In fact, and I say this carefully, because it undoes me. He seems to keep the demos. He seems to be the kind of Maker who does not delete the mumble.

We have this backward. We think we have to arrive finished. We think the mess disqualifies us, that the coughs and the flat notes and the missing words are proof we are not real, not worthy of the recording. So we hide the rough folder and we perform the master, and we come to Him only on our best days with our best takes, carefully in tune. And all the while the promise stands open behind us: a bruised reed He will not break. The very brokenness we are hiding is the exact thing the promise was made about.

## Keep the file

I have stopped deleting my rough takes. It felt disloyal, once I understood.

Because here is the strange, hopeful truth about those files: the master could not exist without them. Every finished song I have ever released began as a croak in the dark, a bruised reed, a mumble where the words weren't. The polished version did not replace the rough one. It *grew out of it*. The rough take was not the failure of the song. It was the song's first honest breath. And a Maker who threw out every rough take on principle would never finish anything at all, because there is no finished thing that did not once sound terrible.

So I think Yahuah keeps our rough folders for the same reason I have learned to keep mine. Not because He is stuck with us. Because He is not finished. The cracked reed in His hand is not being discarded. It is being held, and mended, and slowly tuned, by the only Musician patient enough to get music out of a broken pipe.

If you have been hiding the unfinished version of yourself, hoping to show up one day with the master, stop waiting. Bring the rough take. Bring the mumble and the flat notes and the placeholder where the words won't come. He is not going to break it. He never breaks the half-made thing. He bends down over it, and He does not put it out, and He keeps humming the melody you cannot finish yet, until the day it plays clean.
