In a memory-care unit at the end of a long hallway, a man who can no longer reliably name his own children is singing every word of a hymn without missing one.

His daughter visits on Sundays. Most weeks he is not sure who she is. He searches her face with a terrible, apologetic effort and comes up empty, and it costs them both something neither will say out loud. But when the volunteer sits down at the old upright piano in the common room and plays the first few bars of a hymn he learned as a boy, something rises up out of a part of him the disease has not been able to reach. His eyes clear. His mouth finds the words. All of them. Verse after verse, in order.

The names are gone. The song is not.

Anyone who has sat in that room knows the strange ache of it, that a tune can be the last light on in a house where nearly every other window has gone dark. And it raises a question worth stopping on: why should *that* be the thing that lasts? Not the theology he could once explain. Not the sermons he heard a thousand of. The hymn.

Here is what I have come to believe about it, standing where I stand.

A hymn does not enter you the way a lecture does. A lecture knocks on the front door of the mind, presents its argument, and waits to be evaluated. You can agree with it or dismiss it, and either way you have kept it at arm's length, out in the room where you decide things. But a melody does not knock. It comes in through a window you did not know was open, sets the truth down in a room far below the one where you argue, and leaves before you have decided whether to allow it.

That is why grown men who have not wept in years come apart at a funeral the moment the organ starts. It is not that they suddenly agree with the doctrine. It is that the song has walked straight past the guard they have posted at the door of their own heart their whole adult lives, and touched the boy who used to stand in a pew between his mother and his father and did not yet know he was supposed to keep his composure.

The hymn is not moving the man. The hymn is reaching the child the man used to be, who is still in there, and who never learned to be ashamed of being moved.

Now follow that one step further, because this is the part I most want a father to hear.

What gets sung over a child in the early years goes down into that deep room and stays. Not the room where they will later win and lose arguments about the faith, the room underneath it, where the foundations are. Decades on, when the arguments have all been had and half of them forgotten, when a man is bewildered by his own life or lying in a hospital bed or losing his own name a piece at a time, it is not the case *for* God he reaches for. He could not reconstruct it if he tried. It is the tune his grandmother hummed while she did the dishes. It is the hymn the little country church sang every single week until it wore a groove in him so deep that dementia itself cannot dig it out.

We spend so much anxious effort trying to hand our children the right *conclusions*, and conclusions are the first thing to blow away. Meanwhile the thing that actually lodges and holds, the thing that will still be there when the mind is failing and the world has narrowed to one room and one visitor whose name won't come, is what we *sang*. The Scriptures knew this long before anyone studied it. The longest book in the Bible is a book of songs, and a people who could not always read were told to put the truth in their mouths and teach it to their children by singing it, on the road, at home, lying down, getting up. Not because a song is a nice wrapper for the lesson. Because the song is how the lesson survives the person forgetting it.

This is the quiet reason the old hymns still undo grown men. They are not merely old. They are *early*. They were planted before the defenses were built, in the years when a child sang without deciding whether to believe, and so they got underneath the place where believing later became complicated. And a thing planted that deep does not uproot. It waits. It waits through the rebellion and the wandering and the long clever years of keeping God at arm's length, and then one ordinary afternoon a piano finds the first three notes and the whole buried thing stands up at once.

So let me say the plain word, the way plainness is the only thing that ever gets believed in a room like that memory-care hallway.

If you have children, or grandchildren, or a niece who ends up at your table on holidays, sing to them. Not performance. Not a program. Sing the old ones over them while they are small, badly and off-key and often, at bedtime and in the truck and while the dishes soak. You will feel like it is doing nothing. You are wrong. You are laying a foundation in a room they will not remember you building, and you are laying it in the one material that lasts when memory, health, and even certainty have gone.

And if you are the grown man this is really about, the one who has kept the door guarded a long time, who feels something buckle when the hymn starts and hurries to steady it before anyone sees. You might stop steadying it for once. That is not weakness leaking out. That is the boy in the pew, still in there, still able to be found. The song is not embarrassing you. It is carrying you somewhere.

It is carrying you home. It always was. That is the whole reason it was put in you.

You're in the fold now, and He knows your name, take your time learning His.
