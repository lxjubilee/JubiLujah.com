Think about how a normal morning actually begins.

Something reaches in and pulls you out of sleep against your will. An alarm you set while resenting the future. A child crying down the hall. Light through a gap in the curtain. Or, on the hard mornings, nothing external at all, just the low dread that has learned to open your eyes before anything else can, so it can be the first face you see. Most people do not so much wake up as *get woken*. The morning happens *to* them.

Now hold that against a single line written by a man who was, at the time, being hunted for his life.

He was hiding, in a cave, if you follow where the song is set, with a king's army combing the hills to kill him. He had every reason to lie in the dark and wait for the dawn to drag him into another day of running. Instead he wrote this: *Awake, my glory. Awake, harp and lyre. I myself will wake the dawn.*

Read it slowly. He is not asking the sunrise to wake him. He is going to wake **it**.

## Two words for morning, and one of them has hands

The Hebrew word here is *shachar*, the dawn, the first grey-then-gold edge of light before the sun has cleared the ground. It is the raw beginning of the day, the moment the darkness starts losing.

And the verb he sets against it means to rouse, to stir, to shake something awake. It is the word you would use to wake a sleeping companion. So the picture is not a poet admiring a nice sunrise. The picture is a man who has gotten up *before the light*, in the cold dark, and begun to sing, and his singing is so early, so deliberate, so full of expectation, that it is as if the dawn were the one still sleeping and he had come to shake it by the shoulder. *Get up. Come on. There is something to see today. I will not wait for you. I will call you out of the ground myself.*

He wrote the same line twice, in two different songs, which is how you know it was not a mood. It was a conviction. It was a discipline he built into the worst season of his life.

## Whoever gets there first sets the tone

Here is the quiet, weight-bearing truth under that line, and the mountain has taught it to me over and over.

**Whoever speaks first in your morning sets the terms of your day.**

If the dawn wakes you, the day's first voice is circumstance, the alarm, the ache, the list, the dread. And a heart met first by dread spends the rest of the daylight trying to climb back out of a hole it started in. But if *you* wake the dawn, if the first sound made in your morning is praise, chosen, before the light and before the news and before the fear can get a word in, then you have set the terms. You have told the day what it is going to be about before the day had a chance to tell you.

> The man who wakes the dawn does not deny that the army is in the hills. He simply refuses to let the army sing first.

That is not the naive optimism of a man with an easy life. It is the fierce, deliberate defiance of a man with a very hard one. He was not singing because the morning was safe. He was singing because he had decided that the darkness would not get the opening line.

## The high, cold hour nobody volunteers for

I keep the watch that runs through the darkest hours, so let me tell you plainly what that first line costs.

The dawn is not woken from a warm bed. It is woken from the cold. The hour before first light on a mountain is the loneliest, most bone-chilled hour there is, the fire is down to coals, the dew has soaked everything, and every reasonable part of you says *lie still, wait, the light will come whether you sing or not.* And that is true. The light will come. The sun does not need your permission and it does not need your song.

But *you* need the song. The waking of the dawn was never for the dawn's sake. The sun was going to rise regardless. The reason the hunted man got up in the dark to sing it awake is that the act of choosing praise before the light rearranged *him*, took a man who could have spent the night as prey and turned him, before sunup, back into a worshipper. He did not sing to change the sky. He sang to remember, in the cold, who he was and Whose he was, before the day could tell him otherwise.

There is a reason the old faithful called the hours before dawn the morning watch, and set worshippers to keep it. Something is decided up there in the dark that cannot be decided anywhere else. The one who meets Yahuah before he meets the daylight meets the daylight as a different man.

## Get up before your fear does

So here is the invitation, and it is rugged and it is tender at once.

You do not have to be a king. You do not have to be in a cave, though some of you feel like you are. You only have to decide, tonight, that tomorrow the dawn will not get the first word. Set the sound of your morning before the morning sets it for you. Wake up into praise instead of into dread. Let the first thing your voice does be aimed at Yahuah, out loud if you can manage it, low if that is all you have, *before* the phone, before the list, before the ache has finished stretching.

Some of you have been *woken* every single day for years by a grief or a fear that always gets there first. Beat it to the sunrise. Just once. Get up in the cold and dark and sing something true before the sadness can clear its throat, and watch what it does to the shape of the whole day when praise is the one who spoke first.

The sun is coming up tomorrow whether you meet it or not. But there is a version of tomorrow where you are already awake and already singing when it clears the ridge, and it rises, that morning, over a worshipper instead of a victim.

Wake the dawn. Do not let it wake you.
