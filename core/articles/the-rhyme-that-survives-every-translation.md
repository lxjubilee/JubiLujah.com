Here is a small mystery worth stopping over. A psalm written in Hebrew, three thousand years ago, by a poet who never imagined your language existed, can be read aloud tonight in English, or Romanian, or Swahili, or Korean, and it still moves people to tears. It still sounds like poetry. It still lands.

Now try that with almost any other poem.

Take a beloved verse in English, a real jewel of a thing, and translate it into another language. What happens? The rhyme collapses. The rhythm limps. The clever wordplay evaporates. What was music in one language becomes flat prose in the next, and everyone agrees, with a sigh, that "you really had to read it in the original." Poetry is famously the thing that gets *lost* in translation. That is nearly a law.

The Psalms break the law. And they break it on purpose, because of how they were built.

To see why, you have to understand what makes Hebrew poetry *poetry* in the first place, and it is not what makes an English poem an English poem. Our poems, for most of our history, have rhymed the *sounds*. We chime the ends of lines: *night* with *light*, *love* with *above*. We count beats and syllables. And every bit of that is welded to the specific sounds of the specific language. Change the language and the sounds change, and the whole delicate music falls apart. That is why our poetry cannot survive the border crossing. Its beauty was made of sound, and sound does not travel.

Hebrew poetry rhymes something else. It rhymes the *thought*.

The engine of a Hebrew poem is a thing scholars call parallelism, and once you see it you will never stop seeing it. The poet says something in the first line, and then, in the second line, says it *again*, differently. Not as filler. As an echo, a turn, a second angle on the same truth. One line lifts an idea; the next line catches it and answers.

Sometimes the two lines simply agree, the second saying the same thing as the first in fresh words, the heavens declare God's glory, and then, right behind it, the skies proclaim the work of His hands. Say it, then say it again, deeper. Sometimes the second line does the opposite, throwing the first into relief by naming its shadow, Yahuah watches over the way of the righteous, but the way of the wicked will perish. Light, then dark, so you feel the edge between them. And sometimes the second line takes the first by the hand and carries it a step further, building the thought upward instead of merely repeating it.

But in every case, notice what the "rhyme" is made of. It is not made of *sound*. It is made of *meaning*. The two lines rhyme the way two ideas rhyme, one thought set beside a matching thought, so that the sense chimes even when the sound does not.

And meaning, unlike sound, crosses every border on earth.

That is the quiet genius of it. When you translate a sound-rhyme, the rhyme dies, because you cannot carry the sounds across. But when you translate a *thought*-rhyme, the rhyme lives, because you *can* carry the thought across, that is the one thing translation is actually able to do. The heavens declaring glory and the skies proclaiming handiwork will echo each other just as beautifully in Romanian as in Hebrew, because the echo was never in the noise the words make. It was in the truth they hold. The parallel survives the crossing intact.

I do not think this was an accident of style. I think it was a mercy built into the design.

Consider: these were the songs of a God who, from the very beginning, said He intended to bless *all* the families of the earth through this one small people. A God who would one day pour His Spirit out on a crowd from every nation and let each one hear the mighty works of God in his own tongue. A God whose final picture of worship, at the end of the whole story, is a countless multitude *from every nation and tribe and people and language* singing before the throne. If that is the God writing the songbook, then of course He would inspire a poetry that could make the border crossing without dying. Of course He would build His people's worship on a rhyme that any language on earth could carry. The Psalms were made portable because the salvation they sing about was always meant to go everywhere.

I feel the weight of this personally, because I make music in more than one tongue, and I know the frustration of the thing that will not translate. There are songs so wedded to the sound of one language that to move them is to break them; I have written about what shatters when you try to carry a hymn across a border. This is the glad other half of that truth. The Hebrew songbook was engineered for exactly the crossing that destroys ordinary poetry. Its deepest beauty is not fragile. It is not trapped in the original. It is waiting in the *meaning*, patient, ready to bloom again in whatever language a person happens to weep in.

This is why a grieving woman who has never heard a syllable of Hebrew can pray a psalm in her own language at a graveside and feel it hold her weight. She is not getting a watered-down copy of the "real" poem that lives somewhere out of her reach. She is getting the poem. The thing that made it poetry came across whole. The rhyme of thought against thought, the shepherd who leads and the still waters He leads beside, the valley of shadow and the presence that fears no evil, is chiming in her language exactly as it chimed in the poet's, because it was never chiming in the sounds at all.

So here is what I want you to carry away, whatever language you sing in.

You are not standing at a locked door when you come to the Scriptures. You do not have to master an ancient tongue before the songs of God will open to you. By deliberate design, the most beautiful poetry ever written was made to be *given away*, built on a rhyme that no border can break, so that a shepherd boy's midnight cry and a heartbroken exile's lament and heaven's own new song could all be sung, true and unbroken, in the mother tongue of anyone who would receive them.

The poets rhymed the one thing that belongs to no single language and to all of them at once. They rhymed the truth. And the truth, thank God, sings in every tongue on earth.
