I wanted to tell the story of salvation to people who think they are too strong to need it. So I wrote a whole album in the voice of a fire chief.

*Jesus Saves from the Fire* is staged like an intimate piece of theatre, one hardened man alone in a warm pool of light, felt piano and cello underneath, telling his rescue to a small, hushed house. No stadium, no choir wall. Just a survivor at the edge of the stage who has something he cannot stop saying. I built it that way because the truth at the center of it is too personal to shout. It has to be leaned in and told.

## A man who runs toward the smoke

The opening songs are the chief in his element, and they are proud in the best sense of the word. "The Bells Go Off", two in the morning, boots by the bed, out the door. "Run Toward the Smoke". Everybody on the street is running out, and here come the engines against the current, the only ones driving in. *I don't run away, I run toward. I don't stand back, I kick the door.* That is a real and holy courage, and I honored it, because I have known people who spend their whole lives being the running-in, reaching-in hand for everyone else.

But every one of those early songs has a crack in it, a rumor the chief keeps hearing. A survivor comes out of a sealed room no hose could reach, no soot on her cheek, no smoke in her hair, and she points back into the flame and says, *"He was in there."* A Firefighter in white. White gear with no soot, no name, no crew. There and then gone. And He gets to the ones the chief never could. This is the God of Isaiah, the One who says *when you walk through the fire you shall not be burned* (Isaiah 43:2), and He is the fourth man Nebuchadnezzar saw walking loose in the furnace with the three who would not bow (Daniel 3:25).

## The honest ceiling of human strength

I could not write this record without the song that admits the job has a limit. "Some Don't Come Out" is the chief telling the truth the posters never show, the night the stairwell folded early, the night he was one room too slow, the list of names he carries alone. *The chief is not a god in a yellow helmet, the chief is a man with a job he can't always do.* And that admission is not despair. It is the crack where the wonder starts. Because if he could not reach them, then who was walking in?

The middle of the album is the evidence piling up. "They All Describe the Same", three strangers who never met, three different fires, and every statement describing the identical impossible figure. A courtroom would call that corroboration. "The Crew Says It's Jesus", the rookie coming out grinning, the older men who have buried friends nodding along, a name written in the corner of the whiteboard that nobody erases. And then, because I will not cheapen anyone's real struggle, "Hard to Believe", the chief alone in the empty bay at midnight, wanting it to be true and unable to make himself pretend. *It's not that I'm against You, it's not that my heart is hard. It's just I've buried too many to be careless.* I gave doubt its dignity there, in the honest cry of the father in the Gospel: *I believe; help my unbelief* (Mark 9:24).

## The night the rescuer became the rescued

Everything turns on track eight. "When I Cried Out" is the album's peak, and it is the moment the man who sent everyone into the danger becomes the one trapped in it. The warehouse floor gives way and drops him to the basement, the heat pressing him flat, his low-air alarm chirping in the dark, the one sound a firefighter is trained to dread. He keys his radio and gets static. His own call sign goes out unanswered. *The chief who sent everyone into the danger couldn't reach himself, couldn't reach a thing on his own.*

And there it is, the terrible, saving mercy of the whole gospel. There is a rescue your own arms cannot manage. So he goes down on a floor too hot to kneel on, and every word he ever doubted comes up out of him at once as a single word, a name, and a glove he has never felt closes around his wrist. *I could not reach myself, so I cried out, "Jesus", and the One I couldn't believe came down for me.* That is 2 Samuel 22:17 made flesh in a basement: *he reached down from on high and took me; he drew me out of many waters.* And it is the promise that holds the entire record: *everyone who calls on the name of the Lord will be saved* (Romans 10:13).

> I could not reach myself, so I cried out, and the One I couldn't believe came down for me.

## The proudest thing he will ever say

The last act is the chief owning the reversal. "The Chief Got Rescued" is a man laying down twenty years of being the strong one, *I used to think the strong don't need it, I used to think that grace was for the weak*, and calling his rescue the proudest thing he will ever talk about. There is no helmet, no rank, no record of valor that saves a man the way grace does. He did not earn it and he did not deserve it, and that is precisely the point (Ephesians 2:8–9). **Yeshua** is the Son of Man who *came to seek and to save the lost* (Luke 19:10), and there is no such thing as being too far gone for the One who runs the wrong direction, toward the screaming, when the world runs away.

## Who I made this for

I made this for the person who has fixed everything for everyone and cannot reach the one thing that matters, the addiction they cannot beat, the diagnosis, the marriage that is all smoke in the kitchen, the grief they cannot get out of their head. For the skeptic sitting in the cold bay at midnight, half-doubting and half-leaning toward the fire. For everyone who has been the strongest person in the building and is quietly running out of air.

Hear me the way that chief would tell it to one person across a table: you do not have to clean up first, and you do not have to crawl out on your own. You just have to cry the name. There is a hand in the smoke. The same door **Yahuah** opened for a hard old fire chief is open still, the same door, the same hand, the same rescue, and somewhere the bells are going off right now, and He is already running toward you.
