There is a moment in one of Paul's letters that I love the way you love catching a preacher off guard. He is writing to a young leader named Timothy, and most of the letter is exactly the kind of thing you would expect, practical, grounded, a little dry. How to choose the people who will lead. What kind of character to look for. How a person ought to conduct themselves in the household of God. It is the prose of someone running a house: sensible, useful, plain.

And then, without warning, the ground shifts under the sentence. Mid-thought, Paul says that the mystery at the heart of true godliness is great beyond argument, and the moment he says it, his writing stops walking and starts to sing. Six lines come out, short and balanced, rising and falling with a rhythm the surrounding prose does not have. He was manifested in the flesh. Vindicated by the Spirit. Seen by angels. Proclaimed among the nations. Believed on in the world. Taken up in glory. Six beats, and then it is over, and he goes back to his housekeeping as though nothing had happened.

If you read it out loud you can feel the change in your own mouth. The instructions are prose. Those six lines are not. They are patterned, chantable, built to be carried. And most who have studied it closely believe the reason for that is simple and wonderful: Paul was not composing those lines on the spot. He was quoting. He reached, mid-letter, for a song the churches already sang, the way a preacher today might break off from his own words and drop in a line from a hymn everyone in the room could finish, because it says the thing better and truer than fresh words could.

I want to be honest that we cannot prove this the way we can prove the sun rose. Scripture does not stop to tell us, *and here I am citing a hymn.* It is a reading, a very old and widely held one, drawn from the shape and rhythm of the lines and from a handful of other places in the letters where the same thing seems to happen. But hold it in your hand for a moment, because if it is right, and I think it is. It changes the order of things in a way that matters enormously.

## The doctrine was sung before it was filed

We tend to imagine that doctrine came first, cold and precise, hammered out by careful men in careful rooms, and that worship songs came along afterward to warm it up and make it singable, the melody a kind of sugar coating on the medicine of truth. First the theologians settle what is true; then the songwriters set it to music.

But the fossil buried in the middle of that letter suggests the order ran the other way. Before there were creeds ratified by councils, before there were systematic theologies with numbered chapters, before there was a single volume you could pull off a shelf, there were believers, many of whom could not read a word, singing the whole arc of Yeshua in six lines they could carry out the door and never lose. The song came first. The textbooks came later, to explain and defend what the church was already singing on the way home from work.

That is not a small rearrangement. It means that when the first Christians wanted to hold onto the most staggering claim in human history, that the eternal God appeared in a human body, was raised and cleared by the Spirit, was witnessed by the very angels, announced to every nation, trusted across the world, and carried up into glory. They did not reach first for a definition. They reached for a tune. The heaviest theological lifting the church has ever done, it did in the form of a song, because a song was the one container strong enough and portable enough to carry that much weight into that many lives.

## Why a song was the wise choice

Think about who those first believers were. Scattered across an empire, mostly poor, often unable to read, frequently under pressure, meeting in borrowed rooms. You could not hand that church a library; most of them could not have used it, and any scroll could be seized or burned. But you could hand them a song. A song needs no shelf. It cannot be confiscated from a person who has it by heart. It survives a bad memory, a dark room, a long imprisonment, because it is stored in a deeper place than the mind alone. It lives in the rhythm, in the breath, in the body. Give a slave six sung lines about Yeshua and you have given her a Christology that no master can take away and no fire can reach.

And a song does one more thing a definition cannot. It compresses. Those six lines are not a full explanation of the incarnation; they are a full *possession* of it. They take the entire span, from the highest heaven, down into flesh, through death and vindication, out to the nations, and back up into glory, and pack it into something a child could learn before she could spell any of it. The whole descent and ascent of God in one breath. That is not simpler than doctrine. That *is* the doctrine, delivered in the one form that a whole church, top to bottom, could actually keep.

> The church's first theology textbook had no pages. It had a melody, and everyone owned a copy.

## You are singing in an unbroken line

Here is why I tell you all this, and it is not so you will win an argument about how the New Testament was written. It is so that the next time you stand in a room and a song gathers the whole gospel into a few tight lines. He came down, He was raised, He is exalted, He is coming. You will know what you are actually doing. You are not doing something modern. You are not doing something sentimental or soft, a lesser cousin of real theology. You are doing the single oldest Christian thing there is. You are doing the very thing Paul caught the first believers doing and could not resist joining, right there in the middle of his own letter.

The confession has always lived in the song. That is where the church has kept its treasure from the very beginning, not because it was too lazy to write it down, but because it was wise enough to know that a truth this precious needed to be stored somewhere it could not be lost. In the mouths of the people. In the breath of the poor. In six lines a tired believer could hum walking home, carrying the entire mystery of God-made-flesh in a form light enough to hold and strong enough to hold *her.*

So sing the big ones. Sing the songs that dare to put the whole story into a handful of lines. When you do, you are laying the same course of truth the first builders laid, one singable stone at a time, and you are joined, across two thousand years, to a room full of people who could not read the letter Paul was writing, but who already knew the song he stopped to sing.
