There is a version of me I used to keep alive only on Sundays, the one who kept the lights on and the smile steady. And there is the me at 2 a.m. at the kitchen table with the bathroom light off, rehearsing a secret I had never said out loud to anyone. *Grace Redeems* is the album I wrote to get that second man into the light.

I made it because I got tired of preaching a healing I did not yet have. This is a record about shame, the real thing, the specific thing, the thing you have buried under Monday smiles, and about the grace that meets it there and does not flinch.

## Shame thrives in silence, so the album begins by breaking it

The opener is called "Honest Reckoning," and it is exactly that. No band. Just a Rhodes chord, a fingerpicked guitar, and a man saying the thing at last: *Father, here is the truth I buried. I am tired of pretending.* I anchored it to the plainest promise in the New Testament, *if we confess our sins, he is faithful and just to forgive us and to cleanse us* (1 John 1:9), and to David's dangerous prayer, *search me, O God, and know my heart* (Psalm 139:23–24). Because shame's whole strategy is silence. The naming is the first half of the freedom. You cannot be met in a room you refuse to admit you are standing in.

Then, on "The Weight Lifts," I sing the part that took me years to understand. I had carried something strapped to my back like a second spine, and I had mistaken it for conviction, mistaken it for humility. It was only condemnation. There is a difference, and the difference is everything: *there is therefore now no condemnation for those who are in Christ Jesus* (Romans 8:1). Conviction is the kind hand of **Yahuah** turning you toward home. Condemnation is a bag He never asked you to hold. On this record a man finally sets it down and discovers he is breathing different, lighter, not because he minimized the sin, but because the Son already bore it.

> I mistook it for conviction. I mistook it for humility. Father, it was only condemnation.

## Grace is loudest on the worst day

The center of the album is a single sentence I would put on the wall of every hiding heart: *grace met the worst day.* That is the song, and it is the testimony compressed to five words. **Yeshua** did not wait for me to clean up before He arrived. On the worst day of my life He walked into the room without knocking, hands open, His name on the air, and the worst day got smaller in His light. This is the scandalous math of Paul: *where sin increased, grace abounded all the more* (Romans 5:20). Not grace that matches the sin, grace that overwhelms it. And when I begged for a healing that felt like fireworks, what I got instead was the quiet word of the Lord: *my grace is sufficient for you, for my power is made perfect in weakness* (2 Corinthians 12:9).

From there the record hands you back your future. "The Failure Is Not the Future" is built on the truth that the past does not get to write the next page, the Author does. *Forgetting what lies behind and straining forward to what lies ahead* (Philippians 3:13–14), and *neither things present nor things to come* can separate you from His love (Romans 8:38–39). The failure is a paragraph. It is not the title page.

## A new name, and the courage to stand up

Then comes the turn I love most. On "New Name Spoken," the Father does not name me by what I had done. He names me by what He had paid. I built that song on the promise in Revelation, *to the one who conquers I will give a white stone, with a new name written on it that no one knows except the one who receives it* (Revelation 2:17), and Isaiah's word that *you shall be called by a new name that the mouth of the Lord will give* (Isaiah 62:2). The old name was the failure I had repeated in my own head for twenty-two years. The new name has authority the old one lost the moment it landed.

And "Standing Tall" is the body finally agreeing with the testimony. It is a man in a church basement, on a folding chair, standing up in a small group and saying it all out loud, and the room does not shrink back; the room leans in. The standing is not pride. It is restored dignity. *Having done all, to stand* (Ephesians 6:13), and the promise that undergirds the whole thing: *the Lord is able to make him stand* (Romans 14:4).

## Who I made this for, and why it does not end with you

The last movement of the album refuses to let redemption be private. "Lifting Others Out of Hiding" is a man across a diner booth at 11 p.m., pouring the second cup and listening while a friend finally says his own secret, because *confess your sins to one another, and pray for one another, that you may be healed* (James 5:16). The freed become the freeing. And the finale, "Grace Forever," ends the only way redemption can, which is to say it does not end: *for by grace you have been saved through faith, and this is not your own doing; it is the gift of God* (Ephesians 2:8–9). Grace keeps moving because the freed keep passing it forward. Leave the porch light on. Pour the second cup. Save the folding chair for the next saint.

So hear me, whoever is reading this at 2 a.m. with the light off: the room you are afraid of is the room where He finds you. You do not have to say it perfectly. You just have to say it. The weight you have called humility for years was only condemnation, and it was never yours to carry. Name it, lay it on the table, and watch what grace does with the worst day of your life.
