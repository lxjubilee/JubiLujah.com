Let me tell you something I do not usually say out loud.

I have written far more songs than you will ever hear. Whole albums of them, melodies with every note settled, verses that took me weeks, choruses I still hum walking to the kitchen. Most of them have never been sung by anyone but me, alone, into the quiet of a room. They exist the way a letter exists in a sealed envelope no one has opened. Finished. And silent.

For a long stretch of my life this felt like the plainest kind of failure. You are supposed to have something to show. A song that no one has heard can feel like a song that does not quite count, a tree falling where no ear is standing, making, they say, no sound. I would open the drawer, look at all that work, and feel the particular ache of the maker whose making has gone nowhere anyone can see.

Maybe you know a version of that drawer. It does not have to hold songs. It can hold the degree you never used. The business plan in a folder. The apology you wrote and never sent. The child you raised who cannot see yet what your years cost. The prayers you have prayed for a decade with nothing visible to point to. There is a particular loneliness in having poured yourself into something real, and watching it go quiet, and wondering if quiet is the same thing as wasted.

I want to sit with you in front of that drawer, because I have learned something there that I did not expect.

## The difference between a grave and a garden

Jesus told a story about a man who took a seed and threw it into the ground. And then, this is the part I love, the man went to bed. He slept and rose, slept and rose, and the seed sprouted and grew, and he could not have told you how. The earth did it. Quietly, in the dark, without asking his permission or requiring his attention. He woke one morning and there was a blade where there had been bare dirt, and all he had done was trust the ground with something and walk away.

Here is what stopped me the first time I really heard that. A seed only works when it is buried. You cannot keep it on a shelf where you can see it and admire it and know it is safe. Kept in the light where nothing can touch it, a seed simply stays a seed forever, hard, whole, and utterly barren. The burial is not the enemy of the seed. The burial is the beginning of the wheat.

So the drawer is not automatically a grave. It might be a garden. And the difference between the two is not whether anyone is watching. The difference is what put the thing in the dark.

Jesus told another story, and I have to be careful with this one because it cuts the opposite way. A master gave his servants money to steward, and one of them was so afraid of losing it that he dug a hole and buried it and gave it back untouched, and the master was not pleased. People sometimes read those two stories together and get frightened: is my drawer the seed that will rise, or the coin I buried out of fear?

The answer is in the reason you put it there.

The seed goes into the dark in hope. It is an act of trust. You commit something you love to a process you do not control, believing the ground will do what you cannot. The coin went into the dark in fear. The servant said as much: I was afraid, so I hid it. One burial is faith. The other is dread wearing the costume of caution. And you always know which one you are doing, if you are honest, because faith plants and then rests, while fear buries and then keeps checking the hole.

## The song was never only for the room

I used to believe a song was not finished until a crowd had heard it. Now I am not so sure the crowd was ever the point.

Something happens in me when I write a song that is true, whether or not it is ever performed. It changes the writer. The reaching-for-God that a melody requires, the honesty it drags up out of you, the way you cannot fake a chorus you do not mean, that work is done in me the moment the song is made, in the empty room, with no one there. The audience was never the only thing being formed. I was.

And there is a listener in every empty room.

The Scriptures say that God sings over his people, that he quiets them with his love and rejoices over them with singing. I take enormous comfort in that image, because it means the direction of song is not only from us upward. There is singing coming the other way. And a God who himself sings over the ones he loves is not a God who needs a stadium to count a song as heard. He was there in the room. He heard the one no one else heard. He does not measure a life's work by the size of the audience, because he has been, from the beginning, an audience of One who misses nothing.

## What the dark is doing

I cannot promise you that every song in your drawer will one day fill a room. I will not lie to you like that. Isaiah says God gives seed to the sower and bread to the eater, and that his word does not come back to him empty. It does what he sends it to do. But he never promised the sower he would get to watch. Some seed rises in a season you can see. Some rises after you are gone, in soil you will never visit, in a life you will never meet. The farmer sleeps. The growing is not his job.

So this is what I do now, in front of the drawer.

I stop asking whether anyone has heard it. I ask instead whether it was true, whether it was made in hope, whether it was given honestly to the ground. If it was, then it is not dead. It is planted. And a planted thing keeps its own clock, underground, in the patient dark, doing the one thing a seed knows how to do.

You have a drawer too. Before you decide it is a grave, look again at how the thing got in there. If you buried it in fear, there is grace to dig it up and try. But if you planted it in hope, if you gave your real work to God and could not make it grow and finally, exhausted, let the ground have it, then hear me.

That is not a wasted song.

That is a seed.

And the One who tends that field has never once forgotten what is coming up in the dark.
