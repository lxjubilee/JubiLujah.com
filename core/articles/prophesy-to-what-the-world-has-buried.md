The hand of Yahuah came on the prophet Ezekiel, and set him down in the worst place I can imagine anyone being asked to worship.

A valley. And the valley was full of bones. Not fresh graves, not the recently fallen, the text is almost cruel in its precision. He was led back and forth among them, all the way around, so he could take in the full scope of it, and what he saw was that there were *very many,* and they were *very dry.* This was not a battlefield with the smoke still rising. This was old death. Death so complete that the flesh was long gone and even the bones had bleached out in the sun. Whatever had lived here had been dead so long there was nothing left to grieve, only to catalogue.

And Yahuah asks him a question that must have felt like a taunt. *Son of man, can these bones live?*

I love how Ezekiel answers, because it is the most honest thing a person can say when they are staring at something that dead. He does not manufacture a triumphant *yes.* He does not despair into a flat *no.* He says: *O Sovereign Lord, You alone know.* Which is prophet-speak for: everything I can see says no, and I am not going to pretend otherwise, but I have learned that what I can see is not the whole story, and the verdict is Yours, not mine.

That answer is worth memorizing, because you are going to need it. It is the exact posture of faith standing over a hopeless thing: honest about the death, and unwilling to be the one who declares it final.

## The assignment that sounds insane

And then Yahuah tells him what to do, and it is the strangest command in a book full of strange ones.

*Prophesy to these bones.* Speak to them. Say to the dry bones, hear the word of Yahuah.

Stop and feel how absurd that is. There is no one to preach to. A valley of bones is the least promising congregation in the history of the world. Bones cannot hear. Bones cannot respond. Bones are, by every measure available, past the reach of words. If you wanted to design a scene to make a preacher feel foolish, you could not do better than one man standing in a field of skeletons, opening his mouth, and *talking to them.*

But that is the assignment. Not to bury them. They are already as buried as death gets. Not to weep over them, the time for weeping was centuries ago. To *speak life over them.* To stand in the middle of the most hopeless thing in the landscape and address it as though it had a future.

And I have to tell you plainly: this is the assignment a great many of you have been given, and you have mistaken it for a funeral.

You are standing over a valley of dry bones. A marriage that has been dead so long there is nothing tender left to mourn. A prodigal whose heart went cold years ago. A church that everyone in town has already written the obituary for. A dream, a calling, a nation, a person, that has been past the reach of hope for so long that even you have stopped expecting anything. And you have been treating your assignment as a graveside service, showing up to grieve, to tend the plot, to keep the memory decent.

But Yahuah did not put you in that valley to hold a funeral. He put you there to prophesy. To open your mouth and speak His word, and yes, His song, over the very thing you have privately buried.

## The sound comes before the life

So Ezekiel obeys. He preaches to a field of bones. And while he is still speaking, something begins to happen, and the first thing that happens is a *sound.*

The text says there was a noise, a rattling, and the bones began to come together, bone to bone. As he kept prophesying, sinews formed on them, and flesh came up, and skin covered them over. The valley of scattered death reassembled itself into row upon row of whole bodies, right there in front of him, while the word was still in the air.

I want you to notice the order, because it is the pattern for everyone who worships over a dead place. The sound came first, and the coming-together followed the sound. The word went out over the bones before a single bone had moved, and the movement answered the word. Ezekiel did not wait to see the bones twitch before he was willing to speak. He spoke into total stillness, on nothing but the command of Yahuah, and the stillness broke open *because* he spoke.

That is what a song over a dead valley is. It is not a reaction to the resurrection. It is the sound the resurrection comes up through. You do not wait until you see the marriage soften, the prodigal turn, the church revive, and *then* start declaring the goodness of God over it. You declare it into the silence, over the bones, before there is one shred of evidence, and the coming-together answers the declaration.

## But standing is not yet living

And here is the detail I most need you to see, because it is where most of us lose heart.

When the bones had all come together, sinew, flesh, skin, whole bodies filling the valley, the text stops and says something devastating. *But there was no breath in them.*

They were assembled. They were whole. They looked, for the first time in ages, like something. And they were still not alive. A valley full of perfectly formed corpses is, in some ways, an even harder place to keep believing than a valley of honest bones, because now it looks *so close* and it is still not moving. That is the cruelest stretch of any long-dead thing coming back, the stage where the pieces are in place and the life has not yet arrived.

Some of you are living in exactly that gap right now. The bones have come together. Things have improved on paper. The structure is back, the form has returned, it looks better than it did, and there is still no breath. And you are tempted to read the missing breath as failure, as proof that the whole thing was a false hope after all.

But look at what Yahuah tells Ezekiel to do. He does not tell him to give up because the bodies are not breathing yet. He tells him to prophesy *again*, this time to the breath itself. *Come from the four winds, O breath, and breathe on these slain, that they may live.* And Ezekiel obeys a second time, and the breath comes into them, and they live, and they stand on their feet, an exceedingly great army where a moment before there had been only bones.

Here is the mercy hidden in the two stages. The coming-together is often the part you can see, and the breath is always the part only Yahuah can give. Ruach, the word for breath here is the same word for wind, and the same word for the Spirit of God. You can prophesy the bones together. You cannot manufacture the breath. That comes from Him, on His timing, from the four winds. Your job is to keep speaking; His job is to breathe. And the gap between the assembled body and the first breath is not your failure. It is the space where you prophesy again and wait for the wind.

## The honest word, and the invitation

Let me be a truthful prophet before I let you go, because this vision was given to a people in exile who felt exactly the despair you feel, and it did not pretend their bones were not dry.

Yahuah tells Ezekiel what the valley means: these bones are the whole house of Israel, and they are saying, *our bones are dried up, our hope is lost, we are cut off completely.* That is the real sentence underneath every dry valley, *our hope is lost.* And Yahuah does not scold them for feeling it. He meets it. He does not promise the breath will come on their schedule, and I will not promise you it will come on yours. Some valleys stay bones for a long time, and the ones who prophesy over them faithfully do not always live to see the army stand. This is not a formula where enough declaring guarantees a resurrection by spring.

But hear the thing the vision exists to say, and let it get all the way down into your dried-up places: *what looks past all hope is not past the reach of the word of Yahuah.* He specializes in the valley everyone else has catalogued as final. He asks *can these bones live* not because He is unsure, but because He wants you to hear yourself say *You alone know*, and then to open your mouth anyway.

So go stand over the thing you buried. Stop treating your assignment as a graveside. Prophesy. Speak, and sing, the goodness and the promise of Yahuah over the dry and the still and the long-dead, before you see one bone move. And when the pieces come together but the breath has not yet come, do not quit in the gap. Prophesy again. Call for the wind.

The valley is not your funeral. It is your congregation. Open your mouth.
