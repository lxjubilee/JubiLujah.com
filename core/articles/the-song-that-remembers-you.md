Her daughter told me the story with her hand pressed flat against her own chest, the way people do when they are holding something in.

Her mother had been slipping for a long time. The names had gone first, then the faces, then the thread of a sentence, then the daughter's own name, which was the hardest morning of all: to walk into the room she had walked into a thousand times and be met with the polite, pleasant, terrible smile you give a kind stranger. The woman who had raised her no longer knew who she was. There is no gentle way to carry that.

And then one afternoon someone in the care home began to play an old hymn. One of the ancient ones, the kind that woman had sung every Sunday for seventy years, back when Sundays and years and everything else still held together.

And her mother sang it. Every word. In order. All the verses, the ones nobody remembers past the first, the ones you have to read off the page even when your mind is whole. Sang them through, in tune, with feeling, her face open and unclouded, as though the fog had rolled back for exactly the length of the song and set her down, whole, in a place she still completely knew. She could not name her own daughter sitting beside her. But she knew every word of the song.

Her daughter said, *it was like she was still in there. Like the song went and found her.*

I have not stopped thinking about it since, because there is a real reason it happened, and the reason is one of the most quietly staggering things we have learned about how we are made.

## Stored somewhere deeper

The kind of memory that fails first in a mind that is coming apart, the names, the appointments, the faces, the what-did-I-come-into-this-room-for, is stored in one region of the brain. It is fragile. It is often the first thing the disease takes.

But music does not live only there. A deeply known song, one sung a thousand times, bound up with melody and rhythm and emotion and the movements of your own mouth, gets laid down in *many* places at once, woven through parts of the brain that the disease reaches late, or sometimes never. Which means a person can lose the memory of their own wedding and still sing every word of the song that was played at it. The song is not filed with the facts. It is written deeper, in a place the erosion does not reach, held by a self that is still there long after the surface has gone quiet. This is not sentiment. It is why music therapists can walk into a locked and wordless silence with a familiar tune and watch a person surface, eyes clearing, mouth moving, *someone home*, for as long as the music lasts.

The song outlives the memory of the song. What you sang deeply enough, long enough, gets stored below the water line, where the storms that take everything else cannot get to it.

And the moment I understood that, I understood something about worship I had never had words for, and something about God I badly needed.

## What you sing gets stored below the waterline

The Scriptures talk this way about the deep things, as buried treasure, planted seed, words hidden down where they cannot be stolen. One of the old songwriters said he had *hidden God's word in his heart*, not memorized it for a test, *hidden* it, buried it somewhere internal and safe, so that it would be there when he needed it and could not have reached for it deliberately. I always read that as a discipline, a technique. Now I read it as a mercy built into the architecture of a human being. There is a place in you below the waterline, and the things you put there, sung, repeated, loved into the deep grain of you, get held in a keeping that outlasts the parts of you that fail.

This is why I have stopped being embarrassed about repetition in worship. There is a modern nervousness about singing the same true things over and over, as if depth required constant novelty. But you are not only enjoying a song when you sing it for the hundredth time. You are *burying* it. You are lowering it, word by word, past the fragile surface memory and down into the deep keeping, laying up something that will still be there on a day you cannot picture, a day when the surface has gone dim and you can no longer reach for God on purpose, and something older than your failing mind will start to sing, and the song will come and find you.

You are, right now, choosing what your soul will still know when it can no longer know much. That is not a small thing to be doing on an ordinary Sunday.

## He does not forget

But here is where the whole thing turns, and where I would sit down beside you and say it softly, because it is the part that steadies me most.

The terror underneath a story like that woman's is not really about melody. It is the fear of being *forgotten*, of a self dissolving, of a person you love vanishing behind their own eyes, of one day being someone no one recognizes, and worse, of being someone who no longer recognizes God, whose faith fades along with the faces, who reaches the end unable to hold onto the One they spent a life holding.

And to that specific fear the Scriptures speak with a tenderness I can hardly get through. Yahuah says, to His people growing old: *even to your old age I am He, and to your gray hairs I will carry you. I made you, and I will bear you; I will carry, and I will save.* Hear the direction of it. It does not say *keep holding on to Me.* It says *I will carry you.* The strength in that promise is not your grip on Him. It is His grip on you, and His does not loosen when yours does. Another of the old songs pleads, *do not cast me off in the time of old age; do not forsake me when my strength is spent*, and the whole weight of Scripture answers: He won't. He cannot forget you, because you were never being held by your memory of Him. You were being held by His memory of you, and His does not fail.

That is the deepest thing the song in the care home was telling us. When that woman sang every word she could no longer explain, it was a picture, in miniature, of a rescue larger than the song. Something in her was still held, still whole, still reachable, waiting below the waterline the whole time the surface was failing, and a familiar melody was enough to reach down and touch it, and prove it was still there. Even in her, even then, someone was home. And if a mere song, buried deep enough, can go and find a person the world had given up as gone, how much more the God who buried Himself deeper still, who wrote His own faithfulness into a place no disease can reach, and who has promised to come, at the very end, and carry home the ones who can no longer carry themselves.

## Bury the true things now

So here is what I want to leave with you, whether you are young and whole or already watching the surface of someone you love begin to go.

Sing the true things now, and sing them deep. Not for the feeling of the moment, for the keeping. Bury them below the waterline, in the place the storms cannot reach, so that on the day you cannot summon a single fact about God on purpose, the deep grain of you will still know how the song goes.

And do not be afraid of the fog, for yourself or for the ones already in it. The self you fear losing is held lower than the part that fails, in a keeping older than your memory, by a God who has promised to carry you when your strength is spent and to come, at the last, and sing you the rest of the way home.

The song remembers you when you cannot remember it. And He remembers you even then, especially then, most of all then.
