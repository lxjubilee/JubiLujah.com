Of everything John was shown in that vision of the throne room, one image keeps circling back to me, because it does not behave the way a reward is supposed to behave.

Around the throne he sees twenty-four elders. They are seated on thrones of their own. They are dressed in white. And on their heads are crowns of gold. So far, this is a picture of honor, these are dignified, elevated, *rewarded* figures, given seats and robes and crowns.

But watch what they actually *do* with the honor. Every time the living creatures near the throne give glory, the twenty-four elders fall down, and they *take off their crowns and throw them*, down, at the foot of the throne, before the One seated there. And then, presumably, they pick them up, and rank comes, and they do it again. Over and over. The whole vision of them is a picture of people who cannot stop *casting down* the very thing they were given to wear.

That is the sound of the highest worship in the Bible. Not a solo. Not even a song, first, a *gesture.* The clatter of every crown in the room hitting the same floor at the same feet.

## These crowns were not given cheaply

You have to feel the weight of what is being thrown, or the scene goes soft on you.

The word John uses for these crowns is not the word for a king's inherited diadem. It is the word for the *victor's wreath*, the crown you won. The one placed on the head of the runner who finished, the fighter who prevailed, the one who endured to the end. It is the crown Scripture elsewhere promises to those who love Yahuah, who stay faithful under trial, who run the race and do not quit. It is *earned.* It is *deserved,* in the truest sense the word can carry.

Who the twenty-four elders are, the vision does not spell out, and I will not pretend to more certainty than the text gives. Many have seen in the number an echo of the covenant people and the apostles together, or of the twenty-four courses of temple worship come to their fulfillment. Hold those gently. But whoever they represent, this much the picture makes unmistakable: they are the *rewarded.* The ones who came through. The ones with something on their heads worth keeping.

And that is exactly what makes the gesture so overwhelming. It is easy to bow with empty hands. It costs nothing to lay down a reward you never received. But these are people holding the genuine article, real crowns, really won, really theirs, and the truest instinct of their worship is to get them *off* their own heads and onto the floor before the throne as fast and as often as they can.

> An empty-handed bow is humility. A crowned head that keeps throwing the crown down is worship. These are the ones with the most to keep, and they cannot stop giving it away.

## Where all the distinctions fall

Now widen the lens, because the throne room does not stay small. As the vision opens out, the worship swells until John sees a multitude no one could count, from *every* nation, every tribe, every people, every language, gathered into one enormous song before the same throne.

And here is what happens to all the differences. They do not get erased. John still *sees* the nations, the tribes, the tongues; the distinctions are still there, still beautiful, still gloriously many. But not one of them is any longer a *ranking.* Every crown is on the same floor. Every people is at the same throne. The martyr and the latecomer, the great apostle and the unknown believer from a village whose name never made a single record, all of it laid down in the same place, all of it facing the same light, all of it singing the same worthiness to the same Lamb.

That is the harmony this whole vision has been building toward. Not the flattening of everyone into sameness, heaven keeps every nation and tongue, keeps the whole staggering variety. But the *dissolving of every distinction into an offering.* On earth our differences are constantly being sorted into hierarchies: who is ahead, who is more, who has earned the better seat. In the throne room those same differences remain, but the sorting is *over,* because everyone has thrown their sorting-token, their crown, their rank, their reward, onto the same floor. When there is no more ladder, difference stops being competition and finally becomes *harmony.* You can only truly sing *with* someone once you are no longer measuring yourself *against* them.

## What we do with what we've earned

Let me bring this all the way down to where you live, because this vision is a rehearsal for something, and the something is now.

You have crowns. Maybe not many, maybe not the ones you wanted, but you have them, the thing you built, the trial you endured, the ministry that bears fruit, the reputation you earned, the gift that genuinely is better than the next person's. Real crowns. Really yours. And the daily temptation, the very air of this age, is to *keep them on your head*, to let what you have won become your seat, your standing, your quiet sense that you are, in some measure, ahead.

The elders show you the other way to hold a crown. Wear it lightly enough that it comes off easily. Win everything you can win, run the race, endure, be faithful, let Yahuah crown you, and then practice, now, the one motion the redeemed will be doing forever: taking it off and setting it down before a throne that is not yours. Not because the crown is worthless. Because the One at the throne is worth so much more that keeping the crown on your own head, in his presence, becomes simply *unthinkable.*

And notice what that gesture does to a room. When you stop wearing your reward as a rank, you become able to worship *shoulder to shoulder* with people you would otherwise be measuring yourself against. The person whose gift outshines yours, the one further ahead, the one further behind, none of it is a threat anymore, because all of it is on the floor. The casting down of the crowns is not only how we honor Yahuah. It is the only way a crowned and unequal people ever become *one* worshipping body. The distinctions fall, and the harmony rises in the exact space they leave.

So whatever you have won, hold it the way the elders do. Loosely. Gladly. Ready to throw. And when you gather with a roomful of others who are learning the same motion, listen for it, the sound of every reward in the room landing at the same feet, and every unlike voice, freed at last from the ladder, rising as one.

That is where all of this is going. We may as well start setting our crowns down now.
