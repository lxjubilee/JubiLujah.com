Paul is finishing a long letter to a difficult church in a Greek city. He has argued, corrected, encouraged, and now he is signing off in his own hand. And in the last breath of the letter, in the middle of all that Greek, he writes one word that is not Greek at all. He writes it in Aramaic, the everyday language of Yeshua, the tongue of the first believers back in Jerusalem, and he does not translate it. He just sets it down, raw, in a city that could barely say it: **Maranatha.**

Think about how strange that is. Paul is the great translator of the faith, the man who carried the gospel out of its Hebrew cradle into the wide Gentile world, who worked so hard to make the message speak to every people. And yet here, at the most personal moment of the letter, he refuses to translate. He leaves the word in the mother tongue. Some things, apparently, are too precious to hand across the border in another language. Some words you keep in the tongue they were born in.

## What the word is reaching for

So what does it mean? Here we have to be honest and careful, because the Aramaic can be divided in two ways, and both are beautiful.

Split one way, *Marana tha*. It means **"Our Lord, come!"** It is a cry, a plea, an outstretched-hands prayer. *Come. We are waiting. Do not delay.* Split the other way, *Maran atha*. It means **"Our Lord has come,"** or *is coming*, a confession, a settled certainty. Most who have studied it lean toward the first: the imperative, the longing, the *come.* But I love that the word holds both, because that is exactly where the scattered church has always lived, between *He has come* and *come again,* between the memory and the ache, one foot in what is finished and one hand reaching toward what is not yet.

At its root are two simple pieces: *Mar*, the Aramaic word for *Lord*, *Maran*, **our** Lord, and the verb *to come.* That is all it is. *Our Lord, come.* Two heartbeats of a sentence. And the early believers said it so often, and held it so dear, that it traveled untranslated into churches full of people who had no idea how the grammar worked. They said it the way you keep a word your grandmother used, not because you have parsed it, but because it carries her in it.

## The handful of words no one would translate

Maranatha was not alone. As the faith moved out into every culture, a small handful of words simply refused to be translated, and the church let them stay foreign on purpose.

*Abba*, the intimate Aramaic word a child uses for a father, stayed *Abba* in Greek letters written to people who did not speak Aramaic. *Amen* stayed *Amen.* *Hosanna*, the old cry meaning *save us now,* stayed *Hosanna.* And *Maranatha* stayed *Maranatha.* These were the words the church decided it would rather keep whole than make convenient. They are the family words, the ones so worn smooth by love that translating them would feel like sanding off the fingerprints.

> Some words you translate so the whole world can understand them. A few you leave untranslated so the whole world can share them.

There is something in that I want you to catch, because it is the opposite of what you would expect. You might think the mark of a truly global faith would be that *everything* gets translated into everything, until no foreign word survives. But the early church did the reverse with these few. It let a scattered people, speaking a hundred languages, all say the same untranslated Aramaic cry, so that a believer in Rome and a believer in Antioch and a believer in some far village, none of whom shared a tongue, could still say one word together and mean the same longing. The untranslated word became the shared word. The foreignness was the point. It kept the family speaking with one voice across every border.

## The cry the gathered church kept saying

We know this word was not just an academic phrase, because the early church kept it in the middle of its worship. One of the oldest surviving descriptions of how the first believers gathered, a short manual from the generations right after the apostles, not Scripture but a real window into how they prayed, records the congregation crying *Maranatha* as they came to the Lord's table. Tradition preserved it right there at the meal, at the moment they remembered His body broken and looked ahead to His return: *Our Lord, come.*

Picture it. A room of ordinary people, many of them poor, many of them from peoples that had only recently heard the Name at all, breaking bread in the shadow of an empire that could crush them. And the word on their lips is not a slogan of resistance or a demand for rescue from Rome. It is a homesick cry for a Person. *Marana tha.* Our Lord, come. They were not mainly asking for their circumstances to change. They were asking for *Him.* The whole weight of their hope had narrowed down to one word in one tongue: come.

And here is the thing that gives me chills. That same cry stands at the very end of your Bible. The final book closes, after all its thunder and all its visions, with a small Greek prayer that is simply *Maranatha* wearing a Greek coat: *Come, Lord Yeshua.* The Spirit and the Bride say come. The Aramaic longing that Paul left untranslated at the end of a letter is the same longing that closes the entire canon of Scripture. The book of God ends not with a period but with an outstretched hand. The last word of the whole story is, essentially, *come.*

## For the scattered and the far-off

I carry worship into places where the church is small, guarded, and far from any center, where believers meet quietly, in tongues that no famous hymn was ever written in, in countries the maps call closed. And *Maranatha* is a word I love to leave with them, precisely because it is not mine and not theirs. It belongs to none of us and to all of us. It is older than every one of our languages, worn smooth by two thousand years of frightened and faithful people saying it in the dark.

To the believer who feels too foreign, too far out, too newly arrived to have any real place in the great song of the church, hear this. You do not need a polished vocabulary of worship. You do not need to master the language the insiders use. You can say the same one word the very first believers said, the word Yeshua's own friends said in His own tongue, the word that closes the Bible: *come.* Two heartbeats. Our Lord, come. It fits in any mouth, in any nation, on any night.

That is the mercy of a cry too holy to translate. It levels everyone. The scholar and the new believer, the insider and the outsider, the fluent and the fumbling, all say the exact same untranslated word, and Heaven hears it exactly the same. There is no accent that spoils it. There is no dialect that misses it.

So when words run out, and they will, reach for the one that never needed translating in the first place. Lift your hand toward the dawn and say it with the whole scattered family across every century and every border.

*Marana tha.* Our Lord, come.
