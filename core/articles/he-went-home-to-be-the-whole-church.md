The strangest evangelism story in the Scriptures happens on an empty road, with no crowd, no building, no follow-up plan, and exactly one convert, and it may be the most far-reaching one of them all.

There was a man traveling home along the desert road that ran down from Jerusalem toward Gaza, the road out toward the edge of everything. He was a court official from Ethiopia, a high one, the treasurer of the queen, a man of real power in a kingdom at the far southern rim of the known world. He was also a eunuch, which mattered enormously in that world, because the old law had a line about men like him too, the same kind of small print that kept certain people at the edge of the assembly, close enough to look in, never quite allowed all the way to the center. So here was a man who had come a very long way to worship in Jerusalem, and had almost certainly been kept at the fringe of the very temple he had traveled to reach. A foreigner. A eunuch. Powerful in his own land, and quietly excluded in the one he had crossed the world to visit.

He was riding home from all of that, and he had a scroll of the prophet Isaiah open across his lap, reading it aloud to himself in the moving chariot, trying to understand a passage about a servant led like a lamb to the slaughter, one who was humiliated and denied justice and cut off from the land of the living. He did not understand it. And he said so, out loud, to no one, on an empty road, going home.

## God moved a preacher to reach one man

Now watch what Yahuah does, because the scale of it tells you His heart.

There was a man named Philip, in the middle of a wildly fruitful ministry in a city where crowds were coming to faith and miracles were breaking out, the kind of assignment any of us would guard with both hands. And the Spirit pulled him off of it and sent him to a desert road. Not to a crowd. To a road. To wait for a single chariot. Heaven interrupted a revival to reach one foreigner riding home alone.

Philip runs alongside the chariot, hears the man reading the prophet aloud, and asks him the question that starts everything: *do you understand what you are reading?* And the official gives one of the most honest answers in all the Scriptures, *how can I, unless someone guides me?*, and he invites the stranger up to sit beside him. So there, on a moving chariot on an empty road, Philip begins with that very passage of Isaiah, the servant led to the slaughter, and he tells the man the good news of Yeshua, that the suffering one the prophet saw was the Messiah, cut off not for His own sin but for the world's, and raised, and alive, and for him.

And the foreigner believes. Right there. On the road. And they come to some water, a pool, a stream, the account does not fuss over the details, and the man says, with the eagerness of someone who has just found the thing he crossed the world to look for: *look, here is water. What is stopping me from being baptized?* And the answer, gloriously, is nothing. Nothing is stopping him. Not his nation. Not his body. Not the small print. Not the fringe he had been kept on in Jerusalem. Nothing. So Philip baptizes the excluded man in a roadside pool, and the moment he comes up out of the water, the Spirit takes Philip away, and the official never sees him again.

And here is the line the whole story has been building toward, the one I cannot read without something catching in my chest: he went on his way rejoicing.

## One man carrying a nation's whole church

Stop and consider what actually rode away down that road.

There was no church in Ethiopia. There was no congregation waiting to receive him, no elders, no building, no other believer in a thousand miles who could sing the songs he had just learned to love. Philip was gone. The apostles were far behind him in Jerusalem. Ahead of him were weeks of desert and then a kingdom that had never heard the name of Yeshua spoken once. And into all of that emptiness rode one man, a new believer of a few hours, baptized in a puddle by a stranger who had already vanished, carrying, in his single rejoicing body, the entire church of a nation that did not yet exist.

He was the whole thing. He was the congregation and the choir and the first witness all at once. Everything that would ever be sung to Yeshua in his homeland was, at that moment, riding in one chariot, in one heart, out toward the edge of the world. The oldest traditions of the church in that part of Africa look back to this road, to this man, as their beginning, though the Scriptures themselves do not follow him home, and I will be honest about where the record ends and where the tradition takes over. What the Scriptures do tell us is the essential thing: that he went home rejoicing, and that he went home carrying the only fire for a thousand miles.

## The song only needs one carrier

I have spent my working life carrying worship into guarded and unreached places, in the tongues of hard countries, Arabic, Turkish, Romanian, the languages spoken where there is no congregation to receive you and no building to sing in. And this story on the Gaza road is the one I return to when the smallness of the work threatens to discourage me, because it corrects a lie I keep having to unlearn.

The lie is that the gospel travels by crowds. That a nation is reached by campaigns, by numbers, by the full apparatus of a movement arriving in strength. Sometimes, thank Yahuah, it does. But look at how the whole church of Ethiopia may have begun: not with an army of missionaries, but with one excluded foreigner, one honest question on an empty road, one afternoon of Scripture explained beside a stranger, one baptism in a roadside pool, and one man riding home rejoicing with the fire in his chest and nobody beside him. The song does not need a choir to cross a border. It needs one carrier who cannot stop singing it.

That is unbelievably good news for anyone who feels like the only believer in their whole world. The one at work who has no one. The one in a family, a village, a country, a language where they are the entire congregation, where they are, right now, the whole church of a nation that does not yet know it has one. Heaven has interrupted revivals to reach people like you on empty roads, precisely because it knows what a single rejoicing carrier can plant in a place that has never heard.

## Go on your way rejoicing

So take the ending of the story for your own, wherever this reaches you.

You may be riding home to a place with no one in it who shares the song. You may have been kept, all your life, on the fringe, near enough to look in, never quite allowed to the center, marked by some line of small print you never chose. The man on the Gaza road was all of that, and Yahuah crossed a desert to reach him, and answered his one honest question, and left nothing standing between him and the water. And then He sent him home, not to wait for a church to arrive, but to *be* one, in one rejoicing body, all the way to the edge of the world.

Nothing is stopping you. Go on your way rejoicing. You may be the whole church of a nation that has not been born yet, and the fire you are carrying is enough to start it.
