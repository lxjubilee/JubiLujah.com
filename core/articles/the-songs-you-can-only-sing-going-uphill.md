Three times a year, whole families put on their sandals, packed bread and dried fruit into a cloth, and started walking toward Jerusalem. They walked for days. And they walked, almost the whole way, uphill.

That last part is not a figure of speech. Jerusalem sits high in the Judean hills, and nearly every road to it climbs. In the Hebrew way of speaking you never went "over" to the city and never went "down" to it from the surrounding country. You went *up*. Up to the feast. Up to the house. The geography and the language agreed: to come to the presence of Yahuah was an ascent, a going-up, a climb that made your legs burn before it ever touched your heart.

And they had songs for the walk.

Tucked into the middle of the Hebrew songbook is a small, tight cluster of fifteen short psalms, each one wearing the same heading at the top. In Hebrew that heading is *Shir HaMa'alot*, a song of the goings-up, the ascents, the steps. Fifteen of them, in a row. And the old understanding of what they were for is beautifully simple: these were the songs the pilgrims sang on the road, climbing toward the city, and later the songs sung as worshipers came up to the house itself.

I want you to notice what kind of songs a person actually needs on a long climb, because it tells you everything about how these were built.

They are short. Almost every one of them is brief enough to hold in your mouth without a scroll, a handful of lines, portable, learnable by a child on your shoulders. They are not grand cathedral pieces meant to be performed by a trained soloist in a still room. They are walking music. Crowd music. The kind of song a tired family can carry over a rise and pick up again on the far side.

And here is the thing that has stayed with me for years: they are honest about the walk.

The stack does not open in Jerusalem. It opens far away. The very first of these ascent-songs is the cry of someone stuck in a distant place, worn down by living among people who hate peace, aching to be somewhere else. That is where the climb begins, not on the mountaintop, but in the low country of longing, homesick and not yet moving. The first uphill song is sung by someone who isn't home and knows it.

Then, step by step, the little collection climbs.

Somewhere along the road one of them lifts its eyes to the hills and asks where help is coming from, and answers itself: help comes from the Maker of heaven and earth, the Keeper who does not doze off while you sleep. That is a road-song if there ever was one. You sing that at the point in the walk where your ankles are done and the hill has stopped being scenery and become an enemy. You sing it because you need to be reminded that Someone awake is watching the climb you can barely finish.

Others in the stack sing of the sheer relief of arriving, feet standing at last inside the gates, the joy of a crowd going up together. One of them tells the plain truth that unless Yahuah builds the house, the builders wear themselves out for nothing, and that He gives sleep to the ones He loves, a mercy you feel most sharply after a hard day on foot. One sings that the people who go out weeping, carrying seed, will come home singing, carrying the harvest. One paints a picture of family unity as something like fragrant oil running down, or dew settling on the hills, the kind of image that lands when you have been walking for days *toward* your people. And the last of them, the fifteenth, is sung at night, at journey's end, in the house at last, a blessing traded back and forth in the dark between the worshipers and the ones keeping watch.

Look at the shape of the whole thing. It starts in exile and ends in the house. It starts homesick and ends blessed. It is a road, laid out in songs, from the far country all the way up to the presence of God.

Now I have to be careful and honest with you, the way I always try to be, because tradition and Scripture are not the same weight. The Bible does not hand us a footnote that says, "These were the pilgrimage marching songs, sung in this order, on the road." That connection is the understanding of the tradition, a very old and very reasonable one, and there is a lovely companion tradition that these fifteen songs matched fifteen steps in the Temple, sung by the Levites as they climbed them. I offer both as tradition, not as a claim the text makes for itself. But the songs themselves give the tradition its warrant. Read them in a row and the road is *right there*, built into their movement, from distance to arrival, from the low place to the high one.

And here is why I keep coming back to them.

Some songs you can only understand from the middle of the walk.

If you have arrived, if everything is settled, the house is reached, the ache is gone. You can read these psalms, but you cannot quite *sing* them, not the way they were made to be sung. Because they were written for the in-between. For the person who is genuinely on the way and just as genuinely not there yet. For legs that are tired and a home that is real but still over the next rise. That is a strange and specific emotional address, and most of our worship music never even tries for it. We tend to sing as though we have already arrived, or as though we never left. The ascent-songs do neither. They sing from the road.

That is where most of us actually live. Not in the far country, fully lost. Not in the house, fully home. On the climb. Worn out, believing, homesick, still moving. And the mercy of these fifteen little songs is that they were composed for exactly that person, not to pretend the hill isn't steep, but to give the tired traveler something true to put in his mouth while he keeps climbing it.

So here is the small, quiet invitation.

You do not have to wait until you have arrived to worship. You do not have to feel settled, or finished, or sure of the summit. The oldest pilgrims did not. They sang *while* their legs burned, *while* the city was still a smudge on the horizon, *while* the seed was still going out in tears with no harvest yet in sight.

Find the uphill songs. Sing the one that lifts its eyes to the hills when yours are fixed on the ground. Sing the one that promises the weepers will come home carrying sheaves. Let them do what they were built to do, not carry you to the top in one line, but keep truth in your mouth for the next hundred steps.

The house is real. The climb is real too. And there has always been a song for people who are somewhere between the two, still going up.
