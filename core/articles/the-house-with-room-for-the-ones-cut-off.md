If you wanted to convince someone that the door of worship was truly open to everyone, who would you name? God, in one of the most tender passages the prophets ever recorded, named the two people every worshiper of that era would have quietly filed under *hopeless.* He named the foreigner. And He named the eunuch.

Sit with that choice for a moment, because it is not an accident. The older Law had drawn hard lines. A man wounded in his body, unable to father children, was kept from the assembly. Certain foreign peoples were barred to the tenth generation. If you were the son of a stranger, or a man who had been emasculated in some king's court, you did not need anyone to explain the rules to you. You already knew where you were not allowed to stand. And then, through Isaiah, Yahuah walks straight up to those two exact people and speaks over them the promise they had stopped letting themselves hope for.

## "Do not say what you have already been saying"

Watch how precisely He names their fear, because He quotes the very sentence each of them had been rehearsing.

To the foreigner, the *ben hanekar*, the son of the alien, the outsider by blood. He says: do not let the one who has joined himself to Yahuah say, *Yahuah will surely separate me from His people.* He knows the word already forming in the foreigner's mouth. *Separate.* He will let me near, but never all the way. And the word Isaiah uses for how this foreigner has come, *joined* himself, is *nilvah*, from a root that means to cling, to attach, to be welded on. It is the same root that gives Israel the name *Levi*, the tribe that ministered before God. The foreigner is not loosely associated. He has clung. He has fused himself to Yahuah. And Yahuah refuses to let him call himself an outsider.

Then He turns to the eunuch, the *saris*, a word for one who has been made unable to have children, often a servant of a royal house, and again He quotes the sentence the man has been telling himself: do not let the eunuch say, *I am a dry tree.* A dead branch. A line that ends with me. No sons to carry my name, no daughters to remember me, nothing left when I am gone. It is one of the loneliest self-descriptions in all of Scripture, and God speaks it out loud before He answers it, so the man knows he has been fully seen.

## A name that cannot be cut off

Now here is the promise, and it is so exact that once you see it you will never unsee it.

To the eunuch, the man terrified of being *cut off*, cut off in his body, cut off from any future, cut off from being remembered, God says: I will give you, in My house and within My walls, *a monument and a name* better than sons and daughters. An everlasting name that shall not be **cut off**.

The Hebrew phrase there is *yad vashem*, literally *a hand and a name.* You may know those two words; they are the name Jerusalem gave to its great memorial to the lost, taken straight from this verse. God looks at the one man who was sure his name would die with him and promises him a name inside the house of God that outlasts every bloodline in Israel. The one who feared being cut off is handed a name that can never be cut off. He answers the exact wound. Not a general comfort, a promise shaped precisely to the fear.

> He does not merely open the door to the ones who assumed they were shut out. He gives the one who felt most forgotten the longest memory in the house.

And to the foreigners who join themselves to Yahuah, who love His Name and serve Him, He makes a promise just as staggering: I will bring them to My holy mountain, and make them joyful in My house of prayer. Their offerings will be accepted on My altar. And then the line that gathers it all up, the line Yeshua would one day carry into the temple courts like a torch, *for My house shall be called a house of prayer for all peoples.*

Not a house of prayer with a small side room for outsiders. A house of prayer *for all peoples.* The nations are not tolerated at the edges. The building is named after them.

## The tables He overturned to keep the door open

Now let me take you forward, because this promise has a second act that most people miss.

When Yeshua walked into the temple and drove out the sellers and money-changers and turned over their tables, we tend to read it as a burst of anger at greed. It was more than that. The market He cleared out had been set up in the outermost court, the one place, the *only* place, where a foreigner was permitted to come and pray. The commerce had colonized the court of the nations. The very people this promise was written for had arrived at the house of prayer and found it turned into a marketplace, their space to seek God crowded out by tables and coins.

And what does Yeshua shout as He clears it? He quotes Isaiah. *Is it not written, My house shall be called a house of prayer for all nations? But you have made it a den of robbers.* He was not merely defending reverence. He was defending the door. He was fighting, with His own hands, to keep the foreigner's place to pray from being stolen. The most confrontational thing He did in that temple, He did on behalf of the outsider who had come a long way to worship and could not find room.

That tells you how seriously Heaven takes this open door. It is worth turning over tables for.

## The one man who was both

And if you want to know whether the promise was kept, follow it to a desert road.

There is an official from far-off Ethiopia riding home in a chariot, reading the scroll of Isaiah, a man who was a foreigner *and* a eunuch, both of the fears in one person, the two the earlier Law had shut out, sitting in the same seat. He had gone all the way to Jerusalem to worship and was now heading home. And God does not leave that man to wonder whether the door was really open. He sends an evangelist running across the sand to catch his chariot, to open the Scriptures to him, to tell him about Yeshua, and that foreign eunuch goes down into the water and comes up rejoicing, going on his way with joy. The house of prayer for all peoples reached all the way to him. The dry tree was made green. The one cut off was grafted in for good.

## If you have already written yourself off

I carry this passage into every frontier I am sent to, because I keep meeting the foreigner and the eunuch. Not always by those old names, but I meet the ones who have already decided the song belongs to someone else. The one who says, *He will let me close but never all the way.* The one who says, *I am a dead branch; there is no future in me.* The one who assumes the door of worship has a rule with their name on it.

Hear me carefully, because Yahuah heard you first. He knows the exact sentence you have been rehearsing about why you do not belong. He quoted it before you finished it. And then, over the one who felt most cut off and most far away, He built a whole house and named it after you.

There is room. There was always room. Come in and be made joyful in the house of prayer.
