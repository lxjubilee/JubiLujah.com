In modern Israel, when someone hands you your change, or holds a door, or pours your coffee, you will hear a small warm word land in the air: *todah.* Thank you. It is one of the first words a visitor learns. Children say it a hundred times a day.

What almost no one saying it stops to notice is that it is a sacrificial term. Three thousand years ago, that same word named a specific offering carried up to the altar in Jerusalem, and of all the offerings a person could bring, it was the one that came with a song attached.

I want to tell you about the *todah*, because I think it quietly holds one of the deepest truths about worship that the Bible has to teach, and most of us have walked right past it.

## An offering you could not bring in silence

The instructions for the offerings are laid out in careful detail in the Torah. Most of them are somber business, sin dealt with, guilt covered, blood and smoke and the terrible weight of a holy God meeting an unholy people. You did not sing your way through a sin offering. You came trembling.

But among the peace offerings there was a particular kind, brought not because you had done wrong but because something had gone *right*, because you had been rescued, or healed, or brought home, or simply because your heart was full and you wanted to say so to God. The Word calls it the sacrifice of *todah*, the thanksgiving offering. And it came with bread, loaves brought alongside it, a meal to be shared, eaten in gladness the same day it was offered. A thanksgiving you could not hoard. A joy with a short shelf life, meant to be spent immediately, in company, out loud.

You brought it, in other words, the way you throw a party for a friend who has just come back from the dead. And the songbook shows us exactly what came out of a person's mouth when they brought it. The psalms are full of the language of the *todah*: a rescued person saying they will offer the sacrifice of thanksgiving and call on the name of Yahuah, that they will pay their vows in the presence of all the people. One psalm even wears the heading that it is a psalm *for the todah*, a song written to be sung while the thank-offering was being brought. The offering and the singing arrived together. You did not lay this one on the altar and step back in silence. You laid it down and you *sang.*

## Thanks with the hands thrown open

The word itself is worth turning over in your palm.

*Todah* comes from a Hebrew root, *yadah*, that reaches back to the word for a *hand.* At its heart it pictures a hand thrown out, extended, lifted. To give *todah* is to give thanks the way a body gives thanks before the mind has found any words: hands flung open, arms up, the whole self turned toward the giver. The same root also carries the sense of *confessing*, of openly acknowledging, saying out loud what is true. So buried in this one small word are three things braided together that we tend to keep in separate drawers: to give thanks, to confess the truth about God, and to lift your hands. In Hebrew they are one gesture.

That is why the *todah* had to be sung. Thanksgiving, in this way of seeing, was never a private feeling you kept behind your teeth. It was a *public declaration*, a person standing up in front of the whole congregation and saying, with their voice and their open hands, *look what God has done for me, and it is true, and I will not keep quiet about it.* You do not whisper that. You sing it, so the others can hear, and lift their own hands, and remember their own rescues.

## The offering that never ends

Now here is the tradition I most want to give you, and I will name it plainly as tradition, because it is not a claim the text makes for itself, but it is one of the most hopeful things the old teachers ever said.

They taught that in the age to come, when everything is finally made right, all the sacrifices would one day cease. The sin offerings, the guilt offerings, all of them would fall silent, because in a world set fully right there would be no more sin to cover, no more guilt to carry. There would be nothing left to atone for.

But the *todah*, they said, would never cease. When every other offering had done its work and gone quiet, the thanksgiving offering would still be rising. Because gratitude does not run out when the trouble ends. It is the one thing that only *grows* when everything is finally well. In a world with no more tears, there would be no more sin offerings, and endless, unstoppable thanksgiving.

Think about what that means for the shape of eternity, if it is true. The last thing standing at the altar, when all the rest is finished, is a *song.* The final and permanent language of a redeemed people is *thank you.* We tend to imagine heaven as very quiet and very still. The old tradition imagined it as the one offering you cannot bring without singing, going on and on with no end.

## What still rises when the Temple is gone

The Temple is gone now. The altar where the *todah* was laid down has not stood for two thousand years. And you might think that when the Temple fell, the thanksgiving offering fell with it.

But listen to how the earliest followers of Yeshua spoke, once the smoke of the old altar had cleared. They were urged to keep offering up to God a sacrifice of praise, and the Word describes it as *the fruit of lips*, the harvest that grows on a mouth that openly names Him. Do you hear it? When there was no more altar, the sacrifice of thanksgiving did not die. It came home to where it had always secretly lived: on the lips. The offering became a song, fully and finally, brought not by a priest with a golden pitcher but by any ordinary person with an open mouth and an open hand.

That is the *todah* you can still bring tonight, right where you are. You do not need a Temple. You do not need a lamb or a loaf or a priest. You need only to do the oldest thing a rescued person ever did: stand up, throw your hands open, and say out loud, in front of whoever will hear, *look what God has done, and it is true.*

## The invitation

So here is what I want to leave in your hands.

Stop treating gratitude as a mood that comes and goes and mostly stays silent. The Hebrew world treated it as an *offering*, something you bring, deliberately, and cannot bring quietly. Something with bread to share and a song to sing and hands to lift. Something so central to who God's people are that a whole nation still says the word a hundred times a day without remembering it once meant a fire on an altar.

And take the hope of it, too. If the old teachers were right, then the thank-you you sing on your hardest day is not a small thing that will pass. It is the one offering that outlasts every other, the language you are learning now for a world where there is nothing left to atone for and everything left to be grateful for, forever.

Bring the *todah.* Sing it. It is the sacrifice that was always meant to be sung, and, in the end, the only one still rising.
