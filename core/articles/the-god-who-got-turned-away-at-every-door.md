Every year of my childhood, for nine nights running, we walked the street to be turned away.

That is the *posada.* It is one of the oldest and most beloved traditions my people keep, and if you have never seen it, let me tell you what it looks like, because it is stranger and more beautiful than most people realize. A little procession forms in the evening, candles, a couple of children dressed as Mary and Joseph, the rest of us following behind, singing. And we go from house to house. And at each house we sing a song asking to be let in. *We are weary. We have traveled far. Please, is there room? Will you open the door?*

And the people inside the house sing back. And what they sing, at first, is *no.* No room here. Go somewhere else. We do not know you. Move along. So the procession moves on to the next door, and sings the same plea, and gets the same refusal. Turned away. Turned away again. Nine nights of it, walking the cold street, singing to be let in, and hearing *no* through door after door after door.

As a boy I thought it was mostly a game, a bit of theater with candy at the end, because eventually one door does open, and when it does, everyone pours inside for the feast. But I am grown now, and I have walked that procession too many times, and one year it stopped being a game. One year, singing outside a closed door in the cold, I understood what we were actually doing. We were not playing. We were rehearsing the truest and most heartbreaking thing there is to know about our God.

He knows exactly what it is to be turned away at the door. Because it happened to Him.

## There was no room, and it was written down

We have made the birth of Yeshua so pretty that we have sanded the hard fact right off of it. But the account does not hide it. It says it flat and cold: when the time came for the child to be born, they laid Him in a feeding trough, because there was no room for them. No room. The doors were full, or the doors were shut, or the doors simply did not care, and the God of all creation entered His own world through the one gap left over after everyone with a bed had been accommodated. The King of everything was born in the space nobody else wanted, because there was no room.

Think about what the posada is actually doing, then. It is not inventing a sad story for the sake of drama. It is putting our own bodies inside the true one. When we walk that cold street and sing to be let in and hear *no* through the door, we are not pretending. We are remembering. We are feeling in our own feet what it cost, that the Maker of the world came to the world and knocked, and the world, busy and full and asleep, told Him there was no room.

And it did not stop at His birth. That was only the first door. He grew up and went from town to town, and His own people would not receive Him. He came to what was His, the old letter says, and His own did not take Him in. He stood outside the hearts of the very ones He had come to save, and knocked, and was told again and again: no room. The turning-away at the inn was not a one-time accident of a crowded night. It was the shape of His whole life. He is, in a way we do not like to sit with, the God who spent His days on the wrong side of doors, singing to be let in.

That is who we are walking with, out there in the cold of the posada. Not a God who has always been safely, comfortably inside. A God who knows the exact feeling of the closed door, because He stood outside it Himself, and was turned away, and kept knocking anyway.

## He is still standing at the door

Here is the part of the posada that broke me open, once I finally saw it.

The whole thing turns on a knock and a plea from *outside.* And there is a line in the very last book of the Bible where the risen Yeshua, glorified, seated over everything, needing nothing, describes Himself, and of all the images He could have chosen, He chooses this one. He says: *look, I am standing at the door, and I am knocking. If anyone hears My voice and opens the door, I will come in and eat with him.* 

Sit with how astonishing that is. The God who was turned away at the inn did not respond by kicking the doors down. He could. He is not weak; He is the one who made the wood and the hinges and the hand that bolts the latch. He could break into any life He wanted, take any room by force. And He will not. He stands outside, as He always has, and He knocks, and He sings the plea, *is there room? will you open?*, and He waits for the door to be opened from the inside. He turns the whole story of the closed inn around and makes Himself, forever, the one on the doorstep asking to be let in. The God who was refused a room now refuses to force one.

This is why the posada always, always ends with a door that opens. Because that is the Gospel. After nine nights of *no,* one household sings back a different word, *come in, come in, there is room here, you are welcome, this is your home*, and the door swings wide and the cold street pours into the warm house and the feast begins and nobody is turned away anymore. The whole tradition exists to teach the children, in their bodies, that the turning-away is not the end of the story. The story ends with a door thrown open and a weary traveler brought in from the cold, and it turns out the weary traveler was the Lord all along.

## Open the door

So here is what I most need to say to you, whoever you are and whatever household you keep.

He is still on the doorstep. That is not a memory from a stable long ago; it is a fact about right now. The God who was turned away at every door has come to yours, and He is doing the same thing He has always done, not breaking in, not forcing the latch, but standing in the cold of your ordinary evening and knocking, and singing the same patient plea He sang outside the inn. *Is there room? Will you open the door?*

And you know how full the house gets. You know all the reasons the innkeeper gave, no room, too busy, too late, we do not know you, come back another time. I have given every one of those reasons myself. But the posada taught me, one cold night outside a closed door, that the saddest role in the whole story is the one who keeps the latch shut while the God of glory sings on the step. And the most joyful role, the one every version of the tradition builds toward, is the household that finally sings back *come in,* and swings the door wide, and lets the feast begin.

You do not have to have a fine house. The first door that opened to Him opened onto a room with animals in it. He is not inspecting your accommodations. He only wants in. So open the door. Sing back the welcome. Let the God who was turned away at every other door be received, at long last, at yours.

*¿Hay posada?* Is there room? He is asking it of you right now, in the cold outside your heart. And there is only ever one answer that ends in a feast. Open the door.
