I set the whole first half of this record in a honky-tonk, and I want to tell you why. It is not because I love a barroom. It is because a barroom is one of the last rooms left where people still tell the truth about themselves.

Nobody at the end of the bar is pretending to have it together. The mask is already off. And I had come to believe the church needed to learn how to talk the way people talk when the mask is off, honestly, out loud, about what we have actually done. So *Honky Tonk Repentance* opens on a sawdust floor with a confession, and it does not let up until the confession has grown a spine.

## Why I had to make it

Most repentance records I have heard are private. Just me and my sin and my Savior. And that is true and good as far as it goes. But I kept running into a harder kind of repentance in the Scripture, one we mostly skip, the kind a whole people does together, out loud, for what they did as a people.

Daniel prayed it. Nehemiah prayed it. And **Yahuah** framed the whole thing in Second Chronicles chapter seven: "If my people, which are called by my name, shall humble themselves, and pray, and seek my face, and turn from their wicked ways; then will I hear from heaven, and will forgive their sin, and will heal their land." Notice it is *my people*, plural. A community owning what a community broke.

So the opener is not "I broke the covenant." It is "*We* broke the covenant." The church broke it. The nation broke it. And the very first line of restoration is the willingness to say so without flinching. King David modeled the personal version of it in Psalm fifty-one, after the prophet Nathan cornered him: "against thee, thee only, have I sinned." No excuses, no spin, a man naming the exact thing he had done. This record just takes David's honesty and lets a whole people sing it together.

## What the title means

Honky-tonk repentance means repentance that happens in the room where you actually are, not the room where you would like to be seen.

The confession side of the album walks through the hard specifics, *We Stole the Land*, *We Abandoned the Poor*, *We Silenced the Prophet*, *The Church Ignored the Widow*. I named those on purpose, because a confession that stays general is a confession that costs nothing. Joel chapter two says, "rend your heart, and not your garments." It is easy to tear your shirt for show. It is another thing entirely to let the truth tear something on the inside.

> Vague repentance is just guilt with good manners. Real repentance names the thing it did, out loud, in the room where it happened.

But here is the mercy woven all through it: Isaiah chapter one has **Yahuah** say to a people drowning in exactly this kind of sin, "Come now, and let us reason together: though your sins be as scarlet, they shall be as white as snow." The honesty is not the end. It is the door. First John chapter one promises that if we confess our sins, He is faithful and just to forgive us, and to cleanse us. The barroom is dark, but the light is already reaching for it.

## The heart under the songs

The most important thing on this record is a single structural move, and I built the whole album around it.

For six tracks, it is honky-tonk, barroom upright bass, a lonesome Telecaster, the sound of a man on a stool telling the truth. Then track seven, *Restitution Begins*, the banjo rolls in and the whole thing turns to bluegrass. That is not a production gimmick. That is the theology of the album in a chord change. Because the song says the thing I most wanted to say on the entire record: "Confession alone is not enough. Repentance requires action. Restitution demands that we return what we stole, restore what we broke, honor what we silenced."

I stand that on Zacchaeus. In Luke chapter nineteen, the little tax collector meets **Yeshua** and does not merely feel bad. He says, "if I have taken any thing from any man by false accusation, I restore him fourfold." He gets out the checkbook. And **Yeshua** answers, "This day is salvation come to this house." Salvation and restitution walked in the same door. That is why the record refuses to end at "sorry."

From there it climbs. *The Church Kneels*, *The Road Back*, *Restoration Ahead*, and it lands on new creation, because that is where genuine repentance always lands. Second Corinthians chapter five: if any man be in Christ, he is a new creature; old things are passed away, all things are become new. Revelation chapter twenty-one puts the same promise in the mouth of the One on the throne, "Behold, I make all things new." The album that started on a barroom floor ends with a whole people made new, and I wanted you to feel the road between those two rooms in your own chest.

## Who I made this for

I made *Honky Tonk Repentance* for the church, honestly. For us.

For every believer who has felt the low, uncomfortable ache of knowing that our house has not been innocent, that there are peoples and poor and prophets and widows we looked past, and that saying a soft, general "we've all made mistakes" is not the same as telling the truth. This record is permission to tell it. Not to wallow in it, to walk *through* it, because the far side of honest confession is the cleanest ground there is.

And I made it for the individual on the stool, too. The one who is sure he is too far into the wreck for the church to want him. Hear the deepest note on the album: the Lamentations chapter three promise that His mercies are new every morning; great is His faithfulness. New every morning. Not new if you clean yourself up first. New at dawn, over the same tired sinner, again and again, for as long as it takes.

So go on and tell the truth in whatever barroom you are sitting in tonight. Name it. Do not soften it. And then do the thing the honky-tonk turns to bluegrass to say, get up, go back, and start returning what got broken.

Because the God who told a scarlet people they could be white as snow is still, this very morning, faithful and just to forgive. The confession is the hard part. But it was never the end of the song. It was only the first verse.
