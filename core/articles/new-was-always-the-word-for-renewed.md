There is a small command tucked into the songbook of Israel that most of us read straight past, and I want to slow down on it, because it has been quietly shaping the way heaven asks to be worshiped for three thousand years.

Sing to Him a *new* song.

Not a good song. Not the beloved one everybody already knows the words to. A **new** one. And it is not said once, as a passing suggestion. It is said again and again, across the Psalms, until you start to notice it is a pattern and not an accident. The prophets pick it up. The last book of the Bible closes with it, a song no one had ever sung before, rising in a room full of the redeemed.

Here is the thing that used to trouble me about it. We have old songs that work. We have the reliable ones, the ones that have carried us through funerals and midnight and the long grey Tuesdays of a life. Why would the God who never changes keep asking for something we have never sung? Why new, over and over, from the One who is the same yesterday and forever?

The answer is buried in the Hebrew word itself. And once you see it, you cannot unsee it.

## The word for "new" is the word for the moon coming back

The Hebrew word is *chadash*. New. Fresh. And it comes from a root, the letters *chet, dalet, shin*, that carries the sense of renewing, of making fresh again.

From that very same root comes another word you would never expect to be related: *chodesh*. It means *month*, and more precisely, the *new moon*, the sliver of light that returns to the black sky after the moon has gone completely dark.

Sit with that picture, because it is doing theology.

To the ancient Hebrew ear, *new* did not mean *never existed before*. It did not mean novelty, the shiny thing off the shelf, the trend that will be old by autumn. *New* meant what the moon does. The moon is not replaced every month. There is no second moon. It is the *same* moon, renewed, brought back around, filled again with light after the dark had swallowed it whole.

> A new song, in Hebrew, is not a stranger. It is the old faithfulness, come back around, filled with light again after the dark.

So when Yahuah asks for a *shir chadash*, a new song, He is not asking you to invent a God you have never met. He is asking you to sing the same steadfast One as He shows up *again*, freshly, in a place He has not yet been thanked for.

## You cannot sing a new song about nothing

Now the command starts to make a different kind of sense.

You cannot manufacture a new song. Try it. Sit down and decide to be freshly amazed at a rescue that has not happened yet, and all you will produce is performance, a pretty imitation of wonder with nothing underneath it. A true new song is not something you generate. It is something that *arrives*, pressed out of you by an act of God recent enough to still be warm.

The songbook says it almost exactly like that in one place. A man is sunk in a pit, mud, silence, no bottom to stand on, and he waits, and waits, and then Yahuah reaches down and lifts him out and sets his feet on rock. *And then*, the song says, He put a new song in my mouth. The new song did not come first, to talk him into hoping. It came *after*. It was the sound a real deliverance makes on the way out of a real body. He did not choose it. He could not have. It was given, because something had genuinely changed.

That is why the new one and not the old reliable one.

The old song is a monument, precious, load-bearing, true. It remembers what He did *back then*. But the new song is a witness. It testifies that He is *still doing it*. That the same steadfast love that split a sea is, this very week, splitting something in your own life wide open. A God who only earned old songs would be a God who used to be good. The relentless call for a new one is not Yahuah being restless. It is Yahuah promising you He has not finished. He keeps asking for a song you don't have yet because He keeps intending to do something you haven't seen yet.

## New every morning, and the same root every time

There is one more place that root shows up, and it undoes me every time.

A man who had watched his whole city burn, who wrote an entire scroll of nothing but grief, stopped in the exact middle of the ashes and said this: the mercies of Yahuah never run out; they are *new* every morning. Great is His faithfulness.

That word, *new* every morning, is the same root. *Chadash.* Renewed. The moon coming back.

Which means the mercy that finds you on Monday is not a fresh batch of a different substance. It is the *same* everlasting kindness, renewed to the day you are standing in, arriving with the sunrise, unspent, as if it had been saving itself for exactly this morning. His mercy renews the way the moon renews. The way a new song renews. Same faithfulness. New light.

And that is why He can honestly ask you for a new song even on your hardest day. Not because you are supposed to fake a brightness you do not feel. But because His mercy showed up *new* this morning, before you woke, and a new mercy has a right to a new song.

## So sing the one you don't know yet

Here is the door I will leave open for you, gently.

If the only songs you can find in your mouth right now are the old ones, sing them. They are holy, and they will hold. There is no shame in the reliable song; it is reliable because it is true.

But do not close your heart to the new one. Keep a corner of you listening for it. Because the new song is not a demand that you become a more creative worshiper. It is a *promise* that Yahuah is not done renewing you, that there is a rescue still coming that will need a sound you have never made, a mercy arriving tomorrow morning that the old words cannot quite hold.

The moon goes dark. And then, without fail, it comes back full of borrowed light.

So will you. And when you do, you will not have to reach for the new song.

It will already be rising in you, the same faithful God, sung *new*, because He just did it again.
