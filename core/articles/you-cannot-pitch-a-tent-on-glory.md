There is a moment on the high mountain that I cannot get past, and it is not the glory. It is what one of the men wanted to do with the glory.

Yeshua had taken three of them up a high mountain, Peter, and the brothers James and John, away from the crowds, up above the ordinary world. And there, in front of them, He changed. His face shone like the sun. His clothing went white beyond anything a person could bleach it to, white like light itself. And two of the great figures of the old covenant appeared with Him, Moses and Elijah, talking with Him. It was, by any account, the most concentrated glory these men had ever stood inside of. The veil thinned, for a moment, on a mountaintop, and they got to see.

And Peter, bless him, because Peter is all of us, opened his mouth and said it was good that they were there, and offered to build three shelters. One for Yeshua, one for Moses, one for Elijah. The account adds, almost tenderly, that he did not know what he was saying, because they were terrified.

But look at the instinct, because the instinct is the whole sermon. Peter's first response to overwhelming glory was to reach for a hammer. Let us build something. Let us make this permanent. Let us pitch tents up here and *stay*.

I understand him completely. So do you.

## The instinct to build a shelter on the summit

When the glory finally breaks through, a service where Heaven felt an inch away, a season when God was so near you could have wept just walking to your car, a retreat, a revival, a night the worship would not stop and you did not want it to, the very first thing the human heart wants to do is preserve it. Freeze it. Build a structure around it so we never have to come down. We start planning how to make the mountaintop our permanent address.

And it never works. It cannot work, and the mountain itself will teach you why.

You cannot live at altitude. Ask any climber. The summit is glorious precisely because you cannot stay there, the air is too thin, the exposure too severe, the body cannot sustain it. The high places are for meeting, not for dwelling. They give you something you can get nowhere else, and then they send you down, because the down is where your body was built to breathe and your life was built to be lived. A person who refuses to descend does not preserve the glory. They die of it.

Peter wanted to enshrine the moment. But even as he was pitching his imaginary tents, a bright cloud rolled over all of them and a voice came out of it, this is my beloved Son, listen to Him, and when the cloud lifted, Moses was gone and Elijah was gone and the shining had faded, and there was only Yeshua, ordinary again, and the plain word: get up, do not be afraid. And then they walked back down the mountain. There was no tent. There was no staying. There was a descent, and there was a road.

## What was waiting at the bottom

Here is the part that reframes the whole account for me, and it is the detail we skip.

They came down the mountain, and at the base there was a crowd, and in the crowd a father whose only son was being torn apart by a spirit, and the disciples who had stayed below had tried to help the boy and could not. Chaos. Grief. A father's desperate, wearing-thin faith. That is what was waiting at the bottom of the mountain of transfiguration. Not more glory. A suffering child and a helpless crowd and a job that needed doing.

Do you see it? The glory on the summit was *for* the valley at the bottom. The mountaintop was not a reward to be hoarded. It was a filling station for a descent into exactly the kind of human misery that Peter's three tents would have kept them safely away from. If Peter had gotten his wish, that boy stays broken and that father stays without hope, while three men admire the light on a peak. The glory would have become a place to hide from the very people it was meant to reach.

This is the thing I most want you to carry. The measure of a genuine mountaintop encounter is not how long you managed to stay up there. It is what you were willing to carry back down. Worship that only wants more worship, that resents every descent, that treats the valley as an interruption of the "real" spiritual life on the peak, that worship has misread the whole mountain. The peak exists to send you back to the people at the bottom with something in your hands you did not have before you went up.

## For the one grieving a mountaintop that ended

So let me pastor whoever is reading this from the ache of a glory that faded.

You had a season when God was so close. The worship was electric, the presence was thick, everything mattered and shimmered. And then, without your permission, it lifted. The services feel ordinary now. The nearness dimmed to a normal middle-distance. And you have spent a while grieving that, maybe even wondering what you did to lose it, trying to rebuild the shelter and get back up the mountain by force.

Hear me gently: you did not lose the glory. You were sent down with it. The fading of the shining on that mountain was not a punishment and it was not your failure. It was the same thing that happened to Peter, the cloud lifts, the light fades, and there is only Yeshua and a road going down toward people who need what you were just given. The mountaintop was real. It was also never meant to be your house.

Come down. Bring the fire with you into the valley where a father is losing hope and the ones who stayed below could not fix it. That is not a demotion from the glory. That is the glory finally doing what it climbed the mountain to do. The light on the summit was always aimed downhill, at the very faces Peter's tents would have walled out.

You cannot pitch a tent on glory. But you can carry it down the mountain, and there is someone at the bottom who has been waiting a long time for the ones who have seen the light to finally come back and find them.
