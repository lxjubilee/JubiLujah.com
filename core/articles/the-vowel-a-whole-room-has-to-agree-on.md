Here is a thing that will surprise you if you have never stood in front of a big choir trying to get it to sing one word.

The hardest thing is not getting them to sing the right *notes.* It is getting them to agree on a vowel.

Picture four hundred people, all sincere, all facing the same direction, all singing the same line of the same song. And it comes out as mud. Not because anyone is off-key, the pitches can be perfect and it still sounds like a muddy roar, a word you cannot quite make out, a smear of sound where a sentence should be. And a good director will stop the whole thing, and not fix a single note, and instead say something that sounds absurdly small: *the vowel. Everyone, the same shape. Round the back of it. Same space in the mouth. Again.*

And you do it again, and it is a different universe. The mud resolves into a word. Four hundred throats stop being four hundred private opinions about how to shape a sound and become, suddenly, *one voice saying one thing*, enormous, clear, unmistakable, the word ringing out of the whole room as though a single giant had spoken it. Nothing changed but the shape of the vowel. And everything changed, because that shape was the difference between a crowd making noise in the same room and a body speaking with one mouth.

I have thought about that small miracle for years, because it tells the truth about unity that most of our slogans get wrong.

## Unity is not everyone feeling the same

We talk about being one as if it means we all have to feel identically, same emotion, same temperament, same experience, everyone lit up in exactly the same way at exactly the same moment. And because that never actually happens, we quietly conclude that unity is a fantasy, or that it belongs only to the naturally like-minded, or that we must not really be one because we do not all *feel* one.

But the choir teaches something more precise and far more possible. The four hundred voices that finally spoke as one were not four hundred identical people. They were basses and sopranos, trained and untrained, some grieving that morning and some glad, wildly different in the timbre and story and strength of their voices. Unity did not ask them to become the same person. It asked them to agree, together, on the shape of the word they were all committed to saying. They kept every difference. They gave up only the private shapes that turned a shared word into mud. And in that one surrendered thing, *how we form the word we hold in common*, a room full of unlike people became a single voice.

That is the unity the Kingdom is actually built on, and Scripture is far more careful about it than we are.

## Speak the same thing

When Paul wrote to a church that was fracturing into camps, he did not beg them to feel the same feelings. He appealed to them to *speak the same thing*, that there be no divisions among them, that they be knit together in the same mind and the same conviction. Speak the same thing. He knew that a body is not made one by shared temperament; it is made one by a shared confession, a common word that everyone agrees to shape the same way. You can differ in a thousand things and still be one voice, if the word you all say about Yahuah is the same word, formed in the same truth, not each person privately bending it into a shape of their own.

And there is a promise older than Paul that I love even more, because it makes the whole thing sound like music. The prophet Zephaniah heard Yahuah say that a day was coming when he would give the peoples a pure language, a restored, clear speech, *so that they would all call on his name and serve him shoulder to shoulder, with one accord.* A pure language. One word, shaped the same way, out of every kind of mouth. That is the choir's miracle spoken as prophecy. The healing of Babel is not everyone forced into one culture. It is every scattered, different people finally able to shape the one name the same way, until the whole earth says it as one voice.

## The morning I heard it happen

I lead worship in two tongues, cânt în două limbi, I sing in two languages, and I have watched this land in a room where not everyone even shared a mother tongue. Different languages, different accents on the very same word, and for a while it was exactly that muddy roar, everyone sincere and no one aligned, each voice bending the sound its own way. And then something settled. Not because anyone abandoned their language. Because the room agreed, without anyone announcing it, on the *heart-shape* of the word, the same trust underneath it, the same One being named, and the accents stopped fighting and started fusing, and a genuinely mixed, unlike, could-have-been-divided room said one name with one mouth. I stood there with my throat tight, because I was hearing the thing Zephaniah promised, small and early and real: unlike people, one shaped word.

That is what our disunity almost always is, if you look closely. Not usually a disagreement about the notes. We often sing the same theology, the same Christ, the same hope. What turns it to mud is that everyone is privately shaping the shared word their own way, my emphasis, my grievance, my version of the vowel, until the thing we hold in common comes out unintelligible, a roar instead of a sentence. And the fix is almost never to argue about the notes. It is to round the vowel together. To agree, humbly, on the shape of the word we already share.

## The invitation

So if you are aching over a divided room, a family, a friendship, a congregation that keeps coming out as mud, stop first and ask the choir's question. Is it really the notes? Or is it that everyone is shaping the shared word their own private way, and no one has agreed on the vowel?

Unity does not require you to feel identically or become the same. It requires the humbler and harder thing: to give up your private shape of the common word, and form it the way the whole body forms it, so that the sentence can finally be heard. Keep your timbre. Keep your story. Keep the whole irreplaceable difference of your voice. Surrender only the shape that turns *our* word into *my* noise.

Do that, and a room full of unlike people becomes one voice saying one thing, the name of Yahuah, formed the same way out of every kind of mouth, ringing so clearly that the world outside can finally make out the word.

That is the pure language he promised us.

We can start shaping it now, one agreed-upon vowel at a time.
