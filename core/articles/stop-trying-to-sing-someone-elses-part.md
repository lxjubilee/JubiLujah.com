There was a woman in a choir I led who could not stay on her own part, and the reason she could not is one of the most quietly freeing things I know.

She kept sliding off. The moment we sang all the parts together, her voice would drift, note by note, onto the melody, the main tune, the one everyone can hum, the one the sopranos were carrying up top. And when I stopped us and asked her to sing her own line alone, back to me, I understood why. Her part, sung by itself, sounded *wrong.* It had strange leaps in it. It moved down when your ear wanted it to move up. It landed on notes that felt unfinished, restless, almost mistaken. Sung on its own, in the naked air with nothing beside it, her line sounded like a broken attempt at a song rather than a song.

So of course she kept fleeing it. Who wants to sing the part that sounds like a mistake? Given the choice between her odd, leaping, incomplete-sounding line and the lovely obvious melody floating right next to her, her whole body reached for the melody. She was not being difficult. She was being human. She was doing the thing almost every one of us does with our lives.

But here is what her part actually was, and why I would not let her abandon it.

It was counterpoint.

## The line that only makes sense against another line

Counterpoint is one of the great inventions of music, and it is not the same as a round. A round is one tune chasing itself, everyone eventually singing the identical melody a beat apart. Counterpoint is something braver and stranger: two *genuinely different* melodies, sounding at the same time, each one complete, each one going its own way, and when one climbs, the other is written to descend, so that they lean against each other and hold each other up. They were composed to fit. Not to match. To fit.

And this is the secret of the woman who kept sliding off: a counterpoint line is not supposed to sound whole by itself. Its strange leaps, the places it moves against your expectation, the notes that feel unresolved in isolation, those are not flaws. They are the exact points where it was built to meet the other melody. Play the two lines together and every one of her "wrong" notes turns out to be the most beautiful note in the chord, because it was never meant to stand alone. It was meant to stand *against.* Her odd descending leap was written to catch the melody's rise. Her unfinished-sounding landing was the very place the whole thing bloomed into harmony.

She thought she had been given a broken version of the melody. She had actually been given the one line the melody could not do without.

## What comparison does to a life

I have watched more people than I can count do to their whole lives exactly what she did to her part.

You listen to your own life the way she listened to her line, alone, in the naked air, with nothing beside it. And it sounds wrong. It has strange leaps in it. It moves down where you wanted it to move up. It lands on things that feel unresolved: the quiet calling nobody claps for, the years that went a direction you did not plan, the gift that is not the hummable, obvious, up-front gift. And then you look over at someone whose life is the melody, visible, admired, easy to follow, the one everybody can hum, and you conclude that yours is a failed attempt at theirs. So you spend enormous energy trying to slide off your own part and onto their tune. You try to make your line sound like their line. And every time you do, something collapses, because a choir where everyone abandons their part and sings the melody is not richer. It is thinner. It is one loud tune with all its harmony gone.

The trap is that you are judging your part by whether it sounds whole *alone.* But it was never written to sound whole alone. It was written to sound whole against the other lines God is running through the same room, the very people you keep comparing yourself to. Your strange leaps are where you were built to meet them. Your quiet calling is the descending line that catches someone else's rise. You are not singing a worse version of their song. You are singing the countermelody their song cannot be beautiful without.

## What is that to you?

Yeshua said this to the most comparison-prone man in the Gospels, and He said it bluntly.

It was after the resurrection, on the shore, and Yeshua had just told Peter something costly and personal about the shape Peter's own life was going to take, how he would follow, and in the end how he would die. And Peter, in the middle of receiving his own line, immediately turned around, saw John, and asked the question every one of us asks: *Lord, what about him?* What about his part? What is his going to be? Is his easier, is his better, is mine worse than his?

And Yeshua would not indulge it for a second. *If I want him to remain until I come, what is that to you?* He said. *You. You follow Me.* It is one of the great endings of comparison in all of Scripture. Not a scolding, exactly. A releasing. Your brother's line is My business and My composition; it is not yours to sing, not yours to envy, not yours to measure your own against. You have a part. Sing your part. Follow.

Paul says the same thing in the language of gifts. We have gifts, he writes, that differ, one given to serve, one to teach, one to give, one to lead, one simply to show mercy, and he never once ranks them into melody and accompaniment. Different lines. One Spirit writing all of them. The same Composer who wrote your strange, leaping, unresolved-sounding part also wrote the melody you keep envying, and He wrote them, from the first note, *to fit*, so that they would never need to coordinate, never need to sound alike, and would still, when sounded together, make a harmony neither could make alone.

## The invitation

So stop trying to sing someone else's part. You will never be good at it, and every hour you spend on it is an hour your own line goes unsung, the one line the whole piece is actually missing.

Sing your own strange melody. Yes, it sounds unfinished when you hold it up alone in the dark and compare it to the tune everyone can hum. It was supposed to. It was written for the meeting, not the mirror. *Cântă-ți partea ta*, sing your part, and trust that its oddest, most unresolved-seeming leap is the exact note God placed there to catch someone else's rise.

You are not a failed attempt at anyone. You are the countermelody. And a countermelody has never once been a lesser thing than a tune.

It is the thing that turns a tune into music.
