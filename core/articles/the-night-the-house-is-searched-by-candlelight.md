Picture a house at night with every lamp deliberately turned off.

Then one small flame is lit. And a father, with a candle in one hand and a feather in the other, moves slowly from room to room while his children follow, breathless, hunting the corners and the cupboard-cracks for something they are trying very hard to find, and very much hope is not there.

They are looking for crumbs. And this quiet, candlelit hunt, kept in Jewish homes across the world on the eve of Passover, may be one of the most piercing pictures of the human heart ever acted out by a family on their knees.

## First, the command, which is text

Before I tell you about the candle and the feather, let me set the foundation, because it matters that we know what is Scripture and what is beloved custom laid on top of it.

The command itself is plain and it is ancient. When Yahuah brought Israel out of Egypt, He bound the rescue to a week of bread with no leaven in it, and He said: for these days, no leaven, *chametz* (חמץ), is to be found in your houses. Not eaten. Not merely set aside. *Not found.* Put it away, put it out, let there be none within your walls. The Hebrew even distinguishes the *chametz*, the leavened thing, from the *se'or* (שאר), the small piece of old, fermented starter dough that a baker keeps back to raise the next batch, the hidden agent that makes the whole lump rise.

That is the text. Remove the leaven. The rest, the candle, the feather, the wooden spoon, the ten hidden crumbs, is the tradition that grew up around obeying it, and I want to hand it to you honestly labeled, because the custom is lovely precisely *as* a custom, a way faithful families have found to obey a real command with their whole bodies.

## The search, as it is kept

Here, then, is the tradition. On the night before Passover eve, after the house has already been scrubbed for days, the family performs *bedikat chametz*, "the search for leaven." The lamps are put out so that a single candle can do its slow, honest work in the dark, throwing light into exactly the corners daylight lets you ignore.

The father carries the candle. In many homes he carries a feather to sweep, and a wooden spoon to catch what the feather sweeps, and often a small cloth to wrap it all. And because a house cleaned that thoroughly might yield nothing, and because the blessing said over a search should not be said over an empty search. It became the custom for someone to hide ten small pieces of bread through the house beforehand, so that there is always leaven to find.

So the family hunts. Under the couch. Behind the books. In the pocket of a coat. The children love it; it feels like a game with a flame. And the found crumbs are swept onto the spoon, wrapped up, and set aside to be burned in the morning. What could not be found, and might yet be hidden somewhere, is verbally renounced, declared as ownerless as the dust of the earth. All of that, the ten pieces, the feather, the spoon, the words of renouncing, is the fence the tradition built around the command. Hold it as tradition. But do not miss what the tradition *sees*.

## Leaven, and the thing it always pictures

Because Scripture itself does not leave leaven as a matter of baking. Throughout the Bible, leaven becomes the picture of something small, hidden, and quietly expansive, a little of it working invisibly until the whole lump is changed. And the New Testament makes the meaning explicit: *purge out the old leaven,* Paul writes to a compromised congregation, *for a little leaven leavens the whole lump*, and he names the leaven as malice, pride, the old self we thought we had dealt with.

Now go back into that darkened house and watch what the family is really doing.

They are enacting the search of the heart.

The lamps are out because daylight lets us overlook things; the candle is lit because sin lives in the corners we do not inspect by ordinary light. The leaven is *se'or*, the old starter, the small hidden agent, the thing we keep back "just in case," the compromise we imagine is too little to matter, and it is exactly the little that ruins the whole. The hunt is slow and deliberate and it goes everywhere, because the heart cannot be swept in general; it has to be searched in particular, corner by corner, pocket by pocket.

> A little leaven is enough. That is the terror and the mercy of it, the thing too small to bother with is precisely the thing that changes the whole loaf.

## The candle we did not light ourselves

And here is where the picture opens into gospel and the whole night turns to joy.

For all its beauty, no family with a candle can truly cleanse a house. You can sweep the crumbs you find; you cannot see into your own foundations. There is always the leaven that could not be found, which is why the tradition ends not in triumph but in a spoken surrender, handing the unfindable over to be reckoned as nothing.

This is exactly the posture the gospel meets. There is a searching Light that goes where our candle cannot, the Ruach HaKodesh, who does not shame us in the corners but cleans them; and there is a Lamb, our Passover, in whom the *chametz* we could never fully hunt down has already been carried off and burned. The morning after the search, the found leaven goes into the fire. On the true Passover, so did ours.

So the *bedikat chametz* is not a night of dread. It is a night of hope acted out by candlelight, the hope that the God who commanded the leaven gone did not leave us alone with the sweeping. He came into the house Himself.

You do not have to keep this custom to be caught by what it sees. But you might, this week, do the one thing the tradition was always really about: turn down the easy light, take the honest flame, and let the searching Love of Yahuah into the corner you have been leaving in the dark.

He is not hunting there to condemn you. He is hunting there to carry it out, and to fill the swept house with a feast.
