I want to take you to the ugliest place a farmer can stand, and then I want to tell you what a prophet sang there.

Picture a man walking out at the end of a season into everything that was supposed to feed his family. The fig tree has no buds on it. The vines are bare, no grapes, no wine coming. The olive crop has failed. The fields, where the grain should be standing gold and heavy, have produced nothing. He goes to the pen and the flock is gone, and the stalls where the cattle should be lowing are empty and silent. This is not a bad month. This is ruin. This is a man looking at a year of labor and a family's survival and seeing, in every direction, nothing.

And Habakkuk, because that is who this is, opens his mouth in that field, and what comes out is a song.

We know it was a song. The way his book ends, the words are handed to the worship leader, set for stringed instruments. This is not a private sigh. It is composed. It is meant to be sung by other people, with music under it, out loud. A prophet stood in a dead orchard and wrote a piece of worship music, and he built the whole thing to lean on one small, staggering word.

*Yet.*

Though the fig tree does not blossom, and there is no fruit on the vine, and the olive fails, and the fields yield nothing, and the flock is cut off and the stalls stand empty, *yet* I will rejoice in Yahuah. I will take my joy in the God who saves me.

I have read a great many brave sentences in my life. I am not sure I have ever read a braver one than that.

## The "yet" is the whole theology

We skip past that word because it is short. Do not skip it. Everything is hiding in it.

Look at what Habakkuk does *not* do. He does not pretend the orchard is fine. He does not slap a worship song over the top of his loss and call the loss a blessing. He is a prophet; he refuses to lie about reality. He names every single empty thing, one after another, unflinching, no figs, no grapes, no oil, no grain, no flock, no cattle. He gives the disaster its full inventory. He does not shrink it to make God look bigger. He tells the truth about how bad it is.

And *then* he says *yet.*

That word is a hinge, and on one side of it is everything that is gone, and on the other side is the God who is not. Habakkuk does not resolve the tension. He does not explain the ruin or receive a reason for it. He simply refuses to let the empty field have the last word about who Yahuah is. The circumstances get a full and honest hearing, and then the prophet turns his face and worships anyway, not because the evidence supports it, but in open defiance of the evidence.

That is not denial. Denial says the field is not empty. Faith says the field is empty *and Yahuah is still good,* and it will hold both of those in the same breath without letting go of either.

## Why he had to sing it, and not just think it

Here is the thing I cannot get past, as someone who makes songs.

He could have just believed it quietly. He could have had that conviction in his heart and left it there. Instead, he set it to music and handed it to the musicians. He turned his most private, most costly act of faith into something a congregation would sing.

I think he knew what a song does that a thought cannot.

A thought you can lose by morning. But a melody buries itself in you. Sing something enough times and it starts singing *you*. It plays in the back of your mind while you wash dishes, it surfaces at two in the morning when the fear comes to be counted. Habakkuk was not writing a slogan. He was planting a *yet* deep enough in his own body, and in the bodies of everyone who would ever sing his song, that it would still be there the next time the fields failed. He was arming the future.

And here is the mercy of it: he did not write that song for the people whose barns were full. Nobody with a good harvest needs a *yet.* He wrote it for the ones standing exactly where he stood, in the ruined orchard, at the end of the empty season, so that when they got there, and they would, a song would already be waiting in their mouths. He wrote them a way to worship when there was nothing to point to.

## Worship as an act of defiance

I need to say plainly what this kind of song actually is, because we have made worship such a soft and pleasant word.

Worship in the empty field is not a warm feeling. It is an act of war. It is a heart looking straight at despair, real despair, earned despair, despair with all its receipts, and choosing to declare the goodness of Yahuah anyway. The dark's whole strategy is to convince you that your circumstances are telling the truth about your God. That the empty stall means He forgot you. That the failed harvest is His verdict on your worth.

A song of *yet* calls that a lie to its face.

It says: I have seen the evidence, all of it, and I have weighed it, and I am telling you the evidence is not the truest thing in this field. The truest thing in this field is the character of the One I am singing to, and He was faithful before the harvest and He is faithful without it. My joy was never growing on those vines. It has a deeper root than that.

That is the anthem of the not-yet. It is worship offered before the answer, on nothing but Who He is. And there is nothing the darkness can do with a person who sings like that, because pressure was supposed to be the off-switch, and they left it on.

## The honest part I will not leave out

Let me be a truthful prophet and not just an inspiring one.

I am not promising you that Habakkuk's fields filled back up by spring. The song does not say that. He does not sing *yet I will rejoice because the harvest is coming next year.* He rejoices in Yahuah Himself, full stop, with no guaranteed reversal attached. And I will not attach one for you either, because worship is not a coin you feed into Heaven to purchase next season's crop. The moment we make it a technique, we have set up the cruelest lie there is, that the one whose field stays empty simply did not worship correctly.

I refuse to say that over you. So does Yahuah.

Some of you are standing in a genuinely empty field, and it may not fill the way you are begging it to. Your *yet* is not a down payment on a specific outcome. It is something far more unshakable than that: it is a heart that has decided its joy does not live in the harvest, so the harvest cannot take its joy.

That is the one thing the empty field can never do to you.

So go out to your bare orchard today, and tell Yahuah the truth about every empty thing, name them all, He can bear it. And then, when you have finished the honest inventory, sing the smallest, fiercest word a suffering person owns.

*Yet.*

Hang your whole heart on it. It will hold.
