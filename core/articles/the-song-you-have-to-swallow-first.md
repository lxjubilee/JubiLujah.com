Before Yahuah let Ezekiel preach a single sentence, He handed him a scroll and told him to eat it.

The setting is exile, which matters. Ezekiel was a displaced man among displaced people, sitting by a foreign river in a foreign land, one more captive from a broken nation, when the heavens opened over him and he saw the glory of God, the wheels, the living creatures, the brightness he could barely find words for. And he did the only thing a person can do in front of that much glory: he fell on his face. Then a voice, and the Spirit set him back on his feet, and the sending began. But notice how it began. Not with a stage. Not with a crowd. Not even, yet, with a message he was allowed to speak. It began with a command about his own mouth, and it was the strangest command imaginable: open it, and eat what I am about to give you.

A hand reached out to him holding a scroll, and it unrolled in front of him, and Ezekiel could see it was written on both sides, front and back, filled edge to edge, and what was written there was words of lamentation, and mourning, and woe. That was the content of the thing he was told to swallow. Not a scroll of comfort. Not an easy word. Grief on the front and grief on the back, the hard truth about a people who had turned away, the weight of everything Yahuah had to say to a nation that did not want to hear it. Eat this, He said. Fill your stomach with it. And then go, and speak to the house of Israel.

So Ezekiel ate it. And here is the detail that turns the whole scene on its head, the thing I have never been able to get to the bottom of: it was in his mouth sweet as honey.

Stop there, because that sentence holds nearly everything I want to say to you about worship and the prophetic word. A scroll full of lamentation and mourning and woe, and in the mouth of the man who ate it, it tasted like honey. The content was heavy. The taste was sweet. And both were true at once, because it was His word, and His word is sweet no matter how hard the thing it carries. That is the paradox at the center of every true prophetic song. It can name the sorrow, confront the sin, tell the truth the room came in to avoid, and still, because it came from Him, be sweeter on the tongue than anything the world calls comfort. The sweetness is not the absence of the weight. The sweetness is the mercy hidden inside the weight, the taste of a God who cared enough to speak at all.

But do not rush past the first command, because it is the one we most need. He did not tell Ezekiel to announce the scroll. He told him to eat it. Before one word went out from the prophet's mouth to the people, the whole of the message had to go into him, down past his teeth, into his stomach, made part of his own body. And that order is not incidental. It is the entire method. You cannot deliver a word you have only read. You can only deliver a word you have swallowed. The prophetic song is never first a performance aimed outward at a crowd; it is first a meal taken inward, digested, allowed to become you before it is ever allowed to leave you.

We have gotten this exactly backwards, and I feel it in myself more than I like to admit. We treat the songs and the truths of Yahuah as material to be handled, content to arrange, present, get across the platform to the people out there. We learn the words without ever eating them. We can sing a line about surrender we have never swallowed, declare a truth we have not digested, hand a congregation a word that has not yet cost us a single meal of our own. And it always sounds hollow, because it is. A word that has only passed across your eyes and out your mouth, without ever going down into your stomach, has no weight when it lands, because it never got any weight in you. The people can tell. They can always tell the difference between a singer who is performing a scroll and one who has eaten it.

And here is the hard grace of what he was eating. The scroll was full of lamentation and mourning and woe, which means the word Ezekiel had to make part of himself was not a comfortable one, and the people he was sent to were not a receptive audience. Yahuah told him plainly: they are a rebellious house, hard of forehead and stubborn of heart, and whether they listen or refuse to listen, you are to speak. He was not sent to a crowd that would cheer. He was sent to briars and thorns, to people who might reject every syllable, and he was told to go anyway, with the sweet-and-heavy word already inside him, and to be the sound Yahuah wanted heard whether it was welcomed or not. That is the sending. That is what it costs to carry a true song. It is not the promise of applause. It is the willingness to swallow a hard word, be changed by it, and then speak it to people who may want none of it, because faithfulness to the One who gave the scroll matters more than the reception of the ones who hear it.

And it does cost. When Ezekiel finished eating and was carried back to the exiles by the river, he sat among them, overwhelmed, for seven days before he could say anything at all. The word had gone in, and it had done something to him first, and he needed a week of silence to recover from having swallowed it before he was ready to speak. That is not weakness. That is what a real word does to the one who eats it. It works on the messenger before it ever works on the message's hearers. The song reorders the singer first.

So let me hand this to you as plainly as I can, whether you are a worshipper, a leader, or someone God has quietly been handing a scroll you have been afraid to eat. Do not try to sing what you have not swallowed. Do not carry a word to others that you have not first made part of yourself, sat with, wept over, let it name the thing in you before you dare let it name the thing in them. Eat the scroll first. Yes, it may be heavy; the true ones usually are. But you have His own promise about the taste. To the one who actually takes it in, even a word full of lamentation turns to honey in the mouth, because it is His, and there is nothing He gives His people, however weighty, that is not finally sweet.

And then, only then, with the word all the way down inside you, go and be the sound He is sending. Not to be applauded. Not to be received. Simply to be faithful to the scroll you swallowed, and to trust that a song eaten before it is sung will land with a weight no performance ever could, in a world that is starving, whether it knows it or not, for a word someone actually ate.
