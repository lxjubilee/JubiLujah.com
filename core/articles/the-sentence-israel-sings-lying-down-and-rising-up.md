Ask a person who grew up in the Jewish world what the first words they ever learned were, and very often you will hear the same short line. Not a nursery rhyme. Not their own name. A sentence about God, taught to them before they could tie a sandal, sung over them at bedtime, planted so early they cannot remember a time before they knew it.

In Hebrew it opens with the word *Shema*, "Hear." *Hear, O Israel: Yahuah is our God, Yahuah is one.*

That single line, and the words that follow it, may be the most repeated sentence in the history of the human race. And when someone asked Yeshua which command was the greatest of them all, He did not reach for something new. He reached for this. He began His answer by quoting it, *Hear, O Israel*, and then the command that comes right after it: to love Yahuah your God with all your heart, and all your soul, and all your strength. The greatest commandment, from the lips of the Messiah, was a line His people had already been singing for a thousand years.

I want to sit with what that line was *made* to do, because it tells you something about worship that most of us have never been taught.

When these words were first given, in the book of Deuteronomy, they came with instructions attached, and the instructions are almost startlingly physical. Keep these words on your heart. Teach them to your children. Talk about them when you sit in your house and when you walk along the road, when you lie down and when you get up. Bind them as a sign on your hand. Set them between your eyes. Write them on the doorposts of your house and on your gates.

Read that again and notice: not one of those instructions is "think about this occasionally." Every single one is about *placement*. Where the words go. On the children. On the road. On the hand. On the doorframe. On the mouth, first thing in the morning and last thing at night. The command was never merely to *believe* the line. It was to *locate* it, to stitch it into the times and the surfaces and the rhythms of an ordinary life until you could not get through a normal day without running into it.

And so a people built exactly that. To this day, faithful Jews say these words in the morning and again in the evening, *when you lie down and when you rise up*, straight out of the text. Small cases holding the words are bound on the arm and the head at morning prayer; a small case is fixed to the doorpost, touched on the way in and the way out. The line meant to be first on the lips of the day becomes the last thing said at its close. The sentence is not visited on a holy day. It is *lived in*, every twelve hours, at the hinges of waking and sleeping, for a whole lifetime.

There is a further tradition, and I will name it carefully as tradition and not as Scripture, because it is one of the most moving things I know. By long custom, these are the words a person tries to make the very last words of their life, the confession on the lips at the moment of death. *Hear, O Israel: Yahuah is our God, Yahuah is one.* The first sentence a child is taught, aimed to be the last sentence a life will speak. Learned before memory, carried through every ordinary morning and night, and offered up at the end. A single line meant to bracket an entire human existence from its first words to its last.

Now here is where I, as a teacher of worship, cannot stay quiet.

We have a habit, in much of the church, of treating repetition as the enemy of sincerity. Say something too often, we fear, and it goes dead in your mouth, becomes a rote, mumbled, meaningless thing. And there is a real danger there; the Scriptures themselves warn against honoring God with lips while the heart is far away. Repetition *can* rot into routine. That is true.

But the Shema stands as a three-thousand-year witness to the opposite truth, and it is a truth we have badly forgotten: some things you cannot know until you have said them ten thousand times.

You do not deeply believe that Yahuah is one by encountering the idea once, being impressed, and moving on. You come to *know* it, the way you know your own breathing, by placing it on your lips every morning and every night for decades, until it is no longer information you have learned but ground you are standing on. The daily saying is not the enemy of the meaning. Over a lifetime, the daily saying is *how the meaning gets in.* A truth said once is a fact. A truth sung twice a day for seventy years becomes the shape of your soul.

This is exactly why worship songs matter more than we admit, and why the choruses we sing over and over are not the shallow cousins of the sermon. A song you sing a thousand times is not being wasted by the repetition. It is being *planted*. It is doing to you slowly what the Shema was designed to do, locating a truth in the rhythms of your ordinary days until it is no longer something you occasionally consider but something you cannot get through a Tuesday without.

The Hebrew mind understood that a human being is formed less by the grand rare moment than by the thing done again and again at the hinges of the day. What you say lying down and rising up is what you eventually become. The single line, sung morning and night, is quietly building the person who sings it.

So let me leave you with the invitation buried in all of this.

Choose your line. Not carelessly, choose it the way a people chose the greatest sentence they knew and then wove it into everything. Find the truth about God you most need to become ground under your feet, and stop treating it as something to encounter once and admire. Say it when you lie down and when you rise up. Put it on the doorpost of your day. Sing it until it is boring, and then keep singing it until, on the far side of boring, it turns into bedrock.

The line Yeshua called the greatest was a line His people had already worn smooth with three thousand years of daily saying. He did not think the repetition had emptied it. He knew the repetition was how it got all the way in.

What sentence do you want to be true of you when you are too weak to think, the words already worn into the deepest part of you, waiting, ready to be the last thing you say? Start singing it now, lying down and rising up. A lifetime is not too long to spend learning one true line by heart.
