There is one scroll in your Bible where the name of God is never written.

Not shortened, not hinted, not tucked into a corner, simply absent, from the opening word to the last. No booming voice from heaven. No prophet standing up to say *thus says the Lord.* No parted sea, no fire on an altar, no angel with a sword. Just a story about exiles in a far-off empire, a beauty contest, a paranoid official, a sleepless king, and a series of coincidences so precisely timed that by the end you cannot breathe. The book is *Esther.* And out of this one book, this book where God is never named, an entire feast was born.

The Hebrews call it *Purim* (פורים). And I have come to love it more than almost any feast on the calendar, because it is the feast for everyone who has ever wondered whether God was still in the room when they could not see His hand at all.

## The feast named after a gambler's dice

The name itself tells the story, and it is right there in the text, so I can hand it to you without inventing a thing.

In the book, a powerful man named Haman sets out to destroy every Jew in the Persian empire, the whole scattered nation, far from home, living at the mercy of a foreign throne. And to choose the day for the slaughter, he casts lots. The word for that lot is *pur*, a Persian word, a foreign word, the very dice of the empire that wanted them dead. He rolls the *pur* to pick the date the Jews will die.

And when the whole thing is turned upside down, when the gallows Haman built for his enemy becomes his own, when the scattered people are not destroyed but delivered, the feast that remembers it is named, of all things, after his dice. *Purim.* The *lots.* As if to say: the very instrument the enemy used to seal our doom is the name we now shout at our party. The empire rolled its dice against a far-off people, and the dice came up rescue.

There is a defiant joy in that name that I cannot get over. The feast is not called *the deliverance* or *the victory.* It is called *the lots*, a permanent, laughing reminder that what looked like blind chance in the hands of a tyrant was something else entirely.

## The hidden hand

Here is why the missing name is not a flaw in the book but the whole point of it.

In every other great rescue in Scripture, you can see the hand of God move. The sea splits. The walls fall. The fire comes down. But the exiles in Persia did not get a visible miracle. They got a *chain of coincidences.* The right woman happens to become queen. The king happens to have a sleepless night. He happens to call for the royal records. The records happen to fall open to the one page that mattered. The timing happens to line up so perfectly that the villain walks into his own trap at the exact moment he means to spring it.

Read it as luck, and it is the luckiest story ever told. But nobody who reads it carefully believes it is luck. The absence of God's name is not His absence from the story. It is a summons to *look harder*, to see that when there is no pillar of fire out front, there is a hand underneath, moving every piece into place without ever showing its face. The book leaves His name out so that you will find Him in the pattern. And once you have seen it, you cannot un-see it: this is what God's care looks like when it works in the dark.

> The name of Yahuah is never spoken in the whole scroll, and His fingerprints are on every single page. That is not the story of His absence. It is the story of how He rescues the ones who cannot see Him work.

There is one line the whole feast hangs on, and I will paraphrase it rather than risk bending the words: a relative says to Esther, in her moment of terror, that perhaps she has come into the kingdom *for such a time as this*, that maybe the whole strange arrangement of her life, the exile and the palace and the crown she never asked for, was quietly aimed at this one hour when a scattered people needed someone on the inside. Neither of them can see the hand. But both of them bet their lives that it is there.

## From mourning to a good day

And then comes the turn that gives the feast its flavor forever.

The book says the month that was meant for the people's grief was turned for them from sorrow into gladness, from mourning into a good day, and so they were told to keep it every year as days of feasting and joy, of sending portions of food to one another and gifts to the poor. Sit with that. The command at the heart of Purim is not merely *remember.* It is *feast, and make sure the outsider and the destitute are feasting too.* The rescued people are told to turn around and fill the hands of the ones who have nothing. A grief that was flipped into gladness overflows, by command, to the far-off and the poor.

I have to say honestly where this feast comes from: Purim is not one of the appointed times commanded at Sinai. It was established later, by a scattered people in exile, inside the book of Esther itself, to mark a rescue that happened far from the temple, far from Jerusalem, far from every familiar sign of God's presence. It is, from its very origin, the diaspora's feast, the feast of the ones who were too far away to see the fire fall, and who learned to trust the hidden hand instead.

## For the ones who cannot see His hand

Which is exactly why I want to put it into the hands of anyone reading this from a far country, a hard place, a season where God seems to have gone silent.

You may be living in a stretch of your life with no visible miracle in it, no voice, no sign, no sea splitting in front of you, just a run of ordinary days and unexplained turns and a quiet ache to know whether He is even there. Purim was made for you. It is the feast that celebrates a rescue no one saw coming, worked by a God who never once stepped into the frame, on behalf of a people who felt utterly forgotten at the edge of an empire.

The greatest hidden hand of all finally showed its face in Yeshua, the God who had been working underneath the whole story stepped into it in the flesh. But even now He often works the Esther way: unseen, patient, arranging the sleepless nights and the strange timings of your life *for such a time as this.* The absence of the name was never the absence of the God. He is closer to your dark season than the fire ever was to theirs.

So keep the feast in your heart, wherever you are. Send a portion to someone poorer than you. And trust the hand you cannot see, because the whole book was written to tell the far-off exile one thing: mourning does not get the last word. The last word is a feast.
