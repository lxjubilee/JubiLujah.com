My grandmother could not have told you what the word *theology* meant. She left school young to work. She read slowly, and mostly she read one book, and she read it out loud with her finger under the words. If you had put a shelf of doctrine in front of her she would have dusted it and made you lunch.

But she hummed.

She hummed at the stove in the mornings, low, with her back to the room, stirring something that had been simmering since before I woke. She hummed on the bus. She hummed folding the laundry and hummed walking to the market and hummed at the sink at night when she thought the house was asleep. And here is the thing I did not understand until I was grown and had studied the books she never opened: every one of those hums was a sermon. She was preaching the whole time. She just did it in a key instead of a paragraph.

I want to tell you about the theology my grandmother hummed, because I have come to believe that some of the deepest truth any of us will ever hold got into us exactly this way, not through the front door of the mind, but through a side door somebody left open with a song, when we were too small to guard it.

## What a song does to a child who is not listening

I was not trying to learn anything. That is the first thing to understand. I was a boy underfoot in a small kitchen, waiting for food, thinking about the street. I was not listening to my grandmother's humming the way you listen to a teacher. I would have told you I did not hear it at all.

But it went in anyway. It went in the way rain goes into ground you did not till. Years later, grown, far from home, in a season so dark I could not have told you my own name, a melody surfaced in me from nowhere, unbidden, and I found my mouth moving to it before my mind had agreed to anything, and it was hers. The exact tune she hummed at the stove. And riding underneath that tune, carried on it like a boat on a river, was a truth about God I had apparently believed my whole life without ever deciding to: that He is good, that He does not leave, that the morning comes. I had not learned it. I had absorbed it. She had pressed it into me with a melody when I was too young to argue, and it had waited, folded up small, for the exact day I would need it.

The Scriptures actually command this, though we have mostly forgotten it is a command. Fix these words in your heart, they say, teach them to your children, talk of them when you sit at home and when you walk by the road, when you lie down and when you get up. Sit and walk and lie down and rise. That is not the schedule of a classroom. That is the schedule of a kitchen. That is a grandmother at a stove. God's chosen delivery system for the deepest things was never mainly the lecture. It was the ordinary day, sung over and over until the truth became part of the furniture of a child's soul.

## Honest about what was hers and what was His

Now I want to be careful here, because I love my grandmother and I will not lie for her, and love that lies is not love.

Not everything she hummed was Scripture. Some of it was her people's tradition, the old songs handed down, the rhythms of a faith carried across an ocean and worn smooth by generations of hands. Some of it, if I am honest, I could not find a chapter and verse for, and I have looked. There was folk faith mixed in with the true faith, the way there is in every family that has loved God for a long time in a hard place. I am not going to pretend the humming was flawless. It was human.

But here is what I have learned to say plainly. The *core* of it was true, truer than a lot of the polished things I was taught later by people who could cite every reference. Underneath the folk melodies and the borrowed customs, the thing she was actually pressing into me was the real Gospel: that God came down, that Yeshua carried what I could not, that grace is real and free and mine, that death does not get the last word. That part was not tradition. That part is the solid rock the whole thing stands on. And she had it. She had it in her bones, in her humming, in the way she cooked and forgave and kept getting up in the dark to pray.

So I do the work she could not. I take what she gave me and I hold it up against the Book she read out loud with her finger under the words, and I keep what is gold and I let the rest go with gratitude. That is not a betrayal of her. That is the most faithful thing a grandson can do, to love the inheritance enough to sort it honestly, to carry forward what was truly the Lord's and lay down gently what was only ours.

## You are humming something right now

Here is what I most need you to hear, and it is not really about my grandmother at all. It is about you.

You are humming something over the people around you right now. Whether you sing or not. Whether you have children or not. There is a tune your life makes, a repeated melody of what you fear and what you trust and what you believe about God when you think no one is listening, and the small ones underfoot are absorbing it exactly the way I absorbed hers. Not through your lectures. Through your kitchen. Through the thing you hum at the sink at night, the mood you carry to breakfast, the truth or the lie about God that rides underneath your ordinary day.

That should frighten you a little, and it should also set you free. It should frighten you because they are learning your real theology, not your stated one, the God you actually trust, not the one you can define. But it should set you free because you do not have to be a scholar to hand down the deepest thing there is. My grandmother proved that. She could not have passed a doctrine exam. She passed on the faith anyway, whole and load-bearing, by humming the goodness of God over a boy who was not even listening, until the day the darkness came for him and the song was already there.

So hum the true thing. Learn it well enough, from the Book she read with her finger under the words, that what you press into the ones you love is gold and not just custom. And then trust the side door. Trust the melody to go in where the lecture never could, and to wait, folded up small, for the exact morning it will be needed.

My grandmother is with the Lord now. But I still catch myself humming at the stove. And when I do, I know exactly what I am doing, and exactly who taught me, and exactly Who was in the song the whole time.
