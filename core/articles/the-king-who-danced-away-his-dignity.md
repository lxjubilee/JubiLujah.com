Picture a king in the middle of the street with his dignity coming apart at the seams, and loving every second of it.

That is not how we are trained to imagine royalty. A king is supposed to be composed. Measured. He waves from a distance. He lets the crowd come undone while he stays perfectly still, because his stillness is the whole point of him. And then you open the old account of the day they carried the ark of Yahuah up into the city, and there is the greatest king Israel ever had, whirling and leaping in front of a whole nation with everything he has, and you realize this man has forgotten he is supposed to be watched.

He was not performing. He was overflowing. And he had a crown to lose.

## He danced with everything in him

The account says David danced before Yahuah *with all his might*. Sit on that phrase for a moment, because we skate right over it. Not a little swaying. Not a dignified nod of the head from the royal seat. The word pictures a man whirling, spinning, a body thrown into motion so completely that there is nothing held back in reserve. Elsewhere the same scene calls it leaping, feet off the ground, gravity temporarily overruled.

And here is the detail everyone rushes past. When David danced, the text is careful to tell us what he was wearing: a simple linen *ephod*. A plain priestly garment. Not the royal robes. Not the crown and the color and the weight of office that told everyone at fifty paces *this is the king, keep your distance.* He had taken all of that off. In front of the whole house of Israel, the most powerful man in the land stripped down to the plainest thing a worshiper could wear and made himself, for one glorious afternoon, indistinguishable from the least person in the crowd.

That is not an accident. That is a decision. A king knows exactly what his robes are worth. They are the visible margin between him and everyone else. And David looked at that margin, the very thing that made him special, and set it down in the dust so he could dance closer to the ground, closer to the people, closer to the ark where the Presence rode. He did not protect his dignity. He spent it. He poured it out like the drink offering it was.

> The crown is not the cost of worship. The crown is what worship is generous enough to forget it is wearing.

## The window and the woman behind it

Now look up. There is a window in this story, and there is a face in the window, and her name is Michal.

She was Saul's daughter. She had once loved David. And on the day of the greatest joy the city had ever seen, she is not in the street. She is inside, above it all, watching through glass. The account says that when she saw the king leaping and dancing before Yahuah, she *despised him in her heart.* That is a strong word and the text does not soften it. She looked down at the celebration, literally down, from a higher window, and something in her curdled.

When David comes home, still glowing, still half-out-of-breath, she meets him with a comment sharpened to a fine point. How the king of Israel honored himself today, uncovering himself in front of the servant girls like some vulgar man with no shame. You can hear it, can't you. The exact tone. The cold politeness of contempt. She did not shout. Contempt never needs to shout. She simply informed him that he had embarrassed the office.

And David, oh, I love him for this, does not apologize. He does not shrink. He says, in effect: *You think that was undignified? You have not seen anything yet. I will be even more undignified than this. I will humble myself even lower in my own eyes. And those servant girls you sneer at, by them I will be held in honor.* He looks at the price she is trying to charge him for his joy, and he says he intends to pay double.

There is a cost to that scene, and the text names it plainly. Michal, to the end of her days, had no child. The woman who despised the dance stayed barren. I am not going to draw a hard line of cause and effect where Scripture only sets two facts side by side, but Scripture *does* set them side by side, on purpose, in the same breath. The one at the window, arms folded, keeping her composure, keeping her distance, keeping her dignity fully intact. She is the one the story leaves empty. And the one in the street who lost his dignity entirely is the one whose house Yahuah swore would never lack a throne.

## Which one is really watching from the window

I want to be tender here, because I think most of us have stood at that window more often than we have danced in that street.

The window feels safer. Up there you can appreciate worship without being undone by it. You can admire the ones down below, note their sincerity, maybe even envy them a little, all while keeping your careful reputation buttoned to the collar. Nobody at the window ever looks foolish. That is the whole appeal of the window. It is a place to keep your dignity clean and dry while the celebration happens to other people.

But hear me, mi gente, the window is also where the joy cannot reach you. Michal was closer to David than anyone in that crowd. She was his wife, inside his own house. And she was farther from his joy than the last child dancing at the back of the procession, because she had decided that her composure was worth more than the celebration. She kept her dignity. It cost her everything the dignity was supposed to protect.

The gospel does not leave this on David. The King who came after him, the greater Son of David, Yeshua, did the very same thing on a scale that swallows the story whole. He had a crown that made David's look like tin, and he laid it down. He wrapped himself not in a linen ephod but in our whole flesh, made himself indistinguishable from the least of us, and poured out not his dignity but his life in the dust of a hill outside the city. The whole faith is founded on a King who would not stay at the window.

So come down. I am asking you gently and I am asking you with a grin. Come down out of the safe high room where you watch other people worship and keep score of how they look. There is a street full of the ridiculous and the radiant, and Yahuah is riding right through the middle of it, and nobody down there is thinking about you at all. They are far too undone by Him to notice whether your dancing is any good.

Take the crown off. It was never the treasure. Throw it in the dust and dance closer to the Presence, closer to the people, closer to the ground where the King you follow already laid Himself down. The window keeps you tidy. The street keeps you His. And on the day the whole family is finally gathered and the music will not stop, I promise you this: not one soul is going to wish they had spent their life being more composed.
