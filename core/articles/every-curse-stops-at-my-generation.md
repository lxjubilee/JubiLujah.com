We all came from somewhere, and that somewhere came from somewhere before. There are rooms in me I did not decorate and voices in me I never hired. *Family Blessing* is the album I wrote about that inheritance, the part of the family tree you carry whether you chose it or not, and about the God who is older than the bloodline and speaks louder than it.

I did not write it as a nostalgia record. I wrote it as a chain-breaker. Because somewhere along the line, one person has to decide that what got handed down does not get handed down any further.

## The bloodline speaks, but He speaks louder

The album opens with my grandfather's voice, unpolished, over a held chord: *Child, before you hear a word I sing, hear me tell you this, Jesus wanted you. The Father wanted you before you were born.* Then the song "Bloodline" does the honest work of naming what we carry, and it stands on a promise from the very last page of the Old Testament: *he will turn the hearts of fathers to their children, and the hearts of children to their fathers* (Malachi 4:6). And it stands on Paul's word that **Yeshua** was *born under the law to redeem those under the law, so that we might receive adoption as sons* (Galatians 4:4–5), and that *he redeemed us from the curse* by becoming it for us (Galatians 3:13). The bloodline gets the last word it pretends to have only until the Father speaks.

Then the record gets specific and uncomfortable, the way it has to. "The Tone I Swore I Wouldn't Use" is a man hearing the old sharpness leak out of his own mouth at the kitchen mirror at 6 a.m., the sigh he inherited, the door-slam he memorized, the words his father never had for him that he almost did not have for his son. This is where cycles actually break or don't: at the sink, in a single sentence, with the Spirit moving your tongue back. *Let no corrupting talk come out of your mouths, but only such as is good for building up* (Ephesians 4:29). I asked **Yahuah** to retune the instrument of my mouth, and He did, not by shaming the broken thing, but by teaching it a new sound.

> The bloodline speaks, but You speak louder. Father, You turn the hearts of fathers to the children.

## The empty chair, and the Father who takes it

Some of us grew up staring at an empty chair at the head of the table. "Empty Chair" is that song, and it rests on the tenderest name for God in the Psalms: *a father to the fatherless, a defender of widows, is God in his holy dwelling, God sets the lonely in families* (Psalm 68:5–6). The Father did not arrive late to that table. He sat down in the empty chair and then pulled mine right up next to His, because *every family in heaven and on earth is named* from Him (Ephesians 3:14–15). If the chair at the head of your childhood table was empty, hear this carefully: it is not empty now.

And the blessing has to be handed forward on purpose, in small repeated holy things. "Pass It Down" is a verse taped to a fridge in 1989, the card yellow now, the tape brown, the verse the same, the inheritance that outran three moves and a will. That is the actual mechanism God gave us: *these words shall be on your heart; you shall teach them diligently to your children, and write them on the doorposts of your house* (Deuteronomy 6:6–9), the faith that *dwelt first in your grandmother Lois and your mother Eunice* (2 Timothy 1:5).

## The blessing breaks, and the whole house breathes

The turn of the album, and the song I would hand anyone who is the first believer in their line, is "The Blessing Breaks." It is a man calling his sister at 9 p.m. and saying the words out loud: *Beth, every curse on this family stops at my generation.* And the phone goes quiet a long time, and then she says, *I receive that. I receive that. I receive that.* The next morning she calls back, her son slept through the night. That is not superstition; that is what happens when one mouth in a family finally breaks a silence in the name of the One who *redeemed us from the curse* (Galatians 3:13). The curse hits the bedrock of the Son and it shatters. The blessing breaks because He broke first.

And once it breaks, it multiplies. "The Table Is Long" is the blessing become a climate, a table that keeps growing because the Father keeps adding chairs, the leaf pulled out for the cousin who left, the neighbor who never darkened a church door, the man who used to be an enemy. *Compel them to come in, that my house may be filled* (Luke 14:23). "Prodigals Welcome" keeps the porch light on for the one who left at nineteen, because the father in the parable *saw him while he was still a long way off* and ran (Luke 15:20).

## Who this album is for

It is for the one who swore they would parent differently and heard the old voice come out anyway. For the first believer in a hard line, wondering if the past gets a permanent vote. For the person who inherited a silence and is deciding, right now, whether to pass it on or break it.

The album ends where it began, with my grandfather's voice, and then the blessing handed to the next mouth: *the Lord bless you and keep you; the Lord make his face shine upon you* (Numbers 6:24–26). *One generation shall commend your works to another* (Psalm 145:4). So decide it today. As for me and my house, we will serve the Lord (Joshua 24:15). The curse stops here. The blessing runs forward. And the God who is older than your bloodline has already carved a place at the long table for the children who come after you.
