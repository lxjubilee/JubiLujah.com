There was a night my voice simply quit on me in the middle of a song.

We had been going for hours. It was one of those gatherings in the courtyard behind my tío's house, the kind that starts with a few people and a guitar and somehow, by midnight, has the whole block leaning over the wall. I was leading, calling out the lines the way I had learned to, the way my people have always done it, and somewhere in the third hour my throat closed up on me. Nothing came out. I opened my mouth to sing the line and there was only air.

And here is what I will never forget. The song did not stop.

Because I had just sung the call, the room already had the answer ready in their mouths, and they gave it back to me full and loud without me, a hundred voices carrying the line I could not carry, holding it up in the air where I had dropped it, waiting, patient, for me to find my voice again and throw them the next one. I stood there with my hand on my chest, undone, listening to a song I was supposedly leading go on perfectly well without me. And I understood something in my body that night that I had somehow never understood in my head, though I had done it my whole life.

Nobody in the barrio was ever supposed to sing the whole song alone.

## The call was always built to be answered

The way I was raised to sing is called *llamada y respuesta*. Call and response. One voice throws out a line, and the crowd throws it back. The leader sings the verse nobody knows yet, and the people answer with the *coro*, the chorus, the part everyone owns, the line so worn-in and familiar that a child half-asleep on a shoulder can still murmur it.

For years I thought this was just a style. A flavor. Something my people did because it was lively and it kept the tired ones awake. I did not realize it was theology until I started reading the old songs honestly and found that God's own songbook was built exactly the same way.

There is a psalm, the long one that walks all the way back through the whole story of everything, and it is impossible to read it right and miss what it is doing. It names the making of the sun and the moon. It names the sea splitting open and a people walking through on dry ground. It names the bread in the wilderness and the enemies overthrown. And after every single line, without fail, the same answer comes back: *for His mercy endures forever. For His mercy endures forever.* Over and over, more than twenty times, like a tide coming in.

That is not a poem meant to be read by one man alone in a quiet room. You cannot read it that way without it feeling strange, like clapping with one hand. It was written for a leader and a crowd. Somebody up front sang the deed of God. He made the heavens, He brought us out, He remembered us when we were low, and the whole assembly of the people roared the answer back: *for His mercy endures forever.* The verse changes every time. The answer never does. The leader carries the story. The people carry the faithfulness. And neither one of them is singing the whole song alone.

That is call and response. It is the oldest way the people of God have ever worshiped, and my barrio did not invent it. We inherited it, the same way we inherited the procession and the drum. We just never let anyone talk us out of it.

## Why the answer has to come from the crowd

I want to tell you why this matters, because I think a lot of good people have been quietly taught to worship as soloists, and it is wearing them out.

There is a moment recorded from the day they finished the great house of God, when the singers and the players had been going and going, and the account says something I find almost unbearable in its beauty: they were *as one.* One sound. The trumpeters and the singers made themselves a single voice to praise the Lord, and it was in that exact moment, when the many became one sound, that the glory came down and filled the house so thick the priests could not even stand to do their work. It was not the skill that brought the glory. It was the *oneness.* It was the many voices agreeing to become one song.

You cannot do that alone. That is the whole point. There is a kind of worship that only happens when you stop being a soloist and let yourself become one voice in a crowd of voices, when you sing the line you know and trust the person beside you to sing the line you have forgotten. The barrio understood this instinctively. When my voice gave out, nobody panicked, because nobody had ever believed the song depended on me in the first place. The song belonged to the room. I was just one throat in it.

And that is the thing I most want you to hear, because I think you may be tired in a way you have not named. If you have been carrying your faith like a solo, like the whole melody rests on you, like you have to hit every note and never lose your voice and never drop the line, then hear me: that was never the arrangement. You were written into a *coro.* You were meant to sing your part and let the room carry what you cannot. When your voice gives out, and it will, everyone's does, the people around you are supposed to already have the answer in their mouths, holding it up in the air where you dropped it, waiting for you to come back in.

## Throw the line, and trust the room

So here is what I would say to you, wherever you sing.

Stop trying to be the whole song. It is not humility to insist on carrying every part yourself; it is exhaustion dressed up as strength, and it was never asked of you. The God who wrote His own songbook for a leader and a crowd did not design you to worship as one lonely voice straining to hold the whole melody in the air. He designed a call and a response. He designed a people who finish each other's lines.

There will be seasons you are the caller, when your voice is strong and the story is fresh in your mouth and you get to throw the great deeds of God out over the crowd. Do it. Sing the verse nobody knows yet. And there will be seasons you are only the *coro*, when you are too tired or too broken to carry anything new, and all you can do is stand in the crowd and answer, *for His mercy endures forever,* over a story someone else is strong enough to sing. Do that too. It is not a lesser worship. It is the answer the whole song was waiting for.

And on the night your voice quits altogether, the night nothing comes out but air, do not be afraid. Look around. The room already has your line. They have been holding it for you the whole time. *La respuesta ya viene.* The answer is already coming. It is coming from the crowd God set around you on purpose, so that no one in the barrio, and no one in His whole wide family, would ever have to sing the whole song alone.
