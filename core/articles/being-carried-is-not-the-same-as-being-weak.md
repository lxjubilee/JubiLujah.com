For most of my life I believed that needing help was a flaw I was supposed to hide.

I had a plan. I had a list. I had it all under control, which is the sentence people say right before it stops being true. I thought asking was a weakness and needing was a defect in the design, so I kept both of them out of sight and paid the price in private. And then a Tuesday hit me sideways and put my knees on the kitchen floor, and the cracks ran right past my hands, past the wall, past anywhere I could hide them.

Somebody knocked once and opened the door. They didn't ask me where the broken place was. They just pulled out the chair, put the kettle on the flame, and said the only thing I needed: *I'm not going anywhere, say nothing if you need to.* Three hours, no fix. No advice. Just a person who stayed until the breath came back. That night is the whole album. I wrote the title line on the floor of that memory: *carry me home when the world gets loud and I'm down to the bone.*

## What "Carry Me Home" Actually Means

I want to be careful with this title, because our culture hears "carry me" and flinches. We have been trained to think the goal of a life is to never need to be carried again, to graduate out of dependence into a fortress of self-sufficiency.

I don't believe that anymore, and the album is my argument against it. Being carried is not the same as being weak. It is the shape love takes when your legs give out. The strong thing that Tuesday was not me white-knuckling it alone in the dark; it was me letting the door open. The bridge of the first song is the confession the whole record is built on: *even when my legs give out, somebody's got me, I don't doubt.* That is not a surrender of dignity. It is the discovery of where your dignity was actually safe all along.

> Asking was never the weakness. Pretending I didn't need anyone, that was the thing quietly breaking me.

## The Album Is a House Full of People Who Stayed

Every song on this record is somebody who showed up.

There is the friend at 2 AM who picked up and said "hello" like I wasn't crying, who didn't try to fix the broken or dry the tear, just stayed on the line until the crickets were louder than my breathing. There is the one who rang the doorbell at 6:47 with soup and bread and three candles and then sat on the couch for nine whole minutes without a word, because the truest thing I could tell you about grief is *I don't need a fix. I need you to stay.* There is my mama, whose paper-soft hands still know my face, who still says "baby" at thirty-two, and the day I realized she was somebody's daughter once too, held just like she's holding me.

There is "Late Night Thank You," the voice memo I recorded three times before I could send it, because I never said it loud enough to the people who saved me without trying. And "Pass the Love Down," the cookbook recipe handed to a niece in her first apartment, the umbrella given away at a bus stop, the love multiplying every time it changes hands.

The turn of the record is a song called "The Carrier." The phone rings at 1:53 in the morning, and this time it is not for me. This time I'm the one who drives across town, pulls the same cedar blanket, makes the grilled cheese from my own mother's recipe, and walks into the very room I once collapsed in. *I was the empty hands held up, now my hands are the holding.* That is the secret the whole album is chasing: you get carried so that one day you can carry, and the love keeps its shape as it moves down the line.

## The Verse Under the Porch Light

Here is the Scripture I built the house on, and it is not subtle once you know to look for it.

Scripture says to carry one another's burdens, that this is not a failure of the Christian life but the actual weight-bearing of it, the way a body holds itself up. It says to weep with those who weep, which is a stunning instruction when you sit with it: not to fix the weeping, not to explain it, but to get down into it and stay. Every "no-fix, just stay" song on this album is trying to obey that one line. **Yeshua** did not keep His distance from human grief. He entered it. He wept at a friend's tomb before He did anything about it.

And the last song, "Porch Light On," is the whole gospel of the record in one image, a light left burning yellow against the navy-blue dark, a screen door somebody never fixed because the squeak *is* the welcome, a kitchen light kept on all night so the room never goes dark. *You can always come home; the door is the door that won't be gone.* That is the oldest homecoming story there is: the father who watches the road, who runs while the child is still far off, who has a house with room enough kept ready. **Yahuah** is the One who leaves the porch light on. Every human who has ever stayed with you in the dark was borrowing His welcome.

> No matter how the road has been, no matter what you carried in, the door is the door of always, and the light was never turned off.

## Who I Made This For

I made *Carry Me Home* for the person on the kitchen floor right now who thinks the strong move is to hide it.

For anyone with a to-do list torn in half and three voicemails they can't bring themselves to open. For the one who forgot what asking even sounds like. For the friend who has been the carrier for everyone else so long they've forgotten they're allowed to be carried too. For all of us who confused self-sufficiency with strength and ended up alone with the weight.

Let the door open. Let somebody put the kettle on. You do not have to say a word, and you do not have to earn the blanket. There is a soft place to land, and the arms that will carry you were carried first, all the way back to a love that has never once let the light go out. Come home. The door won't be gone.

I got you. And so does He.
