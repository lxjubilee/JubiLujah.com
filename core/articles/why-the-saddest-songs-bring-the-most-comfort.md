Picture a woman driving home in the dark, and out of every song she owns, she reaches for the saddest one.

She turns it up. She lets it ache. Somewhere between one streetlight and the next, something in her chest comes loose, not because the sadness lifted, but because for three minutes she was not the only one holding it.

Then the song ends, and a second feeling walks in. Quieter. Meaner. She feels guilty. Shouldn't she want to feel better than this? What kind of person reaches for the wound on purpose?

Maybe you know that drive.

## The thing nobody explains about sad songs

If you have ever chosen the melancholy song in the middle of your own melancholy, you already know it helps. You have probably never been able to say why. It looks like a small act of self-harm. It works like medicine. Both of those are true at once, and the contradiction is worth standing inside, because something honest is hiding in it.

We have been sold a thin idea of comfort. We think comfort means cheering up. Feel bad, apply happy, feel less bad. By that math, playing a sad song when you are already sinking is madness, pouring water into a boat that is already taking on water.

But that is not what is happening. And the reason it is not is the whole point.

> Sorrow set to a melody is not your grief getting worse. It is your grief getting company.

A sad song does two things a cheerful one cannot do for a person in pain.

First, it tells the truth. When you are grieving, the most isolating thing in the world is the pressure to pretend you are fine. A happy song, arriving too early, does not comfort you. It corrects you. It tells you your sorrow is a problem to be solved, an interruption, something the room would rather you put away. The sad song does the opposite. It says: this is real, and it deserves a melody as much as joy does. It does not rush you. It agrees with you.

Second, and this is the part that heals. It sits down beside you.

Grief convinces you that you are the only one who has ever felt this exact weight. It lies. And a song made by someone you will never meet, in a language of pure feeling, reaches across all that distance and says, I have been here too. Someone built this out of a night like yours. You are not the first and you are not alone, and here is the proof, in a melody, arriving right on time.

That is not indulgence. That is fellowship.

## The Bible has a soundtrack, and so much of it is sorrow

Here is what should settle the guilt for good.

The songbook at the center of the Scriptures, the one people have prayed and sung for thousands of years, is soaked in sorrow. Page after page of it is lament. How long? it asks, out loud, unashamed. Why have You forgotten me? It walks straight through the valley of the shadow of death and never once pretends the valley is a meadow.

Yahuah did not edit those songs out. He kept them. He set the crying ones right next to the celebrating ones and called the whole collection holy. He knew His people would need words for the dark, and He made sure the words were beautiful.

There is a man in that book who was swallowed by the sea, and from inside the belly of the dark, he sang. There is a prophet who looked out at barren fields and a failed harvest, no fruit, no flock, nothing, and decided to make a song of it anyway, not because things were fine, but because Yahuah was still his. And on the last night before the worst of everything, Yeshua Himself, with the cross already in view, stood and sang a hymn before He walked out into the garden.

None of them waited to feel better before they sang. They sang their way through.

## Why I leave some chords unresolved

I write songs, and I will tell you a small secret of the craft. Not every song is meant to end resolved.

There is a chord you can land on that does not settle. It hangs there, leaning forward, asking for a home it never quite reaches inside the song. A lazy ear calls that a mistake. It is not. Sometimes it is the truest thing in the whole piece, because it matches the place the listener actually lives. Some griefs are not resolved yet. To tie a bright bow on them would be a lie, and people can hear a lie in music faster than anywhere else.

An unresolved chord is not a failure. It is a held breath. It is the sound of waiting, and waiting is not the same as being abandoned.

So when a sad song does not fix you, when it just stays with you in the ache and refuses to fake an ending, that is the song being honest. That honesty is the comfort. It is not offering you a cure it does not have. It is offering you company you actually need.

> A lament sung is grief that has stopped being carried alone.

That is what your saddest songs have been doing for you all along. They were never the wound. They were the hand that reached into the wound and held on.

So the next time you reach for the melancholy one in the middle of your own dark drive, let me lift one weight off you: put the guilt down. You are not wallowing. You are doing something ancient and human and holy. You are refusing to lie about your pain, and you are letting yourself be accompanied in it.

And here is the door I will leave open, softly.

A sung sorrow reaches for company, and there has been Someone in the passenger seat the whole drive. The One who wrote a whole book of laments so you would have words for the nights you couldn't find your own.

The next sad song you play, you don't have to play it to Him on purpose. But you could. Just once, let it be a prayer instead of a private ache, the same melody, aimed at the only One who has heard every verse of your grief and never once asked you to hurry up and feel better.

He kept the sad songs in the book for a reason.

I think the reason was you.
