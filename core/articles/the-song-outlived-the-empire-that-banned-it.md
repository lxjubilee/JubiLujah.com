Walk the ruins of a great capital sometime, one of the old ones out where the deserts of the Middle East keep their secrets, and pay attention to the quiet.

Not the peaceful kind. The other kind. The quiet of a place that used to roar. Once there were processions here, and drums, and the names of kings shouted until the walls shook. Now a guide reads those names off a card, and no one in the group has heard of a single one, and the wind moves over broken stone the way it moves over anything that has stopped mattering. Tourists take a photo. They check the time. They move on.

That is what an empire finally comes to. Silence, and a card someone reads without feeling.

Now hold that picture against a second one, because the two belong together and almost no one sets them side by side.

Somewhere far from those ruins, in a kitchen, on a bus, under the breath of a woman washing dishes, a song is being sung right now that was written by a captive of that very empire. Written in chains. Written by one of the people that capital dragged off in ropes to entertain the winners. The empire is a field of rubble a guide reads about. The captive's song is on living lips in a hundred languages this morning.

Sit with that arithmetic, because it is the whole sermon. The empire had the armies, the walls, the gold, the decrees. The captive had a melody and a wound. Guess which one is still here.

## They asked for the song as a trophy

The old songbook keeps a lament from exactly that season, and it is one of the most honest things ever written down.

A people sat down by the rivers of a foreign land and wept, remembering home. They had hung their instruments in the branches of the trees along the water, because who can play when everything is gone. And their captors, this is the detail I cannot get past, their captors asked them for a song. Not to comfort them. For sport. *Sing us one of the songs of your home. Entertain us. Perform your holy things for our evening.*

Understand what was really being asked. The empire did not just want the land and the temple and the young men. It wanted the song too. It wanted to take the most sacred sound these people owned and turn it into background music for a winner's feast, to prove that even their worship now belonged to the throne that had beaten them.

And the captives said no. *How can we sing Yahuah's song in a strange land?* They would not hand it over as a party trick. They would not let the holy thing become a trophy on the empire's shelf.

But here is what people miss when they read it fast: their refusal to perform the song was not the end of the song. The refusal *became* the song. That very lament, the ache, the hanging harps, the will not, the cannot, got written down and set to music, and it has been sung ever since by people who never saw those rivers. They would not sing it *for* the empire. So they sang it *past* the empire, over its head, into every century that would come after it fell.

The trophy the empire reached for is the one thing it never got to keep.

## Why the song outlasts the throne

There is a reason this keeps happening, and it is not sentimental. It is structural. It is built into what a song actually is.

A throne rules by force, and force has a shelf life. It can hold a border for a while, censor a page for a while, march a people off for a while. But force has to be renewed every single day by someone with a spear, and the day always comes when there is no one left to hold the spear, and then the whole apparatus goes quiet all at once and becomes a field a guide reads about.

A song rules by love, and love does not need a spear. It does not live in a building you can burn or a scroll you can seize. It lives in the chest, and it moves the only way that cannot be stopped at a checkpoint, from a mother to a child, from a grandfather humming to a boy half-listening, from one trembling voice in a guarded room to the person one wall over who was starting to believe there was no such sound left in the world. You cannot confiscate that. You cannot even find it. By the time a regime notices the song, it is already in ten thousand hearts, and hearts do not open for inspection.

This is why every power that ever set out to erase a people has gone after the songs early, and why not one of them has ever finished the job. You can level the temple and the song walks out in the mouths of the survivors. You can outlaw the gathering and the song is hummed over a cradle where no informer can hear. Empires keep making the same mistake because they measure strength in stone, and the strongest thing in the room was never the stone.

The morning stars were singing, the old account says, while the foundations of the world were being laid, song was there before the first empire drew its first border, and it will be there when the last one is a card a guide reads without feeling. Praise is not a fragile thing the powers permit. It is the oldest and most durable sound there is, and every throne that has moved against it has been outlived by it.

## To the one singing on hard ground

So let me speak to the person for whom this is not history.

Maybe you are worshiping somewhere it costs something. A place on the map that gets shaded a careful color in the news. A home where the curtains stay closed and the volume stays low and a knock at the wrong hour still stops your heart. You love a song you cannot sing at full voice, in a language the guarded places speak, Arabic, Turkish, the tongues of the hard frontiers, and some tired part of you wonders whether a sound that small, sung that quietly, under that much weight, could possibly matter against everything arrayed to keep it down.

Hear me, from one frontier voice to another. It is the empire that is temporary. Not your song.

The thing pressing on you right now feels permanent because it has the spears and the paperwork and the loud confidence of everything that is about to become rubble. It has always felt permanent, to every captive, in every century, right up until the morning the wind was moving over its broken stones and a guide was reading its dead kings off a card, while the song of the ones it tried to silence went on being sung in the kitchens of the free.

You are not on the losing side of this. You are on the side that outlasts.

So sing it, quietly if you must, wounded if you must, one trembling verse into the dark if that is all tonight will allow. You are not making noise against a wall. You are laying down a sound that will still be here when the wall is gone, a witness that Yahuah was worthy even here, even now, even under this. The empire wanted your song for a trophy.

Give it to Heaven instead, and watch what refuses to die.
