There is a scene from the ancient world that I wish every believer could stand inside for just one night, because I think it would change forever the way you hear a sentence you have read a hundred times.

Picture Jerusalem at night, during the autumn Feast of Tabernacles, Sukkot, the great seven-day harvest festival. But do not picture it dark. Picture it *glowing.*

## The great lights of the Temple

Here is the detail I want to hand you, and I want to label it honestly right at the start: what I am about to describe is preserved in the later Jewish tradition, the Mishnah's memory of the Second-Temple celebration, not spelled out in the Torah. So take it as tradition, lovingly remembered. But oh, what a memory.

The sages recalled that in the Court of the Women, during this feast, they set up enormous golden lampstands, towering menorahs so tall that young men had to climb ladders to reach the bowls at the top and fill them with oil. Each lampstand carried multiple great basins of flame. Worn-out priestly garments were torn into wicks to feed the fire. And the tradition says that when these giant lamps were lit, the light that poured out of the Temple courts was so vast that it lit up *every courtyard in Jerusalem.* There was not a home, the old memory says, that the glow of that place did not reach.

Let that sink in as a piece of the ancient world. In an age with no electricity, an age when night was truly, deeply dark, when a single oil lamp was precious and most of the world went to sleep when the sun did, one place in one city blazed so brightly that the whole town glowed with borrowed light. People danced beneath those lamps with torches in their hands. The finest and most joyful men of the nation whirled and juggled flame through the night while musicians played, and the courts roared with gladness until dawn.

> There had never been a light like it. In all the world, on those nights, the brightest thing a human being could stand inside was the house of Yahuah.

## Into the brightest room in the world

Now. Hold that scene, the towering lamps, the golden glow spilling over the walls, a whole city bathed in light streaming out of the Temple, and let me bring you to a moment recorded in the Gospel of John.

Yeshua was in the Temple during this very feast. And in the treasury, in the Court of the Women, the very place where those colossal lampstands stood. He stood up before the crowds and said:

*I am the light of the world. Whoever follows Me will never walk in darkness, but will have the light of life.*

I need you to feel the sheer nerve of that, spoken in *that* room. He did not say it in a quiet corner or an empty field. He said it standing in what may have been the most spectacularly illuminated place on the face of the earth, surrounded by the greatest lamps human hands had ever raised, in the middle of a festival whose light was the talk of the ancient world. And into all that borrowed, oil-fed, ladder-tended, night-long human brilliance, light that had to be climbed to, poured into, and constantly refueled or it would gutter out by morning. He said, in effect: *all of this is beautiful, and all of this is temporary, and I am the real thing it was always pointing to.*

## Light that does not need refilling

This is where the wonder gets almost too large to hold, so let me slow down and touch it gently, the way I touch anything that is both true and enormous.

Every one of those magnificent lamps depended on something outside itself. The oil had to be carried up. The wicks had to be trimmed. The fire had to be tended all night or it would fade. For all their glory, they were vessels *holding* a light that came from somewhere else and would run out if the somewhere-else stopped supplying it. That is the honest nature of every light we make and every light we are, reflected, refueled, borrowed, and forever one dry basin away from going dark.

And into the brightest gathering of borrowed lights the world had ever assembled, Yeshua claimed to be a different kind of light entirely. Not a vessel holding light. The Source. Not a lamp that must be filled, but the flame that fills the lamps. He was not adding one more torch to the festival. He was announcing that He was the thing the whole blazing spectacle had been a rehearsal for.

I love that He chose *that* setting. He could have made the claim in the dark, where any small flame looks impressive by contrast. Instead He made it in a flood of the greatest light people could produce, as if to say, *bring me your brightest. Bring me the tallest lamps you can build and the most oil you can pour and the most beautiful festival of fire your tradition has ever staged, and even here, even now, you are still living in a light that has to be refueled by morning. Follow Me, and you will carry a light that never runs out.*

## The glow that finds every courtyard

Here is the part that turns my heart over.

The tradition remembered those Temple lamps most fondly for one thing: their light did not stay behind the Temple walls. It *spilled.* It overflowed the courts and reached down every street and touched every courtyard in the city, until even the ordinary home far from the center was lit by the glow of the holy place.

That is exactly the kind of light Yeshua is. He does not stay behind a wall. The light of life He offers is not hoarded in a sanctuary for the few who can get close enough to the great lamps. It pours out. It reaches the far courtyard, the ordinary home, the person nowhere near the center of things, the one who assumed the glow was for other people, brighter people, people with ladders and oil and access. And to every one of them He says the same seven audacious words, still ringing out of the brightest room in history:

*I am the light of the world.*

So the next time you read those words in a quiet moment, put them back in the room where He first said them. Feel the golden glow of a whole city lit from one place, the dancing and the torches and the towering lamps that had to be climbed and fed all night. And then hear the voice, steady in the middle of all that fire, offering you a light you will never have to refill, a light that will find you in whatever courtyard you thought was too far from the flame.

You were never as far from the light as the dark made you feel. He has always been the kind that reaches every street.
