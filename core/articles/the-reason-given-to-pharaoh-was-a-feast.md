Say the word *Exodus* and most of us see the same pictures. Frogs and hail. A staff turned serpent. A firstborn plague at midnight. A wall of water standing up on either side of a fleeing people. We remember it as the greatest jailbreak in history, and it was. But the jailbreak was never the point. It was the door. And a door is not a destination.

Go back and listen, really listen, to the sentence Moses was handed to carry into the most powerful throne room on earth. He does not walk in and say, *let my people go, they've suffered enough.* He does not say, *let them go, they deserve their liberty.* Both would be true. Neither is what he was told to say.

He says: **let my people go, that they may hold a feast to me in the wilderness.**

That is the reason given to Pharaoh for the undoing of an empire. Not escape. A feast. And not a feast in a palace or a promised city, a feast in the *wilderness*, in the emptiest, driest, most unpromising room on the map.

## Two masters, one word

Here is where the Hebrew opens the whole thing up.

The word that runs like a heartbeat under the Exodus story is **avad**, to serve, to work, to labor. From it comes **avodah**, which means *service*, and it means both kinds at once. The back-breaking service of a slave, and the lifted-hands service of a worshiper. Same root. Same word.

When the story opens, Israel is doing *avodah* for Pharaoh. Bricks. Straw. Quotas. Backs bent under a service that is only taking, never giving, that consumes people and returns nothing. And Yahuah's demand, again and again, is not *stop serving.* It is: **let them go, that they may serve Me.**

It was never a choice between service and freedom. Nobody in that story gets to serve no one. It was a choice of *masters.* The question the plagues were asking Egypt was never *will Israel be idle?* It was *whose avodah will these hands do?* You were made to serve. The only real question your life has ever asked is *whom.*

> Pharaoh's service made bricks and buried the people who made them. Yahuah's service makes a feast, and the people who make it are the guests.

## He wanted the desert, not the palace

Now sit with the strangeness of the location. He could have asked for anything. He is the one splitting seas. He could have said, *let them go that they may feast in the courts of kings, that the nations may see them honored.* Instead: *in the wilderness.* Three days' journey out, into the waste, to sacrifice to Him there.

Why the desert?

Because in the desert there is nothing to compete with Him. No monuments. No granaries. No stone gods with painted eyes. No security you can point to and say *this is what keeps me.* The wilderness is the one place where a people finds out, with nothing in their hands, whether they will worship the One who brought them or crawl back to the one who fed them. And Yahuah, who loves them, wanted them there. Not to punish them. To have them, really have them, with no rival in the room.

The word Moses uses for what they will hold there is **chag**, a pilgrimage feast, the kind you travel to, the kind your whole body goes to, not a thing you observe from a distance but a thing you *walk into.* The three great appointed feasts of Israel would carry this same word. It is the word for a celebration you go on a journey to reach. And the first one, the founding one, was to be held on sand, under open sky, by a people who owned nothing and had just been handed everything.

## Freedom that forgets its reason turns back

This is the piece the wilderness generation kept losing, and it is the piece we lose too. They were freed *for* something. The chains came off so the hands could be lifted. And every time they forgot the *for*, every time freedom became merely the absence of Egypt instead of the presence of Yahuah, their faces turned back toward the river. *We remember the fish, the melons, the leeks.* You will always romanticize the old master the moment you forget the feast you were freed to hold.

Freedom that forgets its reason does not stay free. It just shops for a gentler cage.

And if that lands hard on you tonight. You who got out of something, who fought your way clear of a job, a house, a habit, a person, a version of yourself, and found the open country lonelier and dryer than you thought escape would feel, hear the old sentence again. You were not brought out only to be *not-there.* You were brought out to feast. The emptiness you are standing in is not the failure of your freedom. It may be the very room He chose for it, because it is the only room with no rival in it.

## The Lamb, and the feast that never ended

You cannot tell this story honestly and stop at Moses, because the whole thing was pointing.

The night the people walked free, they walked out on the strength of a lamb. Its blood on the doorframe, its body eaten in haste, belts fastened, sandals on, ready to move. Israel has kept that feast every year since, and long after, on the night of that same feast, Yeshua took the bread and the cup of it and said, *this is Me.* The Lamb whose blood the whole first Exodus was rehearsing had come to stand in the door Himself.

Jesus did not come to make you comfortable in Egypt. He came to do what Moses' words demanded of Pharaoh: to let a people go, that they may hold a feast to Yahuah, no longer three days out in a borrowed desert, but forever, in His own presence, as sons and daughters and not as slaves. The chains He breaks are real. But He breaks them *for* something. The service He calls you out of is the kind that buries you. The service He calls you into is the kind where you are the honored guest at the table you helped set.

So if you have been treating your freedom as the finish line, stop. It was always the door. Walk through it. There is a feast on the other side, and it has been waiting in the wilderness with your name on the guest list since before Pharaoh ever heard his own doom spoken in a plain shepherd's voice.

You were not just brought out. You were brought *to.* Go and feast.
