She knows every word to the song, and she is not lying when she sings them.

Third row, same seat. Hands lifted at the right moment, because that is what you do here. She served in the nursery this morning. She will carry a meal to the family with the new baby on Tuesday. She reads the chapter before bed most nights, even the nights she reads the same paragraph three times and takes in none of it.

And somewhere under all of it, in a room she has stopped visiting, her heart is as dry as chalk.

Here is what no one has told her. She is not failing. She is worn out from carrying the wrong thing up the wrong hill.

Because she is waiting for revival. The flood. The fire. The day the dryness finally breaks and something real comes rushing back. And she believes, quietly, the way you believe the things you never say out loud, that when it comes, joy will come with it. Joy as the payoff. Joy as the medal they hang on you at the end of the drought, once you have proven you can go without it long enough.

That belief is exactly backwards. And it is the thing keeping her outside.

> Joy isn't the trophy revival hands you at the finish line. It's the door the fire walks in through.

Refuse the door, and the fire has no way in. You can serve at a locked entrance for forty years.

## The man who would not come inside

Someone did exactly this. He stood outside a house shaking with music, close enough to feel the floor move, and would not walk in. And the strangest part of the whole story is that he was the good son.

You know the shape of it. The younger brother took the money, spent it on nothing that lasted, hit the bottom, and came home rehearsing an apology. But the father saw him while he was still a long way off and ran, an old man, running, and before the boy could finish his speech there was a robe, a ring, a calf, and a band striking up. Music and dancing, spilling out the windows into the yard.

And the older son came in from the field.

He heard the music before he saw anything. And he stopped at the edge of the light. He would not go in.

The father did not send a servant to fetch him. He came out himself, into the dark, and pleaded with his own son to come inside. And the son answered, and listen to what a dry heart sounds like when it finally speaks:

All these years I served you. I never once disobeyed. And you never threw me a party.

Served. Never disobeyed. You never gave me. Do you hear the arithmetic in it? He had turned his whole faithfulness into a ledger, and the ledger said he was owed. He was standing six feet from a joy his entire family was already inside of, and the very thing keeping him on the porch was how right he had been.

That is the joy-starved churchgoer, drawn in a single sentence two thousand years old.

## The party was already going

Here is the reframe, and it changes everything.

The father does not say, come inside and I will start a celebration for you now that you've earned one. He says the party is already happening. The fire is already in the house. And then the line that should stop the older brother's heart: everything I have is already yours.

The joy was not down the road, waiting to be deserved. It was through a door he could have touched. He was simply too tired and too right to walk through it.

That is the trap. Duty convinces you that joy is a wage. So you keep working, waiting for payday, and payday is a door standing open the whole time, and you keep walking past it because it feels like it should cost more than that.

## What joy actually is

It is not a mood you manufacture. It is not the froth on top of a good service. Strip it down and Scripture keeps calling joy something much sturdier than a feeling.

On the day a whole nation stood weeping under the weight of the words they had ignored for years, they were not told to cheer up. They were told this: do not grieve, for the joy of Yahuah is your strength. Not the reward of your strength. Its source. The thing you draw on to keep standing.

And when Yeshua Himself walked toward the worst day, what carried Him was a joy set before Him, joy on the front end of the cross, not a prize handed over at the end of it.

Now hold that against the oldest instruction for coming near to Yahuah at all: you enter His gates with thanksgiving, His courts with praise. Thanksgiving is not the party inside. It is the gate. Praise is not the throne room. It is the courtyard you cross to reach it.

Joy is how you get in. It was never what you find once you're in and have finally earned the right to smile.

## So what does the dry one do?

Not work up a feeling. Not fake a face. The older brother did not need to gin up happiness in the yard. He needed to set down the ledger and walk through the door his father was holding open with both hands.

Joy begins as a decision to come inside long before it is ever a feeling. Obedient praise and felt praise are both true worship. You can sing on empty and it still counts, and more often than anyone admits, the singing on empty is the very hand reaching for the door.

You do not have to feel it first. You have to come in.

Revival, the fire everyone is waiting for, does not arrive as a bonus for enough dryness endured. It comes in through the door marked joy. Thanksgiving pries it open. Praise swings it wide. And the fire you were sure you had to earn walks in the moment you stop guarding the entrance with your own faithfulness.

The father is still on the porch. Himself, not a servant. The music is already playing. It did not wait for the tired son to feel ready, and it is not waiting for you.

There is a door in front of the dutiful and the dry, and it was never locked from the inside. It only ever opened inward, toward the noise and the light.

Tonight, before you sleep, say one true thank-you into the dark, not because your heart is in it, but a step ahead of your heart. That sentence is the handle.

Then go quiet, and listen for the music that has been playing the whole time.
