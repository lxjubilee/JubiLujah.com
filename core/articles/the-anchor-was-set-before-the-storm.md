The phone rang at 2:47 in the morning, and the clock became a character in the room.

I have sat with too many people in that exact hour to pretend I don't know it. The biopsy came back. The marriage is over. The child is in the emergency room. The voice on the other end says something you cannot unsay, and the floor you were standing on goes sideways while you are still holding the receiver. I wrote *Anchor in the Storm* for that hour, and for the twelve weeks that come after it, because that hour deserved better than a platitude, and the people living in it deserved a song that would not lie to them.

## I refused to write an album that ends the storm

Here is the thing I decided before I wrote a single line: this record would not pretend the wind dies down by track four. It doesn't. The waves are still breaking at track nine. I have watched well-meaning faith promise people that if they believe hard enough the weather will change, and then I have watched those same people drown in shame when the weather didn't. So I made a different promise. The storm on this album is real, and it is allowed to be real. What changes across the twelve songs is not the weather. It is your relationship to the deck under your feet.

That is why the opener, "The Phone Call," names an actual time. Not "hard times." Not "the valley." *Two forty-seven.* The cup of cold coffee. The chair turned to the wall at 4 a.m. Grief does not arrive in the abstract, and neither should the God who meets you in it. Underneath every track there is a slow, steady kick drum we called the anchor pulse, a heartbeat that will not move even when the melody tears. That was on purpose. The production itself is the theology: the storm is audible, but it is never the loudest thing in the room.

## What the title actually means

An anchor is not an explanation. It is a weight in the deep and a rope in your hand.

The whole album stands on one verse, the writer of Hebrews calling our hope "an anchor for the soul, firm and secure" (Hebrews 6:19). I love that he does not say the anchor calms the sea. He says it holds the soul. There is a world of pastoral difference between those two things. So on "Hold the Rope" I tried to write the plainest song I could, the one a hospice nurse could play for a family while a non-religious spouse in the corner quietly starts to cry. *You don't have to see the Anchor yet. You just have to hold the rope He put in your hand.* You can hold that rope with your eyes closed. You can hold it when you are too tired to say one word to God. He is not asking you for a theology. He is asking you for your hand, and the Spirit of **Yahuah** will carry the rest.

Then, three songs later, the whole record turns over on itself. On "You Were the Deep All Along" the man in the boat stops waiting for the anchor to be thrown and looks down, and realizes it was already set, in the deep, before the storm ever came. The rescue did not arrive from the horizon. It rose from underneath. **Yeshua** was not a rescue coming; He was the floor that had been there the whole time. That is the sentence I most wanted to hand you: you are not catching up to grace. Grace has been under you since before the wind knew your name.

> The Anchor never had to be thrown, Lord. You were already the floor.

## The songs are a slow walk from thrown to held

The middle of the album is where the wind does its worst. "When the Wind Tears at Me" is the altar, a man with the rope going taut in his fists, soaked but not drowned, tossed but not thrown, discovering that grace is deeper than the wind. I sang it low and rasped and let my voice crack, because a voice that never breaks cannot minister to people who are breaking.

But I could not end the record with one man alone on a deck. That is not how rescue actually works. So "Not Alone in This Storm" is the moment the cabin door opens and there are other hands on the rope beside yours, the friend who showed up with soup, the brother who texted at 2:14 a.m., the elder's worn hand on your shoulder in the back of the sanctuary. And "We Hold On Together" says the part pride never lets us say out loud: *when my hands are tired, your hands take the rope; when yours are tired, mine take it.* The church is not a building. It is the hands **Yahuah** sends. Then "Scars Are Evidence" reframes the whole ordeal, your wounds are not your shame, they are the receipt that the rope was in your hand. And "After the Storm" walks you out changed, the deck steady, humming an old and true line: *it is well with my soul*, not because the sea was always calm, but because He walked the deep with you.

## Who I made this for

I made this for the exhausted one reading a diagnosis they can't pronounce. For the parent in the parking lot who has to fix their face before they walk back inside. For the pastor whose public role won't let them fall apart in private. For the believer whose theology promised no storms and whose storm came anyway, and who has been quietly terrified that God moved, or was never there. Hear me: He did not move. He was already in the room the morning the phone rang.

You do not have to hold the rope alone, and you do not have to hold it well. You just have to hold it. The Anchor was set in the deep before you were old enough to reach for it, and the same hands that set it are the ones now closing around yours. The storm does not get the last word. He does, and He is holding you here.
