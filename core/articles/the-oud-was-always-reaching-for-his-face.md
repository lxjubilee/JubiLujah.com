I have played the oud since my hands were too small for its neck. It is the grandfather of instruments where I come from, round-bellied, fretless, a voice more than a machine. And for a long time I used it the way most people use the thing they love: to say things about God. To describe Him. To point at Him from a respectful distance. This album is the day the pointing stopped and the addressing began.

*Maqam of the Heart*, the record I titled, for the world outside my streets, *The Heart Belongs to Yasu'*, is the sound of an instrument finally reaching the One it was always reaching for. The opening song says it in the first breath: *the oud was always reaching for Your face, every string my fathers loved now sings of Your grace.* I did not decide that. I discovered it. The longing that had been in the wood the whole time had a destination, and the destination is **Yeshua**.

## The line that changed everything for me

There is one sentence in the opening track that I could not sing for a long time without stopping. *I do not sing about You, my Lord, I sing to You.*

That is a small grammatical change, *about* to *to*, and it is the entire difference between religion and love. You can describe a person you have never met. You can only address one you know. For most of my life I had beautiful, respectful, distant songs about the Most High, sung in the finest maqam my people ever built. And then there came a day when the wall between about and to came down, and the songs turned around and looked Him in the face. This album is what came out of my mouth after that.

The whole record is arranged as a heart's journey home. It opens giving Him everything, *the whole of my heart belongs to You, every road that I'm walking is a road back to You*, and it closes twelve songs later with "Come Home to You," the circle shut, the wanderer arrived. In between, it does not pretend the road is only bright.

## He is worth singing your grief to

The second song on the album is called "Wounded King," and I put it early on purpose. Before we celebrate, we look at the One who was wounded for us, the Man of Sorrows of Isaiah 53, the King who wears His scars into glory.

I come from a part of the world that knows sorrow the way it knows the sun, daily, and without asking permission. I did not want an album that skipped over that to get to the happy songs. So "Wounded King" takes grief and does not deny it, but redirects it: *in the sorrow I am learning You, my Lord, every yearning note a step toward Your face.* The lament does not stay lament. It becomes a way of drawing near.

That instinct is deep in my culture and deep in the Bible at the same time. Later on the record, "Tears at His Feet" reaches for the woman in Luke 7 who broke open her alabaster jar and wept at the feet of Yeshua, and was not scolded, but received. And "In the Quiet" goes to the one place we least expect God: not the wind, not the earthquake, not the fire, but the low still voice that met the prophet Elijah at the mouth of the cave (1 Kings 19). My people fill silence with hospitality and song. This album makes room for the other thing, the quiet where He actually speaks.

> Every trembling tone a cry upon Your Name, Saviour of the broken, I sing my grief to Your grace.

## The song with no translation

If I had to hand one track to a stranger to explain the whole record, it would be "The Oud Speaks Your Name."

It is a small idea with a large heart behind it. The chorus says: *the oud speaks Your Name, Yasu', no translation, You understand the sound; every string is a prayer, every fret is a psalm, in the wordless of the music, Your nearness is found.* I built that song on a verse that has carried me for years, where the Scripture says the Spirit helps us in our weakness, because we do not know what to pray as we ought, and He intercedes with groans too deep for words (Romans 8). There is a place past language. Everyone who has ever wept and could not explain why has been there. And music is the mother tongue of that place.

That is why I refuse to auto-tune these vocals or trade the real oud, ney, and qanun for machines. The trembling *is* the message. When the string bends between two notes in a way no piano can, that microtonal ache is not decoration. It is the sound of a heart reaching. God made that sound. He understands it without a dictionary.

## The split He healed

There is a wound particular to my people that this album finally names out loud, near the end, in a song called "Arab Heart, His Throne." For a long time I carried a quiet division inside me, a sense that to belong to Christ I would have to become someone else, someone from another country, and leave the oud and the courtyard and the mother tongue behind as though they were the problem.

They were never the problem. In that song I sing the thing I most needed to hear: *You knew the split that was inside me, between the one I was and the One I belong unto, and You healed it with Your body on the cross-beam, You made me whole, You made me one.* I did not have to stop being an Arab to belong to **Yeshua**. It was in Antioch, a city of my own region, that the followers of Jesus were first called Christians (Acts 11). The name was born on our streets. I am not a guest in this faith. I am home in it.

That is who this record is for. It is for the heart that has heard a hundred songs about Jesus and never once heard one that sounded like where it came from. It is for the believer who suspected, secretly, that they would have to leave their own beauty at the door. You do not. Bring the saffron of your mornings and the indigo of your nights, bring the oud your grandfather held, bring the whole divided heart, and let the instrument finish the sentence it started before you were born. It was always reaching for His face. Let it arrive.
