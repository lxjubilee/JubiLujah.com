The first time someone explained the bomba to me, I was sure I had misunderstood the words.

I know drums. I come from a people whose worship has a heartbeat in it, who drummed on shipping crates when the drums themselves were taken away. In almost every music I had ever played, the drummer keeps the time and the dancer follows. The rhythm is the floor and the dance is the house you build on it. The drum leads. Everyone knows this.

So when the old players told me that in the bomba it is the other way around, that the *dancer* leads and the *drummer* follows. I laughed, because I thought it was a joke or a translation problem. Then they took me to see it, and it was neither.

The dancer steps into the center of the ring. The lead drummer, on the barrel drum they call the *primo*, fixes his eyes not on his own hands but on her feet, her hips, the sudden fling of her skirt. And she begins to challenge him. She throws a movement, a stamp, a turn, a snap of the cloth, and it is his job to catch it, to answer that exact gesture with a strike of the drum that lands in the same instant. She feints and he must not be fooled. She holds and he must hold with her. She explodes and he explodes under her. The whole ring is watching a conversation where the body speaks first and the drum has to keep up, and the drummer who cannot follow is the one who is embarrassed, not the dancer.

I stood there and something in my understanding of worship turned over on its side and never turned back.

## Sometimes the body has to go first

We have a deep instinct, especially those of us who lead worship for a living, to make everything wait for the music. The band sets the tempo, the singer sets the feel, and the congregation is expected to *follow*, to fall in line behind whatever we have prepared, to keep step with a rhythm we set for them. And there is a place for that. Order is a gift.

But the bomba insists on a truth that the polished stage tends to bury: sometimes the praise in a person's body has to be allowed to lead, and everything else has to learn to follow *it*. Sometimes the Spirit moves in the least prepared person in the room, the grandmother in the third row, the man who cannot carry a tune, the child who does not know it is not the moment, and their whole body says *He is worthy* before the song has gotten there, and the honest thing, the holy thing, is for the music to catch up to them rather than the other way around.

The dancer in the bomba is not showing off. She is expressing something that must come out, and the drum's entire dignity is spent in trying to honor it. I have come to believe that is a picture of how Yahuah receives our worship. He is not up in heaven insisting we all stay on His tempo before He will pay attention. He is the *primo* drummer, leaning in, watching the feet of the least likely dancer in the ring, delighting to answer the movement of a heart that could not hold still.

## The king who danced ahead of the music

There is a day in Israel's history when the body of the king led and the whole nation's music had to follow, and it cost him something, and he did not care.

The ark of God was coming home at last. David had it carried into the city with an entire orchestra, harps and lyres and tambourines and cymbals and the ram's horn, the full sound of a people who had waited too long for this. And in front of all of it, out ahead of the musicians, went the king himself. He danced. The account does not say he danced with restraint appropriate to his office. It says he danced before Yahuah with all his might. He had stripped off the royal robes and put on a simple linen garment, and he leapt and he whirled at the head of the procession like a man who had forgotten there was such a thing as a throne.

He was the dancer in the ring, and all the drums of Israel were behind him, and the movement of his worship set the pace for the whole city.

His wife Michal watched it from a window, and the Scripture is careful to tell us what was in her heart: she *despised* him. When he came home glowing she met him with contempt, sneering that the king had made a fool of himself in front of the servant girls, uncovering himself like one of the vulgar people who did not know any better. She wanted the dignity kept. She wanted the body kept still and the office protected and the worship kept at a respectable distance.

And David's answer is one I have leaned on my whole life. He told her that it was before Yahuah that he danced, the God who chose him over her father's house, and that he would be even more undignified than this, and humble himself even further in his own eyes, and that the very servant girls she was ashamed of him in front of were the ones who would hold him in honor. He would not shrink the dance to fit her window. He would let his body lead, and let the drums of an entire nation follow, and let his dignity land wherever it landed.

## Let the drum catch up to your joy

I have met so many people who worship like Michal is watching. They keep the body still. They keep the joy at a respectable volume. They will move only after they are certain everyone else has decided it is acceptable, only after the music has clearly given permission, only after the risk of looking foolish has passed. And I understand it, because the barrio taught me long ago that a joyful people are always being told they are too much.

But the bomba, and the dancing king, keep pressing the same question on me. What if your praise was never meant to wait for permission? What if there is a movement of gratitude in you that is supposed to go *first*, ahead of the polished band, ahead of the acceptable moment, ahead of your own carefully guarded dignity, and the honest thing is to let it out and trust that heaven will answer it, that the drum of God will lean in and catch every stamp and turn of a heart that finally stopped holding still?

The dancer does not fear the drum. She trusts that it will follow her. And the God I serve is not standing back waiting for you to compose yourself. He is leaning in, watching your feet, ready to answer the smallest true movement of your joy with a strike that lands in the very same instant.

So step into the ring, mi gente. Let the body say it. The drum will catch up. It always has, it always will, and it will be more undignified than this before it is done.
