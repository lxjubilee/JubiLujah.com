Somewhere along the way we decided that clapping in worship was a little embarrassing.

We treat it like the tolerated cousin of real reverence, fine for the lively churches, maybe, but a step down from the hushed and the holy. If you have ever felt your hands hesitate, half-lifted, unsure whether to join in, I understand. Somebody trained that hesitation into you. And I want to spend a few minutes untraining it, because the psalm that commands the clap did not mean a gentle patter of approval. It reached for one of the most physical, forceful words in the whole language, and it aimed it at the entire human race.

## Taqa: the strike that means business

There is a short, thunderous line near the front of one of the psalms: *clap your hands, all you peoples.* In Hebrew the phrase is built on two words. The second is *kaph*, the palm, the flat open hand. And the first is the verb *taqa*, and *taqa* is where the whole thing comes alive.

*Taqa* is not a soft word. Its basic sense is to strike, to drive, to thrust something home with force. It is the verb you use for driving a tent peg into the ground with a mallet, a hard, decisive, whole-arm blow. And here is the detail that ought to change the way you worship for good: *taqa* is the very same verb the Scriptures use for **sounding a trumpet.** When the shofar is blown, when the ram's horn splits the air over the camp, the Hebrew says they *taqa* the horn. Struck it. Drove the blast out of it.

So read the command again with that in your ears. *Taqa kaph*, strike the palm. The psalm is not asking for polite applause. It is telling you that when you bring your two hands together in praise, you are doing with your body exactly what the trumpeter does with the horn. You are sounding a blast. Your palms are the instrument, and the strike is the note, and the sound that cracks off ten thousand pairs of hands at once is meant to carry the same weight in heaven as a wall of trumpets.

> Your hands and the war-horn share one verb. When you clap, you are not being casual. You are being loud on purpose, the way a trumpet is loud on purpose.

That is why it feels the way it feels. When a whole room strikes their hands together and the sound rolls up like a wave, something in your chest answers it, because your body already knows, underneath all the training, that this is a blast and not a background noise. The old worshipers were not clapping to keep time politely. They were making a joyful thunder with the only trumpet everyone is born holding.

## All peoples, the doors thrown wide

Now catch the other half of the line, because it is just as glorious and even easier to miss. *Clap your hands, all you peoples.*

Not *Israel.* Not the priests. Not the chosen few with the right lineage and the right seats. *All peoples.* Every nation. The psalm swings the doors of the celebration open to the whole world in its opening breath. It is standing in the street, cupping its hands around its mouth, and calling everyone, the neighbors, the strangers, the far-off tribes who had never once been inside the courts, to come and strike their hands and shout for joy to the God over all the earth.

This is the thing the prophets could never stop hinting at and the celebration finally makes obvious: Yahuah was never running a small, exclusive party. The whole trajectory of the story is a God gathering more and more peoples into one enormous, noisy, hand-striking family. When the psalm says *all peoples,* it is prophesying the day when the room would be so full of nations that no single language could hold the sound of the clapping.

And you and I are living downstream of that prophecy. If you are not of Israel by blood, and most of us reading this are not, then *all peoples* is the word that put your name on the invitation. You were not tolerated at the edge of someone else's worship. You were summoned by name into the center of it. The clap the psalm commands was written, in part, so that your hands would one day be in the room.

## Bring your hands into the room

So let me talk to the hesitant ones, the half-lifted hands, the folks who were quietly taught that keeping still is more spiritual than joining in.

Reverence is real, and there is a time for the hushed and the trembling. I would never take that from you. But do not let anyone convince you that *quiet* is the only shape holiness is allowed to take. The same Scriptures that command us to be still and know also command us to strike our palms together and shout. A God who only wanted whispers would not have handed His people trumpets and drums and two clapping hands apiece. He built us to make a joyful blast, and then He *told us to.*

Here is the freedom in it. You do not need a beautiful voice to obey this one. You do not need training. You do not need to be musical at all. You were issued the instrument at birth, two open palms, and the only skill required is the willingness to strike them together and mean it. The tone-deaf and the shy and the ones who swore they would never draw attention to themselves can all sound this trumpet perfectly, because *taqa kaph* has nothing to do with talent and everything to do with abandon.

And do not do it alone if you can help it. The clap was written for *all peoples,* plural, a crowd, because there is a sound that only exists when many hands strike at once, a rolling thunder that no single pair could ever make. That sound is a picture of the whole family: every nation, every age, the abuela and the toddler and the stranger who wandered in off the street, all sounding the same blast to the same God, so loud and so glad that the walls give up trying to hold it.

So bring your hands into the room, mi gente. Lift them, and when the moment comes, do not pat, *strike.* Drive the joy home like a tent peg, like a trumpeter filling his lungs, like a person who finally understands that the God over all the earth threw the doors of His celebration wide open and wrote your applause into the very first line. Clap your hands. All of you. He is worth the loudest thing your body knows how to do.
