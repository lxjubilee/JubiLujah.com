I want to let you in on how a song is actually born, because it is almost never the way people think.

People imagine you sit down with something to say, and then you find a tune to carry it. Words first, music second, the melody as a kind of delivery truck for the message. And sometimes it happens that way. But far more often, for me and for most writers I know, it is the reverse. The tune comes first. A melody arrives, wordless, humming itself in the back of the mind on a walk or in the shower or in that strange floating minute before sleep, and it means something, powerfully, urgently, but it does not yet know what it means. It has a shape and a feeling and no words at all. It is a question in the form of a tune, waiting for the sentence that will finally answer it.

I have melodies I have been humming for years that still have no words. Wordless the whole time. I catch myself singing one in the kitchen, no lyric, just la, just the shape of it, and I know it is about something. I know it is reaching for something true. I simply have not yet found the words that fit it, and I have learned not to force them, because a lyric jammed onto a melody it does not belong to is one of the most obvious lies in all of music. So the tune waits. It hums in me, older than its own words, patient, unhurried, sure of what it feels long before it can say what it means.

I have come to believe this is not only how songs work. It is how the deepest part of a human being works when it turns toward God.

## The feeling that arrives before the words

There is a kind of prayer you have prayed that had no words, and I want to name it, because you may not have known it counted.

It is the ache you cannot articulate. The groan at the hospital bedside when language has completely failed. The wordless swell in your chest at a sunrise, or over a sleeping child, or in a moment of grief so total that every sentence you reach for feels like an insult to the size of it. It is the hum with no lyric, a feeling reaching for God that is absolutely real and absolutely wordless, and because you could not put it into a proper sentence, you may have decided it did not count as prayer at all.

Hear me. It counted. It counted more than the polished sentences.

The Scriptures say something about this that I find almost too kind to believe. Paul writes that we do not even know what we ought to pray for as we should, that we come to God not knowing the words, and that in exactly that place, the Spirit himself takes up our cause and intercedes for us with groanings too deep for words. Read that slowly. There is a wordless prayer being prayed. And it is not a lesser prayer, a failed prayer, a prayer that did not make it to the level of language. It is the Spirit of God himself, praying the melody underneath your words when you have run entirely out of words to sing.

That means the hum in your chest at the bedside was not nothing. It was a tune God was already carrying, older than any lyric you could have managed, and he did not need you to finish the sentence to know exactly what it meant.

## The tune knows before you do

Here is the mystery that keeps me humble as a writer: the wordless melody almost always knows more than I do.

When I finally find the words to a tune I have been humming for years, the experience is never that I invented a meaning and pasted it on. It is that I discovered the meaning that was always in there, that the melody had been about this all along, and I simply had not been ready to understand it. The feeling came first and led the way. The words came later and caught up. The tune was the truth; the lyric was me finally learning to read it.

I think your wordless longings work the same way. That ache you cannot name, the homesickness for a home you have never quite seen, the reaching that has no object you can point to, the tune that has been humming in you since you were a child and that no achievement or relationship has ever fully set to words. I do not think it is confusion. I think it is a melody that knows more than you do. It is reaching for something real. It has a shape and a direction and a certainty, long before you can say what it means. And the great error is to decide that because you cannot name it, it must not be pointing at anything.

It is pointing at Someone. The wordless tune in you was always a tune about God, the way my kitchen melodies are always about something true before I can say what. You have been humming your way toward him your whole life.

## He hears the tune, not just the lyric

There is a picture in the Scriptures I return to constantly: God singing over his people. The prophet says the Lord will quiet you with his love and rejoice over you with singing. And I notice he does not tell us the words. There is no lyric printed. It is described simply as singing, a wordless, exulting melody that God sings over the ones he loves, the way you hum, without words, over something too precious to reduce to a sentence.

If God sings over you wordlessly, then he is not a God who only hears the lyric. He hears the tune. He hears the melody underneath your fumbling sentences, the hum you cannot finish, the ache you cannot name, and he understands it completely, because it is, in the end, a song about him, and he has known its meaning since before you first began to hum it.

So I want to give you back something you may have thrown away.

The prayers you could not put into words were still prayers. The longing you cannot name is not a confusion to be solved but a melody to be trusted. It knows where it is going even when you do not. And the wordless hum in your chest, the one that rises at sunrises and bedsides and in the dark before sleep, is not proof that you have failed to pray properly. It is the oldest and truest song you sing, older than any words you will ever find for it, and it is being heard, every note of it, by the One who has been singing back to you, wordlessly, this whole time.

You do not have to find the words tonight.

The tune is already enough. The One it is reaching for has understood it all along.
