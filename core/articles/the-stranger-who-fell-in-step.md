Some of the most important pastoring I have ever done happened in a truck, on a long drive, going nowhere in particular.

A man in my church was leaving. Not in a dramatic way, no scandal, no blowup, no letter. He was just quietly walking out the back of his own faith, the way most people actually leave, one disappointment at a time. Something he had believed with his whole chest had not come true. A prayer he had staked everything on had gone unanswered in the cruelest possible way, and the songs that used to lift him now sounded to him like a language he had stopped believing in. He was not angry. He was worse than angry. He was done. And when I found out, I did not schedule a meeting or prepare an argument. I asked him if he wanted to ride along with me on an errand two towns over, and he said yes, and for an hour and a half I mostly just let him talk while the road unrolled in front of us.

I did that because of a story I have read a hundred times, and it has quietly reshaped how I understand what worship and pastoring are even for.

It was the first evening after the resurrection, though nobody walking that road yet believed it. Two of Yeshua's followers were leaving Jerusalem, heading for a village called Emmaus, seven miles down the road. And they were devastated. Everything they had hoped for had died on a cross three days before. They had believed He was the one who would redeem everything, and now He was dead, and there were confused rumors flying around the city that no sensible person could make sense of, and they had simply had enough. They were walking away from the place where their hope had been buried. And the Scripture says they were talking about all of it as they went, turning it over, grieving it, trying to make the pieces fit and failing.

And then Yeshua Himself came near and fell in step beside them. Alive. The very one they were mourning, walking along the same road, close enough to touch. And here is the first thing that undoes me: they did not recognize Him. Their eyes were kept from it. The answer to every question breaking their hearts was walking right next to them in the dust, matching their pace, and they had no idea. They thought He was just some stranger, another traveler on the road who happened to be going the same way.

Now watch what the Stranger does, because it is the opposite of what I would have done, and I think the opposite of what most of the church would do.

He asks them a question. What are you discussing as you walk along, He says, and why are you so sad? He invites them to pour it all out. And they do. They stop, their faces downcast, and one of them, Cleopas, actually rebukes the Stranger a little, incredulous that anyone could have been in Jerusalem and not know what had happened. And then they tell Him the whole broken story. They tell Him, to His own face, that they had hoped He was the one to redeem Israel, not knowing they were saying it to the one they were talking about. They lay out their crushed expectations, their confusion, the rumors they cannot believe. And the Stranger lets them. He does not interrupt. He does not cut in with, actually, you have this all wrong. He walks, and He listens, and He lets two heartbroken people say the whole hard thing out loud before He says a word of His own.

He walked with them before He corrected them. He fell in step before He set them straight. And even when He did begin to speak, opening the Scriptures to them, showing them how all of it had to happen, how the suffering was never the end of the story. He did it walking beside them, at their pace, on the road they had chosen, heading in the direction they were already going. He met them on the way out and turned them around from inside the conversation, not by blocking the road but by walking it with them.

And they felt something before they understood anything. Later, after they finally recognized Him and He vanished from their sight, they said the thing to each other that I cannot stop thinking about. Did not our hearts burn within us while He talked with us on the road? The burning came before the recognition. Something in them responded to Him long before their eyes caught up to who He was. Their hearts knew before their minds did.

That is exactly what a song does, and it is why I trust music with the people I cannot reach any other way. A melody gets to the heart before the argument ever reaches the head. The man in my truck could not have received a single point of theology that afternoon; he had put up a wall against all of it. But a song does not knock on the front door where the wall is. It falls in step. It walks alongside the grief without demanding the grief explain itself first, and it makes something burn in the chest before the mind has decided whether to allow it. When we carry a song down a hard road to someone who has stopped believing the words, we are not there to correct them. We are there to fall in step. We are there to be the Stranger who lets them talk the whole seven miles and warms something in them that the argument could never reach.

And here is how the Emmaus road ends, because the ending is the whole invitation. When they get near the village, the Stranger acts as if He is going on farther. He does not force Himself on them. And they urge Him, stay with us, they say, because it is toward evening and the day is far spent. He goes in. And at the table, when He takes the bread and blesses it and breaks it, their eyes are finally opened and they recognize Him, and in that instant He is gone, and they are left with burning hearts and the whole story suddenly making sense in reverse. They had been walking with the risen Yeshua the entire time. He had been beside them the whole road out. They just could not see Him yet.

So let me say this to the one walking away right now, the one reading this on the far side of a disappointment that killed something you used to believe. You have decided you are on that road alone, heading out of the city with your faith in pieces and no one beside you who understands. You are wrong about the alone part. The Stranger has already fallen in step. He is not marching in front of you demanding you turn around; He is walking at your pace, on the very road you chose, letting you say the whole hard thing. And that ache you feel when a certain song comes on and something in your chest burns against your own will, that is not weakness. That is your heart recognizing Him before your eyes have caught up. He is closer than the grief has let you see.

Do not walk the rest of the way alone. When evening comes and the day is far spent, do what they did. Say it out loud: stay with me. He has been waiting the whole road for the invitation, and He will come in, and He will break the bread, and you will finally see who has been beside you all along.
