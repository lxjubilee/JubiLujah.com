There is a line on every mountain where the trees give up.

Climb high enough and you cross it, botanists call it the tree line, and above it the forest simply stops. The soil thins, the wind never rests, the cold comes hard, and the tall confident trees of the lower slopes cannot survive it. What grows up there instead is low and tough and slow: lichen on bare rock, moss in the cracks, small hardy things pressed close to the ground that have learned to live on almost nothing. Everything decorative has been stripped away. Only the essential can breathe at that altitude.

I have spent a lot of my life above the tree line, and I have come to believe there is a kind of worship that can only be learned up there. Not the worship of the green lower slopes, where things come easy and grow tall and fast. A higher, barer, more weathered kind, and most people never learn it, because most people, understandably, never go up that far. You do not arrive above the tree line by accident. You get there by climbing past the place where you wanted to stop.

## The mountain that subtracts

Notice what altitude does. It does not add to you. It takes away.

The higher you go, the less you can carry, the less grows, the less noise there is, the fewer people are with you, the thinner the very air becomes. Everything is subtracted. And this is precisely the opposite of how we are taught to grow spiritually. We think maturity means accumulation, more knowledge, more disciplines, more activity, more added to the pile until we are impressive. But the mountain teaches the reverse. The mountain matures you by subtraction. It takes things away from you until what is left is only what is actually load-bearing.

This is why the wilderness and the high place appear so relentlessly in the story of God's people, and why they are almost always places of loss before they are places of encounter. Yahuah led an entire nation not around the desert but straight through the middle of it, for forty years, feeding them one day's worth of bread at a time so that they could not stockpile, could not hoard, could not carry more than a single day of trust. He was doing to a whole people what altitude does to a climber. He was subtracting them down to dependence. And dependence, it turns out, is not the failure state of faith. It is the summit of it.

We resist this with everything in us. We want a faith that adds up, that accumulates evidence and security and margin, that gives us more to hold. And Yahuah keeps walking us above the tree line, where the things we were counting on cannot grow, because He is after a version of us that can worship on almost nothing, that has learned, like the moss in the rock, to live pressed close to the ground of His character with all the easy provisions stripped away.

## Worship in the cloud you cannot see through

There is a scene that has anchored my whole understanding of this. When Moses went up the mountain of God, the top was wrapped in a thick cloud, smoke and fire and darkness so dense the people at the bottom were terrified of it. And the account says something that should stop us: while everyone else stood back at a safe distance, Moses drew near to the thick darkness, because that was where God was.

Sit with that. The presence of Yahuah was not in the clear bright open where you could see everything. It was inside the cloud, in the place where visibility dropped to nothing. To meet God there, Moses had to walk *into* the part where he could no longer see, up above the tree line, into the weather, where sight fails and only trust can move your feet.

That is a picture of a worship most of us are never taught to want. We prize the worship of clear skies, the kind where we can see what God is doing and sing our understanding back to Him. But there is a higher worship offered in the cloud, when you have climbed past the last place things made visible sense, and you cannot see the outcome, cannot see the reason, cannot see one step ahead, and you draw near anyway, into the thick darkness, because you have decided that is where He is. That worship does not depend on the view. It has already surrendered the view. And it is the strongest, quietest, most unshakeable worship a human being can offer, because nothing external is holding it up. It has learned to breathe the thin air.

And here is the mercy in it: Moses did not come down from that cloud diminished. He came down with his face shining so brightly the people could not look straight at him. The place of least visibility became the place of most glory. What looked from below like a man disappearing into darkness was, up close, a man being flooded with light. The altitude that took everything away gave him the one thing the lower slopes never could.

## For the one climbing above the tree line

So let me speak plainly to whoever is up past their own tree line right now, someone in a season where things have only been taken away.

You have been losing, not gaining. The comforts thinned out, the crowd fell away, the easy answers stopped growing, and the very air of your faith feels harder to breathe than it used to. And you have read all of this as decline, as God withdrawing, as something going wrong.

I want to offer you the mountain's own reading of it. You are not in decline. You are at altitude. The stripping is not abandonment; it is the way the high places make you. Everything the tree line took from you was decorative, tall, fast, easy things that could never have survived where you are going anyway. What is being left in you is the low, tough, weathered, essential faith that can live on almost nothing and worship in the cloud. That faith is rarer and stronger than anything that grows on the comfortable lower slopes, and you could not have gotten it down there. It only grows this high, and it only grows by subtraction.

So do not turn back for the tree line. Draw near to the thick darkness, where you cannot see and cannot pretend, because that is exactly where He is. Sing the bare, weathered, essential song, the one with all the props removed, the one that trusts Him with the view already surrendered. That is the worship of the high places, and it is the strongest sound a person can make.

And when you finally come down, do not be surprised if the people who stayed on the lower slopes cannot quite look at your face. You will have been somewhere they have not, up past the trees, into the cloud, on almost nothing, close to God.
