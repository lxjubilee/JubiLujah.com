He asked to go, and he was told to stay. It is one of the only times in the Gospels that a man begs to follow Yeshua and is turned down, and understanding why is the whole point.

He lived on the wrong side of the lake, in the region of the Gerasenes, Gentile country, part of the sprawl of Greek cities they called the Decapolis, the Ten Cities, a place a devout Israelite crossed the water to reach and did not linger in. And this man was the thing that region was most afraid of. He was possessed by a legion of unclean spirits, and he lived among the tombs. Nobody could hold him. They had tried chains and shackles and he had snapped them like thread. Night and day, the record says, he was among the tombs and in the hills, crying out and cutting himself with stones.

Picture how the region had learned to think of him. He was the local horror. Mothers steered their children the long way around the graveyard. He had a reputation, a whole folklore of failed attempts to bind him. The cities had done the only thing broken cities know how to do with a person they cannot fix. They had written him off. They chained him when they could, and when the chains broke they left him to the tombs and told themselves nothing could be done. He was, to the Ten Cities, the definition of hopeless.

## An afternoon undid a lifetime

Then a boat came across the water, and Yeshua stepped out, and in the span of a single conversation the thing that a whole region's chains could not hold was gone.

I will not linger on the mechanics, the many spirits, the herd of pigs, the sea. What I want you to see is the after. When the townspeople come out to look, they find the man they had all given up on *sitting there, clothed and in his right mind.* Sitting still, for the first time in years. Dressed. Sane. Quiet. The horror of the region, undone in an afternoon, calm as a child at the feet of the man who had done it.

And here is the strange, revealing thing about that region: it frightened them. You would think a healing like that would empty the town into the streets with joy. Instead the record says they were afraid, and they begged Yeshua to leave. He unsettled them more whole than the man had ever unsettled them wild. That is worth sitting with, sometimes the places most used to a certain kind of brokenness are the most disturbed when it is actually healed, because a real deliverance asks the whole town to rethink everything it had settled. So they asked the Healer to get back in His boat and go.

And He does. But watch the freed man.

## The one request he was denied

As Yeshua is getting into the boat to leave, the man who was delivered begs to come with Him. And who could blame him? Everyone he has ever known has feared him. The region wants the Healer gone, and he does not want to be left behind with the people who chained him. He wants to go where the freedom is. He wants to follow.

And Yeshua tells him no.

It is one of the tenderest refusals in all of Scripture. He does not permit him. Instead He says: *go home to your own people, and tell them how much the Lord has done for you, and how He has had mercy on you.* Go home. Go back to the very cities that bound you. Go back to the tombs and the folklore and the mothers who steered their children away from you, and *tell them*. Be, in that hostile region where no apostle would set foot for a long time, the first voice of the mercy of God.

Understand what Yeshua just did. He took the man the Ten Cities had most given up on and made him the region's first missionary. He did not import a preacher from Galilee. He commissioned the local horror. The one person in the Decapolis with an unanswerable testimony, because everyone there had *seen* him at his worst, was sent home to walk the same streets he used to terrorize and say, with his own restored voice, *look what the Lord has done.*

And he does. The record says he went away and began to proclaim throughout the Decapolis how much Yeshua had done for him, and everyone marveled. He became the song of that region. He carried it up and down the Ten Cities on his own two feet, the walking evidence, and he did not stop.

## The witness that went ahead

Here is a detail that undoes me every time. Later, Yeshua comes back to that same Decapolis region, and this time, instead of a town begging Him to leave, there are crowds. Thousands gather. Something had changed in the country between the two visits.

The Scriptures do not connect the dots for us in so many words, and I will be honest about that. But it is hard not to notice that in the years between the first trip and the second, one man had been walking the Ten Cities, telling everyone who would listen how much the Lord had done for him. The region that once begged the Healer to leave now runs to meet Him. The ground had been prepared, not by a campaign, not by a delegation, but by one delivered man who was told to stay home and sing his testimony over the country that had chained him.

That is the whole reason the *no* was a mercy. If Yeshua had let him climb into the boat, the Decapolis would have lost its only voice. His deepest desire, to be near the Healer, would have cost his entire region the one witness who could reach it. So the man who begged to follow was, in fact, given the greater assignment: to stay on hostile ground and become its first worship leader, the opening voice of a gospel a whole region had never heard.

## Sent home to sing

I have carried worship into the guarded countries, Arabic, Turkish, Romanian, the tongues of places that have their own tombs and their own folklore of the hopeless. And this freed man is one of the people I keep close, because he answers the two hardest questions the frontier asks.

The first is: *am I too far gone to be used?* And the answer of the Ten Cities is that the person the region had most written off became the first voice it ever heard for God. There is no ruin so public that Yeshua cannot turn it into the most credible testimony in the whole country. The very people who knew you at your worst are the ones your restored voice will reach hardest.

The second question is quieter and harder: *why won't He take me somewhere better?* Because sometimes the great mercy is the assignment to stay. You beg to leave the hard place, to go where the worship is easy and the crowds already believe, and He says, gently, no; go home to your own people, and tell them how much the Lord has done for you. Your city does not need you to escape it. It needs you to walk its streets as living proof, singing the one song only you can sing there, until a region that begged Him to leave is running to meet Him at the shore.

You may be the whole worship of a place that chained you. Do not envy the boat. Go home and sing.
