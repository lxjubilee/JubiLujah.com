The first time I heard the *colinde* I was a long way from any barrio I knew, in a Romanian village where the snow came up over my boots and the cold got into the seams of my coat and stayed there. I had come to learn how these people worshiped, because I had been told their winter songs were among the oldest in the Christian world, and I wanted to hear them where they lived and not on a recording.

What I did not expect was that they would make me go outside to hear them.

I had assumed, coming from a warmer place, that a winter carol would be an indoor thing, that you would gather around a fire with something hot to drink and sing where it was comfortable. That is not how the *colindători* do it. When the light goes blue in the late afternoon they put on their heaviest coats, the ones that smell of woodsmoke and sheep, and they gather at the edge of the village. Someone carries a star made by hand, a great wooden star on a pole with a candle glowing inside it, and they walk. House to house, through snow that squeaks under the boots, breath fogging in front of every face, they walk out into the cold and they sing.

They do not wait for people to come to the church. They carry the song to the door. And at each house the door opens, and warmth and lantern light spill out into the freezing dark, and the people inside stand in the doorway and receive the news as though they had not heard it in a thousand years, though of course they hear it every winter of their lives.

I stood in that snow with my feet going numb and I understood something I had read a hundred times and never once felt.

## The shepherds could not keep it indoors

Think about how the news of Yeshua's birth actually broke, the first night. Yahuah did not announce it in the palace. He did not send it to the temple first, to the men with the credentials and the good chairs. He sent it to men who were outside in the cold at that very hour, out in the fields at night, keeping the sheep because someone has to and it is not going to be anyone who can afford not to.

The heavens opened over the *shepherds*. Over the ones on the night shift. And the sky filled with a song so enormous that the men fell down in terror before they could take in that it was good.

Here is the part I want you to hold. When the light closed up again and the field went dark and ordinary, the shepherds did not go home and keep the wonder to themselves. The account says they went with haste to find the child, and then, this is the line that undid me in that snow. They could not stop talking about it. They told everyone. They made known abroad what had been said to them about this child, until everyone who heard them was amazed. Men whose whole job was to stay put with the flock got up and became the first carolers in the history of the world, walking from person to person in the cold with news too big to keep inside.

That is exactly what the Romanian village was doing in front of me. They were not putting on a concert. They were being the shepherds. They were rehearsing, in the oldest way they knew, the truth that this news does not belong to the warm room. It belongs to the doorway. It belongs to the cold lane and the far house and the neighbor you have not spoken to since the argument last spring. You carry the star to them. You do not make them come find the light. You bring it, glowing on a pole, out into the dark.

## Worship that costs you the warm room

I have thought about those numb feet for years, and I have decided the cold was not incidental. It was the whole point.

It is easy to sing where it is comfortable. It costs you nothing to lift your hands in a heated building among people who already agree with you. But the *colinde* asks something of the body. It asks you to leave the fire and go out into the weather, to sing badly through chattering teeth, to walk to a door that might not open and knock anyway. The discomfort is not a flaw in the tradition. It is the tradition telling the truth about what the news is worth. You do not carry a small thing into the snow. You only walk out into the cold for something you cannot bear to keep to yourself.

I come from a people who worship loudly and in the street, who let the procession spill down the middle of the road and turn the whole barrio into a sanctuary with no walls. I felt the same Spirit in that frozen Romanian lane, wearing a heavier coat. Different weather, same refusal to let the praise stay indoors. My people carry it out into the heat and the dust; theirs carry it out into the snow and the dark; and in both places the song is saying the same thing to the closed door, *He is born, He is here, come and see, we could not keep it to ourselves.*

## Your song was never meant to stay in the building

Maybe you have made worship a warm-room thing. I understand the pull of it. The world outside is cold in more ways than the weather, and the gathered room is one of the few places you feel safe. Sing there. Be warmed there. God meant that room to hold you.

But do not let the door become the border of your praise. There is someone on your street tonight who has not heard the news in a way that reached them, someone whose door has not opened to real warmth in a long time, someone who would never in a thousand years walk into your building but who might stand in their own doorway if the song came to them. The shepherds did not wait to be qualified. They were the least qualified people in the story and they became the first evangelists in it, simply because they could not shut up about what they had seen.

Put on the heavy coat. Pick up the star. The news is too big for the warm room, and it always was.

Walk it out into the cold, mi gente. Somebody's door is about to open.
