The most senior worshippers in all of creation stood at the center of everything, close enough to touch the throne, and they would not lift their eyes.

The prophet saw it and never got over it. He was carried, in a vision, into the throne room of Yahuah, the King high and lifted up, the hem of His robe alone filling the whole temple, so that the mere edge of Him crowded out the room. And above Him stood the seraphim, the burning ones, each with six wings. Not one of those six was spare. Two carried them in flight. Two covered their feet. And two, this is the detail that should stop us in our tracks, two were spent covering their faces.

Think about who these creatures are. They are not beginners. They are not sinners in recovery, hiding their shame. They are flame itself, made to stand nearest the fire and not be consumed. They have never known a fallen day. If anyone in the universe had earned the right to a familiar glance, an easy nearness, a casual hello to the Holy One, it would be them. And they cover their faces. The oldest, purest, most fireproof worshippers there are will not look directly at Him, and they have been not-looking for longer than our world has spun.

And what were they doing with their voices while their faces were hidden? They were calling to one another the one word Scripture lets us hear them say, holy, holy, holy is Yahuah of hosts; the whole earth is full of His glory. Three times. Not because Hebrew had no other adjectives, but because in that tongue you say a thing twice to make it strongest, and here the strongest was not strong enough, so they went past it. Holy raised to a degree the language cannot otherwise reach. And at the sound of that voice the doorposts shook and the whole house filled with smoke, as if the building itself could not hold the weight of what was being said about the One inside it.

I want to sit us down in front of this, gently but without flinching, because we have drifted a long way from it.

We have learned to be casual with God. Not cruelly, most of us love Him, but casually. We have made Him a warm companion, a helpful presence, a name we drop into songs the way you drop sugar into coffee, to make the hour go down easier. We arrive at worship the way we arrive at a favorite playlist: expecting to feel something, hoping to be moved, ready to leave when the moving is done. And there is real tenderness in that nearness; He did make us His children, and He does draw close. But somewhere in the drawing close we quietly stopped trembling, and a worship with no tremble left in it is not more mature. It is only more forgetful.

Because here is the thing the smoke-filled temple insists on: the seraphim are not afraid of Him the way you are afraid of a threat. They are not cowering. They are the freest, most joyful beings in existence, and they cover their faces anyway, not out of terror but out of truth. They are simply telling the truth about the difference between what He is and what they are. The covered face is not fear of punishment. It is accuracy. It is the only honest posture in front of a holiness that large, and honesty, in that room, looks like awe.

Now watch what the prophet does when he stumbles into the middle of it. He does not raise his hands and sway. He comes apart. Woe is me, he cries. I am undone, a man of unclean lips, and my eyes have seen the King. The nearness does not soothe him first. It exposes him first. He sees the Holy One and instantly sees himself, and the sight is unbearable, not because Yahuah is unkind, but because light that pure shows you every mark. That is what real reverence does before it does anything else. It tells you the truth about the gap.

And this is exactly where the casual worshipper misses the greatest thing, because he never lets himself get near enough to be undone, and so he never gets to what happens next.

What happens next is that one of the burning ones flies to the altar, takes a live coal with tongs, even a seraph does not handle that fire barehanded, and presses it to the prophet's mouth. Your guilt is taken away, he says. Your sin is atoned for. The very awe that broke the man open becomes the doorway through which he is made clean. He did not clean himself up to earn the vision. The vision cleaned him. The trembling was not the end of the encounter. It was the beginning of the healing.

And only then, only after the woe, and the coal, and the mending, does he hear the other Voice. Whom shall I send? And who will go for us? And the man who moments ago was undone on the floor stands up with the answer that has echoed through every sent life since: here am I. Send me.

Do you see the whole shape of it? The awe came first. The exposure came first. The trembling came first. And out the far side of the trembling came the cleanest mouth in the room and the boldest yes in the story. The reverence did not shrink him. It commissioned him. A man too casual to be undone would have left that temple the same as he came. The one who let the glory break him left it as a prophet.

This is what I am pleading with you not to trade away. Not your comfort. He is more tender than you fear. But your tremble. That instinct that says the ground here is different, that this Name is not like other names, that the One we sing to could fill this room with smoke if He drew back the edge of His robe. That instinct is not immaturity to be outgrown. It is the seraphim's own posture leaking into yours, and it is the surest sign your worship has actually arrived somewhere real.

So the next time you open your mouth to sing to Yahuah, let the burning ones stand behind you in your mind. Let their covered faces correct the casual tilt of your own. Do not sing to Him as though He were small enough to be your equal, your mascot, your mood. Sing to Him as what He is, so holy the flames avert their eyes, so glorious the whole earth is only just barely able to hold the rumor of Him.

And when the weight of that lands on you and you feel, for a moment, undone, do not run from it. That is the coal on its way to your mouth. That is the fear of Yahuah doing the one thing casual worship can never do: making you clean enough, and free enough, and awake enough, to finally say send me and mean it.
