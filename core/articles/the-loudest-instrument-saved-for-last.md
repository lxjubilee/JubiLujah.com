If you have ever sat in an orchestra, you know the loneliest, most electric job in the room belongs to the cymbal player. For long stretches they do nothing. They stand there, holding two heavy discs of bronze, counting rests, waiting through whole movements, because the cymbal crash is not for the middle of things. It is for the summit. The composer saves it for the moment the entire piece has been climbing toward, the peak where everything breaks open at once, and the whole hall feels it in the chest.

The book of Psalms was composed by exactly that instinct.

A hundred and fifty songs. Every human emotion in front of God. And on the very last line of the very last psalm, the collection finally reaches for its loudest instrument, the cymbals, and lets them crash.

## The two kinds of clash

The Hebrew here is worth slowing down for, because the psalm does not just say "cymbals" once. It names them twice, with two different phrases, and both are gorgeous.

First it calls for the *tziltzelei shama*, cymbals of *sounding,* of clear ringing, the bright resonant tone you can pick out across a crowd. Then, immediately, it escalates: the *tziltzelei teruah*, cymbals of *teruah.*

And *teruah* is one of the great words of the Hebrew Bible. It is not just "loud." It is the *shout*, the blast of the war-trumpet, the roar a whole army makes going forward, the eruption of a crowd when the king appears, the great cry of joy that goes up when something long-awaited finally arrives. *Teruah* is the sound of a people who cannot contain it anymore. And the psalm ends by asking for cymbals pitched to *that*, not polite cymbals, not tasteful cymbals, but cymbals of the shout, the loudest resonant crash the ancient band could produce, wheeled out for the last line and struck.

> The songbook does not fade out. It does not end on a whisper. It ends on a crash so loud it has its own name for the shouting.

The word itself even sounds like what it means. *Tziltzelim*. You can almost hear the ringing in it, the shimmer of struck bronze. The ancients named the instrument after the very quiver of sound it makes.

## Why the loud thing comes last

Now think about the *placement,* because that is where the wonder lives.

The composer of the Psalter had every option. He could have ended on the tender lyre, the instrument that soothed King David's tormented nights, a soft, intimate, reverent close. He could have ended on the low pipe, breathy and quiet, fading into stillness. Those would have made a "spiritual" ending by our usual instincts, because we tend to file *quiet* under holy and *loud* under fleshly.

He ended on the cymbals. On purpose. He held the loudest thing in reserve through the whole book and unleashed it on the final line.

And that tells me something the church badly needs to hear: **there is a holiness that is loud.** We have a deep and often unexamined bias that reverence means volume down, that the truest worship is hushed, restrained, kept quiet out of respect. And there is a real reverence that is quiet; I would never take the whisper from you. But the Psalms will not let quiet be the *only* holy sound. They end. They climax, they land the whole book's final note, on holy *noise.* On resonance let all the way loose. On the crash of the shout.

Because some things that are true about God cannot be said quietly. There is a joy that would be *lying* if it stayed soft. When a debt is cancelled, when a grave comes up empty, when a people who were nobody are suddenly somebody, the honest sound is not a murmur. It is cymbals of *teruah.* The songbook ends loud because the story it is telling finally becomes too good to keep the volume down.

## What the crash actually is

And here is the piece that turns the whole thing luminous for me, because of what a cymbal physically *does.*

A struck cymbal is almost pure resonance. Where a plucked string gives you a fairly clean note, the cymbal erupts into a dense, shimmering cloud of many frequencies all vibrating at once, and, this is the part I love. It *keeps ringing.* The bronze goes on vibrating long after the strike, the sound blooming and hanging and slowly decaying in the air, filling the whole room with a sustained wash of vibration you feel as much as hear. You do not just hear a cymbal. Your body is briefly inside it.

That is what the Psalms chose to end on. Not a single clean point of sound, but a crash that floods the entire space with resonance and lingers. The last physical event of the whole songbook is a room full of vibration that will not immediately die away, a sound that outlasts the striking of it, still trembling in the air after the hand has fallen still.

I cannot help hearing a promise in that. The book ends, but the resonance does not. The final *hallelujah* is struck like a cymbal and left ringing, and in some real sense it has never stopped, the vibration is still hanging in the air of every room where people gather to make that same joyful noise. You are not starting a new sound when you worship loud. You are adding your strike to a resonance that the last line of the Psalms set going and never let fully fade.

## Permission to make holy noise

So let me hand you what the cymbal player knows.

The crash is not irreverence. The crash was *scored.* It was written into the plan by a composer who could have chosen the quiet instrument and deliberately chose the loud one for the most important moment in the book. Your loud, unguarded, undignified joy in the presence of God is not a lapse in reverence to be apologized for. It may be the most faithful reading of how the songbook actually ends.

There is a time to be quiet before Him, and the Psalms know it well. But do not let anyone tell you that quiet is the only holy volume, because the very last thing the book of Psalms does is reach past every gentle instrument, take up the loudest one in the room, name it after the shout, and strike it, and leave the whole world ringing.

So when the joy comes that is too big to keep soft, do not shrink it. Strike the cymbal. Make the holy noise. You are only doing what the final line of the songbook has been waiting a hundred and fifty songs for someone to do, letting the loudest, gladdest, most resonant sound you have crash all the way open, and hang there, trembling, in the air of a room that God has never once asked you to keep quiet.
