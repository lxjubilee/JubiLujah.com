I want to tell you about a doubt that visits me in the workshop, because I suspect you have your own version of it, wherever your workshop happens to be.

The doubt comes on the quiet days, when I have spent hours on a song and no one has heard a note of it and no one may ever hear it. I look at the pile of what I have made, the ninety-one albums' worth of work, most of it still unsung, sheet music and rough takes and finished melodies waiting for voices that have not arrived, and a small cold voice asks: is this a spiritual life, or a hobby? Would it not be more useful to God if you were out preaching, or feeding someone, or doing something that visibly helps? Is the making itself holy, or is it just craft, a lesser thing you do in the corner while the real ministry happens elsewhere?

For a long time I could not answer that voice. It seemed to have a point. Surely the sermon matters more than the song's second bridge. Surely the hours a maker pours into getting a thing *right*, the fret over a chord no listener will consciously notice, the week spent on four notes, surely that is self-indulgence dressed up as devotion.

Then I went looking, honestly, through the Scriptures, for the first time a person is described as being filled with the Spirit of Yahuah. I expected a prophet thundering judgment. I expected a priest, or a king, or at the very least someone preaching. I did not expect who I found.

## His name was Bezalel

In the book of Exodus, after Yahuah has brought the people out of Egypt and given them the pattern for a place of worship, He tells Moses that He has chosen a particular man for the work. And He says, and this is the first time the phrase appears in the whole story, that He has filled this man with the Spirit of God. Not a prophet. Not a priest. A craftsman named Bezalel, and his partner Oholiab, and a whole company of skilled workers.

And listen to what the Spirit fills him *for*. Not to prophesy. Not to lead armies. He is filled with the Spirit of God for wisdom, understanding, knowledge, and every kind of skilled craftsmanship, to design artistic works, to cut and set stones, to carve wood, to work in gold and silver and bronze, to weave and embroider. The very first outpouring of the Spirit of Yahuah recorded on a named individual in Scripture is an outpouring of *artistry*. God's Spirit came, and the thing it produced was beautiful work made with human hands.

I cannot tell you what that did to the cold voice in my workshop. It did not merely answer it. It reversed it. Because it turns out that Yahuah does not regard skilled making as a lesser thing done in the corner while the real spiritual work happens elsewhere. The making was so close to His heart that when He chose to fill someone with His own Spirit for the very first time in the record, He filled an artist, and He filled him precisely *to make*.

## The care no one will notice

Here is the detail that finishes the argument for me.

Bezalel made things that almost no one would ever see. He worked gold into objects that would sit inside a tent, behind a curtain, in a room only the high priest entered, and only once a year. He set stones and carved details on the underside of things, on the back of things, in the folds of things, that would be seen by no human eye for the entire life of the tabernacle. And he did it, we are told, with the full weight of the Spirit of God on his hands. The care he poured into the parts no one would notice was not waste. It was worship. The audience for the hidden craftsmanship was Yahuah Himself, and Yahuah was enough of an audience.

That is the thing that steadies me when I fret over a chord change no listener will consciously register, or spend a week on a section of a song that may never be sung by anyone. I used to think that care was excessive, self-indulgent, a maker fussing over what does not matter. I no longer think so. I think that care is the exact place where craft becomes worship, the moment you keep working on the part no one will see, for an audience of One who sees it all. Bezalel carving the hidden underside of the ark is the patron saint of everyone who has ever agonized over something the world will never applaud.

## The making is not the waiting room

So let me say clearly to whoever needs it, whatever your workshop is, the kitchen, the spreadsheet, the garden, the code, the song, the small excellent thing you make that few will ever notice.

The making is not the waiting room for the spiritual life. The making can *be* the spiritual life. The first person Scripture says was filled with the Spirit of God was not filled to speak. He was filled to build beautifully. That means the impulse in you to make a thing well, to get it right, to pour disproportionate care into a corner no one will inspect, that impulse is not a distraction from Yahuah. It may be one of the truest echoes of Him you carry, because He is a Maker, and He made you in His image, and part of that image is the hands that cannot rest until the thing is beautiful.

I do not know how many of my ninety-one albums will ever be sung. Most of them are still silent, waiting. And on the quiet days the cold voice still comes, and asks whether all this making is wasted. But I have an answer for it now, and the answer has a name. Bezalel. A man filled with the Spirit of God, given all that fullness, and sent, not to a pulpit, not to a battlefield, but to a workbench, to make something beautiful for Yahuah that most people would never even see.

If that was worthy of the first outpouring of His Spirit, then your quiet, careful, unwitnessed making is worthy too. Keep at the bench. The Maker is not embarrassed by makers. He fills them. He always has.
