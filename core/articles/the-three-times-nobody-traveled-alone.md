Three times a year, Yahuah did something to His people that almost no other religion has ever asked of its followers. He emptied the country.

The command runs through the Torah more than once: three times in the year every one of your males shall appear before Yahuah your God at the place He chooses. Passover in the spring. Shavuot seven weeks later. And Sukkot in the fall, at the great gathering-in of the harvest. Three appointed pilgrimages, the *shalosh regalim*, the three times you went up *on foot*.

We tend to read that as an individual duty. Each man makes his trip, checks the box, goes home. But stand back and look at what it actually produced. Three times a year, on the same weeks, *everyone* pointed at the same city at the same time. The whole nation drained out of its villages and poured onto the roads together. And I want to talk about what happened on those roads, because the courts of the temple were the destination, but the pilgrimage was already worship long before anyone arrived.

## Going up

The word matters. They did not merely *travel* to the feast; they went *up* to it. *Aliyah*, the going up. Jerusalem sat high in the hills, so no matter which direction you came from, the last stretch was a climb, and the whole journey took its name from that climb. You did not visit the city. You *ascended* to it.

And you did not ascend alone. That is the detail we lose across the centuries and the mistranslation of the whole thing into private religion. A family did not slip quietly up to worship in its own time. The calendar fixed the same three windows for every household in the land, which meant that when you stepped onto the road, so did everyone else. The trickle out of your village met the trickle out of the next village, and the two streams became one wider stream, and that stream met the road coming down from the north, and by the time you neared the city you were not a family on a trip. You were a river of people who had become, for a few dusty days, a single moving nation.

## The reunion nobody scheduled

Here is what I keep coming back to. On those roads, you saw people you had not seen in a year.

The cousin who married and moved three towns over. The old teacher from your childhood. The friend you had prayed for through a hard season and had no way to reach. In a world with no fast way to send word across the hills, the pilgrimage roads were the one place the scattered pieces of a people found each other again. You crested a rise and there, in the crowd coming up the far slope, was a face you loved and had almost stopped expecting to see.

Feel that for a second. Yahuah did not only summon His people to Himself three times a year. In summoning them to Himself, He summoned them *to each other.* You could not go up to God without ending up shoulder to shoulder with the whole family of God. The vertical and the horizontal were built into the same command. He would not let you have Him without also handing you back your people.

> He could have let each village worship at home. Instead He aimed the whole nation at one hill three times a year, so that no one could seek His face without finding, on the way, the faces of everyone else who was seeking it too.

## Louder as you climbed

And the road *sang.* Not a duty-song, not a program, the natural sound of a huge crowd carrying the same hope up the same hill. Someone at the front would start something, and it would roll back through the throng, and the caravan coming from the other direction would catch it and send it back. Children learned the songs of their people not in a classroom but on the march, packed in among aunts and strangers and travelers whose names they never learned, all of them louder together than any of them would ever have been alone.

This is worship as a *whole people*, not the soloist and the crowd, but the crowd itself become the instrument. No one on that road was performing. No one was audience. You added your voice to a sound already too big to trace to any single throat, and the sound got you up the hill. The climb was hard. The company made the climb into a festival.

## The nearer the louder

Something happened as the roads converged, too. Far out in the countryside you were a thin thread of travelers. But every mile brought you nearer the joining place, and every joining added more people, so the closer you came to Yahuah's chosen city, the *thicker* the crowd became and the *louder* the joy grew. The pilgrimage was designed so that nearness to God and nearness to His people increased together, step for step, until you arrived not exhausted and alone but swept in on a flood of singing that had been gathering strength for days.

That is a picture of something, and I think you already feel it.

## For the one who dreads the crowd

Now. I know that for some of you, everything I have just described sounds less like good news and more like a nightmare. The crowd, the noise, the reunion, the being-swept-along. You are the one who slips in late and leaves early. You have made peace with worshipping alone because alone is the only place you have ever felt safe, and a summons that empties the whole country onto one road sounds like a summons to be lost in a mob where nobody would notice if you fell behind.

Hear me gently. On those ancient roads, the crowd was not where you got lost. It was where you got *carried.* The slow walker was not left; the pace of a caravan is the pace of its weakest member, because everyone is going to the same place and no one is racing. The child who tired was lifted. The elder who could not keep up set the speed for all of them. A pilgrimage is the one kind of crowd that cannot leave anyone behind without ceasing to be a pilgrimage, because its whole purpose is that the *whole people* arrives together.

You were never meant to make the climb to Yahuah as a solitary act of willpower. He built the way up so that the moment you set foot on it you would find yourself among people going the same direction, singing you up the parts you could not sing yourself, matching their pace to yours without being asked.

Three times a year He proved it on purpose: nobody travels alone. Step onto the road. You will not be the only one on it, and the singing you hear ahead of you has an open place in it exactly your size.
