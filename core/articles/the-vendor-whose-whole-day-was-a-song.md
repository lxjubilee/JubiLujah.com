Before I knew there was such a thing as a worship set, before I could have told you what a bridge was or why a key change lifts you off your feet, I knew the tamale man.

He came up our street every morning while the light was still gray and thin, pushing a cart that squeaked on one wheel, and he sang. Not a song exactly. One long word, stretched and bent until it filled the whole block, *tamaaaaales*, rising at the front, falling soft at the end, a little cracked from the cold. My abuela knew his cry the way you know a voice on the phone before anyone says a name. She would already be reaching for coins before he came into view.

There was the knife-sharpener too, who did not sing at all but played a little wooden flute, five falling notes, the same five every time, and everyone on the street knew what those five notes meant: bring me your dull blades. There was the woman with the basket of *pan dulce* balanced on her head, calling in a voice that could carry three streets. There was the man who bought old iron, and the man who sold brooms, and each of them had a melody that belonged to no one but them. You could stand in our kitchen with your eyes closed and know exactly who was passing, and how far away, and whether they were coming or going.

I thought this was just noise. The soundtrack of being poor. It took me a long time, thirty years and a great deal of travel and study, to understand that the whole street was singing, and that it was teaching me something the sanctuary never quite got around to saying out loud.

## The song was the invitation

Here is what I finally saw. The vendor did not sing because he had leisure. He sang because he had bread to sell and mouths to feed at home, and the song was how the bread found the mouths. The melody was not decoration on the labor. The melody *was* the labor. It went ahead of him into houses he would never enter, up stairs he would never climb, and it said, in a form no closed door could keep out: *there is something here for you, come and get it while it is warm.*

That is not far at all from how the Scriptures describe the voice of God.

We tend to picture Wisdom as a quiet thing, a scholar in a still room. But the Proverbs put her somewhere else entirely. They say she does not whisper in the seminary. She stands at the busiest corner of the city, at the head of the noisy streets, in the gates where the traffic is thickest, and she *raises her voice*. She calls out over the haggling and the cart wheels and the arguments. She cries aloud in the one place respectable people assume God would never lower Himself to shout, the marketplace, the barrio, the ordinary grinding street where people are just trying to make it to the end of the day.

I grew up thinking the holy voice was the hushed one. The one you had to go inside and be quiet to hear. And there is a real and precious quiet with God, I will not take that from you. But the tamale man taught me the other half. Sometimes the voice of God is not the one that waits for you to come and find it. Sometimes it is the one that comes up your street before you are even awake, cracked from the cold, stretched out over one long word, refusing to let a wall or a door or your poverty keep it from you.

## He sold to everyone the same

I noticed something else as a boy, though I did not have language for it then. The vendor's cry did not change based on who lived in the house. He sang the same word up at the fine painted door and the leaning tin one. He did not lower his price for being liked or raise his voice only for the customers who could pay the most. The song went out over the whole street, indiscriminate, generous, the same warm offer to every window.

The prophets caught this and would not let it go. There is a passage where the invitation is thrown wide open. Everyone who is thirsty, come to the water; you who have no money, come, buy and eat without money and without price. That is not the pitch of a merchant protecting his margin. That is a vendor who wants his cart empty by noon not because he was paid but because everyone got fed. That is the strangest economy in the world, and it is the economy of Yahuah. He is out in the street at dawn calling the whole barrio to a table where the poorest can eat first.

We forget this in our buildings. We build the worship for the ones already inside, already fed, already able to read the bulletin and follow the changes. And the song God keeps singing is out on the corner, aimed at the window that never comes in, cupping its hands and calling one more time toward the people the market forgot.

## Your ordinary labor is already a melody

Here is where I want to land it, because I think many of you reading this are tired, and are working jobs that feel about as holy as a squeaky cart.

You have believed a lie that the song and the labor are two different things, that worship is the part where you finally stop working and lift your hands, and the rest of the day is just survival. The vendor never believed that. For him the cry and the work were one motion. The song was how the work reached people. The work was what gave the song something to carry.

I think Yeshua worked a trade with sawdust in His hair for most of His years, and I do not believe He stopped worshiping the Father when He picked up a tool. I think the swing of the hammer had a rhythm and the rhythm had a prayer in it, the same way the drum was praying in me before I had the words. The sacred was not waiting for Him at the synagogue on the seventh day only. It was in the grain of the wood on the second day too.

So sing your street. Whatever your cart is, the cash register, the classroom, the bus route, the sink full of dishes at midnight. There is a melody the Father is asking you to carry up it, an invitation your ordinary passing can push under doors you will never open. You do not have to be inside a sanctuary for your voice to be holy. The tamale man never was. He just came up the street at dawn, cracked and generous and unashamed, and made a whole barrio hungry for something warm.

That was worship. I have been trying to learn his song ever since.

Come and get it, mi gente. It is still warm.
