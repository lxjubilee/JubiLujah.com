The first thing you should know about where I come from is that the worship never once fit inside the building.

We had a room. A small one, with plastic chairs and a fan that lost the argument with the heat by ten in the morning. And every week the singing would start in there, polite enough, and then something would happen that I have watched my whole life and still cannot fully explain. Somebody near the door would prop it open for air. And the sound would find the gap. And once the sound found the gap it was gone, out the door, into the street, past the fruit stand where Doña Marta stacked her mangoes, past the mechanic wiping his hands on a rag, out into the whole barrio, which had never agreed to be quiet in the first place.

We called it *la calle canta*. The street sings. And I do not mean it as a poem. I mean that on the right day, in the right season, the actual road, the asphalt and the potholes and the tangle of wires overhead, became the sanctuary, and it had no walls, and that was the point.

I want to tell you why a faith like that will not stay indoors, because I think somewhere along the way a lot of good people were taught that worship is something you do in a room and then carefully carry home in your pocket, and I think that idea has made them smaller than God ever intended them to be.

Here is what the procession knows that the pocket does not.

## Praise was always meant to take up space

When you read the old accounts honestly, the worship in them is almost never a private, indoor thing. It is loud, and it is outside, and it takes up an embarrassing amount of room.

There is the day the ark of God came home to the city, the sacred thing carried back through the streets after years away, and the king himself went out in front of it. Not seated on a throne watching a program. Out in front, in the road, dancing before Yahuah with everything he had, whirling and leaping down the public street while the whole house of Israel came up behind him shouting, and the horns blowing, and the whole city turning out to see it pass. His own wife watched from a window and was embarrassed for him. And he told her plainly that he would be more undignified than this, that he did not care who saw. The procession did not stay indoors. It went down the middle of the road, and the king led it there.

Go back further and you find the same thing at the edge of the sea, when a company of freed slaves came up out of the water alive and free, and the very first thing they built was not a shelter. It was a song. Miriam took a tambourine in her hand and the women came out after her with tambourines and dancing, and the whole desert, the wide, open, roofless desert, heard a sound it had never heard, because there was no building anywhere for miles and the praise did not wait for one.

This is the tradition I was born into without knowing it had a pedigree that old. In the barrio we did not invent the procession. We inherited it. When we carried the song out the door and down the block, we were doing the oldest thing the people of God have ever done, refusing to let the praise be smaller than the God it was aimed at.

## The street needs to hear it more than the choir does

There is a reason it matters that the worship goes outside, and it is not about volume.

Inside the room, everyone already agrees. That is a beautiful thing and I would never take it from you, the gathered people, the shared song, the roof over the family. But the mechanic wiping his hands on the rag did not come to the room. Doña Marta is minding her mangoes. The old man who has not darkened a church door in thirty years is on his step, and he is not going to walk in.

But the procession walks *past* him.

I have watched it a hundred times, and it undoes me every time. The song comes down the street, and the man on the step who would never have come inside, something in his face changes. He does not stand up. He does not raise a hand. But he goes quiet, and he watches it pass, and I have seen his lips move. Because the worship came to *him*. It did not wait behind a door for him to find the courage to open it. It came out into his ordinary afternoon, into the exact street where he actually lives, and it sang the great deeds of God right there in front of his fruit crates and his broken-down car.

That is the whole heart of the thing. Yeshua did not set up a booth and wait for the crowds to file in and take a number. He went *out*, into the streets, the roads, the hillsides, the shoreline where the fishermen were already working. He met people in the middle of their Tuesday. The Gospel has feet. It was always meant to move.

## Your ordinary road is holier than you think

So here is what I want to leave with you, whether you were raised in a barrio or a suburb or a place where nobody sings in the road anymore.

The distance you have drawn between the sacred room and the ordinary street is a distance God does not recognize. He was never content to be worshiped only under a roof, by people who already agreed, behind a door propped open just enough for air. He is the God who went out in front of the ark and danced down the public road. He is the God who filled a roofless desert with tambourines. He is the God who walked the dusty streets of small towns and called it His Father's business.

And your street, the real one, the one with the traffic and the neighbor you avoid and the shop on the corner, is not a lesser place. It is the exact place the procession is trying to reach. The worship you keep so carefully indoors was never meant to live there full-time. It was meant to find the gap in the door, and go.

So let it out. Hum it on the walk to the bus. Sing it under your breath at the market. Carry the joy down the middle of your ordinary road where Doña Marta and the mechanic and the old man on the step can see it pass. You do not need a permit and you do not need a stage. You need only to stop believing that praise belongs inside.

*La calle canta.* The street is waiting to sing. It has been waiting for someone to prop the door open and let the song find the gap. Let it be you. And watch what happens to the faces along the road when the worship of God finally comes out to where the people actually live.
