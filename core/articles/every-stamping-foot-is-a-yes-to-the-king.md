You have to understand what the dabke is before you understand why I made an album of it for the King.

It is the line dance of my people, of the Levant, of the wedding and the harvest and the night the whole village pours into the courtyard. You link your arm through the arm of the person beside you, and the person beside them, until the line is a living rope, and then you stamp. Hard. In unison. Until the stone answers back and the dust rises and the drum finds the same heartbeat you are all sharing. It is not a performance you watch. It is a belonging you join. And where I come from, it is how we say the biggest yes a body can say: joy that will not sit down.

*Dabke for the King* is that yes, turned toward **Yeshua**. The opening song sets the whole record's rule in a single verse: *link your arm in mine and lift your feet to the floor, the lanterns are swinging and the door is open wide, because Yasu' is the reason and the joy will not be denied.*

## Why a prophet made a dance record

People expect an evangelist and a prophet to make solemn things. Warnings. Weights. And there is a place for those. I have made them. But I became convinced that one of the truest prophetic acts left to us is to dance on purpose, in the open, for the right King.

The album stands on a single scene from Scripture that I cannot get past: David bringing the ark into the city, dancing before the Lord with all his might, and not caring who thought it was beneath a king (2 Samuel 6). His own wife despised him for it. He answered that he would become even more undignified than this. That is the theology of this record in one man's stamping feet. The bridge of the opening song borrows his nerve directly: *David danced before the ark with all of his might. I will dance before my Savior in the lantern light; not for the watching crowd, not for a name to bring, every stamping foot is a Yes to Yasu' the King.*

Not for the watching crowd. That line is the hinge. The dabke is public, but this dance is not for the public. It is for an audience of One, danced in front of everyone.

## The Bridegroom the party was always for

A dabke, most of all, is a wedding dance. And that is not a coincidence I had to force onto the gospel. It is already there, waiting.

The Scriptures end at a wedding. The marriage supper of the Lamb, the feast prepared, the Bride ready and the Bridegroom come (Revelation 19). Every village wedding I have ever stamped my feet at was a small rehearsal for that day without knowing it. So the second song, "Come, My Yasu'," is written as the whole line calling for the Bridegroom to arrive, the Bridegroom who still carries His wounds, because the joy of this feast was bought with them. When I sing "Turn My Heart to You," I am back with David again, joy in motion, gladness the Spirit gives that your feet cannot contain.

> Yasu' is here so let the courtyard ring, every step we stamp is a word of adoration, for the One who turned our mourning into dancing.

That last phrase is not decoration. My region knows mourning. It has mourned more than most. And the promise I am dancing on is that the One who was wounded is the same One who turns mourning into dancing. So we stamp the stone not because there has been no grief, but because grief does not get the last step.

## The curtain that let us in

Halfway through the record the dancing goes suddenly quiet and then breaks open wider than before, in a song called "The Curtain Torn."

Here is what I needed my people to feel in their feet, not just hear in their heads. When Yeshua died, the great curtain of the temple, the veil that kept everyone but one man out of the holiest room, was torn from top to bottom, by no human hand (the Gospels record it; Hebrews 10 explains it). Top to bottom. Torn from God's side down, not ours. The way in was thrown open. The chorus sings, *the curtain's torn because of You, from top to bottom You opened up the door; You carry me into the inner courtyard, I come with boldness through the blood of the Son.*

Do you see why that belongs on a dance record? The dabke needs an open door and a wide courtyard, and the cross made the widest door there has ever been. We are not stamping our way in. We are dancing because we are already in. That is the difference between the striving of religion and the gladness of the redeemed, one is trying to earn the room, the other is already dancing in it.

## Breath, embrace, and the song that will not end

Two more songs hold the heart of this record for me.

"Every Breath Your Name" slows the whole line to the pace of the ney, the reed flute, which is nothing but a hollow tube until breath moves through it. The chorus makes the reed a picture of us: *the lung is the ney and You are the wind; You are the One who gave the breath to Adam, You breathed upon Your own, so breathe on me again.* From the first breath God breathed into the man of dust (Genesis 2) to the day the risen Yeshua breathed on His friends and said receive the Spirit (John 20), it is the same wind. Every breath you have ever taken has His name hidden in it. This song just makes the hidden thing loud.

And "In Your Embrace" is the softest track I have ever put on a dance album, because a wedding is not only stamping. It is also the moment the exhausted, welcomed one finally rests. *I am resting in Your embrace, not as a stranger at the threshold but a child; Your love is the courtyard room I live in.* That is the prodigal home, the head on the Father's chest, the party thrown for the one who came back.

The record ends with "The Song That Never Ends," and its melody quietly returns to the very first track, so the dance loops and the celebration refuses to close, the way heaven's song of the Lamb will refuse to close.

I made this for anyone who was ever told that joy is unspiritual, that reverence must be still, that their culture's gladness had no place at the feet of Christ. Come and stamp the stone. Link your arm through mine. There is a King in the courtyard, the curtain is torn, the feast is spread, and every stamping foot is a yes He is glad to hear.
