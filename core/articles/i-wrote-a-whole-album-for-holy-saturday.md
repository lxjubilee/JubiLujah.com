## The Loud One Made a Quiet Record

I am the psalmist of the praise break. I am the one who turns waiting rooms into worship rooms and ordinary Tuesdays into celebrations. So when I told people I was making an album that spends its first three songs in a minor key and does not let a single trumpet play until the very last track, some of them thought I had lost my nerve.

I had not lost my nerve. I had finally found my honesty.

Because here is a thing the loud church does not always admit: roughly a third of the Psalms are laments. The songbook God authored is full of grief that never once loses its address to Him. And most of us live a great deal of our lives not on Friday, where the sacrifice is clear, and not on Sunday, where the tomb is empty, but on *Saturday*. The day in between. The day when the cross is over and the resurrection has not arrived yet and you are just sitting in the silence, holding a question no one can answer.

So I wrote a whole album for Holy Saturday.

## Holding the Question Open

The record opens with the oldest honest prayer in the world: *how long, O Lord?* It is Psalm 13, verse one, the question David asked and did not get punished for asking. I set it at two-fourteen in the morning, an unanswered text glowing on a screen, a worn Bible page, an empty chair at the breakfast table.

I did not resolve it quickly, and I want to tell you why. If I rush you to the answer, I am not comforting you. I am dismissing you.

> He is in the "how long." He hears the unwhispered song.

That is the theology holding up the whole first act. **Yahuah** is not embarrassed by your question. He is *inside* it. The second song leans on the hardest verse in the Bible, Psalm 22, verse one, *my God, my God, why have You forsaken Me?*, and reminds you that **Yeshua** Himself sang that exact line from the cross. He did not float above the abandonment. He prayed it. Which means when you pray it, you are not falling out of faith. You are standing in the footprints of the Son of God.

## Why the Brass Waits

The most deliberate decision on this record is one you feel before you can name it: the instruments are rationed.

There is no Hammond organ, no brass, no arena drums for the first five songs. The cello carries the weight alone. The Hammond does not arrive until the sixth track, on the exact line where the album turns, *yet I will trust*, borrowed from Habakkuk, who looked at the fig tree that would not blossom and the empty stall and chose praise anyway, and from Job, who said *though He slay me, yet will I trust Him.* That is not triumph. That is gritted teeth. So the first warm sound on the whole album is the sound of trust decided in the dark, not felt in the light.

And the brass, the full, blazing, celebration brass that people expect from me in the first thirty seconds. I held it back for eleven songs. It does not sound until the final track. The first trumpet on the entire album *is* the morning. I wanted you to have to wait for it the way grief makes you wait for joy, so that when it finally breaks, your body knows it was earned.

> Weeping for a night, but joy comes in the morning.

That is Psalm 30, verse five. But I refused to let the morning cheat the night out of its due.

## The Song I Could Not Sing Twice

Every honest lament record has a track that costs the writer something. On this one it is a song about an empty cradle, a nursery painted yellow at the twenty-second week, a hand-stitched name-blanket folded on a changing table, a pregnancy lost. I built it on Psalm 139, the passage that says we are knit together in the secret place, because I needed the God who does the knitting to be the one holding the loss.

In the middle of that song the whole band cuts out and a father's voice speaks over the silence. *I held the baby before you did. I am holding the baby now. I am holding you. The empty cradle is not the end of the story.* I have watched that moment undo rooms of people who were told to *stay strong* when what they needed was permission to grieve in the presence of God. Lament is not the absence of faith. It is faith refusing to let go of God's sleeve while it weeps.

### The God Who Was Not in the Earthquake

Near the end of the journey there is a song that resolves the whole ache in the quietest possible way. It goes to Elijah, in 1 Kings 19, the prophet in the cave on the mountain, burnt out and wanting to die. A great wind tears at the rocks, but the Lord is not in the wind. An earthquake shakes the ground, but the Lord is not in the earthquake. A fire passes, but the Lord is not in the fire. And then, a still, small voice.

> Not the earthquake, not the storm, not the fire. The still small voice transforms.

I wanted that song on the record because so much of our grief is spent waiting for **Yahuah** to show up in the spectacular, the sudden healing, the loud deliverance, the earthquake that fixes everything. And He so often comes instead as a hush after the hard week, a whisper the storm could not drown. Being held in that hush is not a lesser answer than the fireworks. It is frequently the truer one. The God who meets you in the quiet is not the consolation prize. He is the point.

## Sunday Was Already Reaching Back

The cinematic turn of the album is a song built on an idea I cannot stop marveling at. We walked through Holy Saturday not knowing that the resurrection was already at work in the dark. Mary went to the garden in the gray of morning expecting a corpse, and the risen One said her name. The empty tomb had been working through the silent hours all along.

> Sunday bled into Saturday, the morning reaching back, and we never were apart.

That is what I most want you to carry out of this record. Even on the day between, when heaven feels sealed like a tomb and the answer has not come, the morning is already reaching backward into your silence. The seed is in the ground. **Yeshua** was Saturday-silent in the grave, but the silence was never emptiness. It was gestation.

I made this album for the funeral parlor and the hospice room and the three-in-the-morning that no worship chorus reaches. For the one who has been told to praise before they were ready and felt more alone for it. You are allowed to sit in Saturday. You are allowed to ask how long. And I promise you, on the authority of a tomb that did not stay shut, the trumpets are coming. Just not one second before the morning. When they sound, you will know you did not skip the night to get there, and that will make the joy unshakeable.
