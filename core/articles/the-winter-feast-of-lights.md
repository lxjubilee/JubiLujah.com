There is a feast that shows up in the Gospels almost by accident, and once you know its story, you cannot read the scene the same way again.

John is telling us about a day in the life of Yeshua, and he sets the stage with two small details that most of us slide right past. It was the *Feast of Dedication,* he says, and it was *winter.* Yeshua is walking in the temple, in the sheltered colonnade named for Solomon, and a crowd presses in around Him and demands, plainly, that He tell them who He is. And there, at that feast, in that cold, He says the sentence that got Him nearly stoned on the spot and has echoed for two thousand years: *I and the Father are one.*

The Hebrews call this feast *Hanukkah* (חנוכה). The word means *dedication*, the same root as the verb for consecrating, setting apart, inaugurating something for holy use. And I want to be honest with you about where this feast comes from, because its story is not like the others, and the honesty makes the Gospel scene burn brighter, not dimmer.

## A feast the Torah never commanded

Passover, Shavuot, the Day of Atonement, these were appointed at Sinai, commanded from the mouth of Yahuah in the Torah. Hanukkah was not. It is not one of the *moedim,* the feasts fixed in the Law of Moses. It was born centuries later, in one of the darkest chapters Israel ever lived, and I will tell it to you as history, because that is what it is.

Long after the last prophet fell silent, a foreign king ruled over Judea, a Greek ruler named Antiochus who set out to erase the faith of Israel altogether. He outlawed the Torah. He forbade the covenant. And in the deepest wound of all, he marched into the temple in Jerusalem, the one place on earth set apart for the presence of Yahuah, and defiled it, desecrating the altar, turning the holy house over to the worship of his own gods. The heart of the nation's worship was profaned. The lamp went out.

And then a small, outmatched family of priests and their followers refused to accept it. Against every reasonable odd, they fought and they resisted, and eventually they took the temple back. And when they had it again, they did the only thing that mattered: they *cleansed* it and *rededicated* it. They gave it back to Yahuah, relit the lamp, and set the defiled house apart for holy use once more. That rededication is *Hanukkah.* A feast not of a battle won, but of a defiled thing made holy again.

There is a beloved tradition attached to it, that when they went to relight the temple lamp they found only one small jar of undefiled oil, enough for a single day, and that it burned miraculously for eight until more could be made. I tell you plainly that this is a later tradition, treasured and retold for centuries, and I will not hand it to you as though the Scriptures recorded it. But whether or not the oil stretched, the meaning of the feast is fixed and unshakeable: a house that had been profaned was purified, and the light that the darkness tried to put out was lit again. That is why it became known as the Feast of *Lights*, a winter festival of lamps kindled against the longest nights of the year.

## Why He chose that feast to say it

Now stand back inside John's scene, because the timing is not decoration. It is the sermon.

Here is Yeshua, at the feast that celebrates a defiled temple made holy again, walking in the very temple courts, and He begins to speak about Himself in a way that keeps circling one idea. He is the shepherd. He gives His life for the sheep. No one takes it from Him. He and the Father are one. He is standing in the rededicated house, on the feast of its cleansing, quietly presenting Himself as the true temple, the real dwelling place of God among men, the one place where Yahuah's presence has come to live in full.

> They gathered that winter to celebrate a house made holy again. And the true House was standing in the middle of them, in the flesh, saying: the Father and I are one.

Feel the layers of it. The feast remembered a lamp relit in the dark. And here was the One who would soon call Himself the light of the world, saying the light shines and the darkness cannot overcome it. The feast remembered a temple purified and given back to God. And here was the One in whom the whole fullness of God was pleased to dwell, the true and final temple that no Antiochus could ever defile. He did not stumble into that feast by chance. He walked into the Feast of Dedication and let its every image gather around Him like a garment, because He *was* what the feast had been reaching for all along. Every relit lamp in every winter window was a small rehearsal for the day the true Light walked into the courts and refused to be put out.

## The house they meant to defile is being rededicated in you

And here is where it comes all the way home, to you and to me.

Because the story of Hanukkah did not end in that temple, and it did not end in that colonnade. The New Covenant says something that should take your breath: *you* are now a temple. The Ruach HaKodesh dwells in the one who trusts Yeshua, making the body a holy house, a dwelling place for the presence of God. Which means the whole drama of the feast, a sacred house defiled by an enemy, and then cleansed, and then rededicated, and then relit, is the drama Yeshua wants to work in you.

Maybe you know exactly what a defiled temple feels like. Maybe something marched into the holy places of your life and profaned them, and you have assumed the lamp is out for good and the house is beyond reclaiming. Hear the feast. Hanukkah exists to tell you that a desecrated house is not a lost house. It can be taken back. It can be cleansed. It can be *dedicated again*, that is the whole meaning of the word, and the light that the darkness tried to extinguish can be kindled once more, and burn.

That is what the One walking in the winter colonnade came to do. He is the Light that the longest night cannot swallow, and He is not ashamed to walk straight into the defiled and reclaim it for Yahuah. So let Him. Let the true Temple make His home in you, cleanse what was profaned, and relight the lamp. There is no winter dark enough, and no house too far gone, for the God who turned a desecrated temple into a festival of lights, and who said, in the middle of it, *I and the Father are one.*
