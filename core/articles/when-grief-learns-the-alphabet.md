When a city has burned, you would not expect the survivors to sit in the ashes and compose poetry in alphabetical order. Grief is not tidy. It heaves and stalls and repeats itself. It does not go A, B, C.

And yet that is exactly what one of the most sorrowful books in all of Scripture does.

The book we call Lamentations is a set of poems written after Jerusalem fell, after the walls came down, the Temple was burned, the people were dragged into exile, and the unthinkable had happened. It is raw. It weeps in the street. It speaks of hunger so terrible it is almost impossible to read aloud. It accuses, it aches, it sits in the wreckage and refuses to be comforted quickly. It is, by any measure, one of the darkest expressions of pain a people ever set down in writing.

And it is built like a spelling primer.

In the Hebrew, most of these grief-poems are acrostics. The first line begins with *aleph*, the first letter of the Hebrew alphabet. The next section begins with *bet*, the second. Then *gimel*, then *dalet*, and on and on, marching in perfect order all the way to *tav*, the last letter, twenty-two letters, twenty-two steps, the whole alphabet walked from beginning to end. In the middle poem, the most intense of them, the discipline tightens even further: three lines for every single letter, aleph-aleph-aleph, bet-bet-bet, grief made to keep step with the alphabet three times over.

Most translations cannot show you this. It disappears the moment the words leave Hebrew, because our letters are not their letters. So generations of readers have wept over these poems and never once noticed the quiet scaffolding holding them up. But it is there, and it is not an accident, and I have come to believe it is one of the tenderest things in the whole Bible.

Because think about what an acrostic *does* to a person in pain.

Grief, left to itself, has no edges. It is a flood with no banks. When the worst has happened, the danger is not only the sorrow. It is the formlessness of the sorrow, the way it sprawls and drowns and never lets you feel that you have said the thing and can now stop. And into that formlessness the Hebrew poets poured a shape. Not to shrink the grief. Not to tidy it up or make it polite. But to give it a container strong enough to hold it while it poured.

The alphabet becomes a set of banks for the flood. It says: *you will begin, and you will get to the end. There is a first letter and there is a last one, and you will walk from one to the other, and when you reach* tav *you will have said it all.* An acrostic is a promise, built into the bones of the poem, that this lament has a bottom and a shore, that the weeping, however terrible, is not literally endless. You can say everything, aleph to tav, the whole alphabet of your sorrow, and then you can lay it down.

There is an old teaching about this that I find beautiful, and I will name it as tradition rather than as something the text claims for itself. Some have said that to grieve through the whole alphabet is to bring *everything* to God, every letter, every sound the language can make, the full range of what a human being is able to say. To lament from aleph to tav is to hold nothing back. Not one letter is left out of the prayer.

And here is what moves me most. This scaffolding is not unique to sorrow. The same technique shows up in the songbook when the heart is *full*, several psalms are alphabet-poems too. The longest chapter in all of Scripture, the great psalm in love with the Word of God, is an enormous acrostic: twenty-two stanzas, one for each Hebrew letter, eight lines apiece, a vast patient walk through the alphabet devoted entirely to loving God's instruction. There are alphabet-psalms of praise, alphabet-poems of trust, even the famous portrait of the excellent woman at the end of Proverbs is an acrostic, her worth spelled out letter by letter from beginning to end.

So the same shape holds both the scream and the song. The alphabet that carries the lament out of the ashes of Jerusalem also carries the praise of a heart overflowing with love for the Word. Whether you are breaking or blessing, the poets reached for the same tool: give the emotion a spine. Let it walk from aleph to tav. Trust the shape to carry what the feeling cannot carry on its own.

I think about this every time someone tells me they cannot pray because they don't know where to start, or they cannot worship because their grief is too big and shapeless to put into words. We tend to believe that real feeling must be spontaneous, that a genuine prayer or an honest song has to come pouring out unplanned, and that any structure is a kind of dishonesty. The Hebrew poets believed almost the opposite. They knew that when you are shattered, structure is a mercy, not a fake. A form is a hand to hold. When the feeling is too much, the shape is what lets you say it at all.

This is, quietly, why the old written prayers and the ordered songs still matter so much. When your own words fail, when grief has scrambled you and you cannot find a single sentence of your own, a form hands you a first letter and says: *start here.* The seasoned prayer, the ancient psalm, the song someone else already shaped, becomes the banks your flood needs. You do not have to invent the whole thing from nothing on the worst day of your life. Someone already built the shape. You just have to pour.

So if you are in the ashes tonight, if the walls have come down in your own life and the words will not come, hear what the alphabet-poems are telling you across all these centuries.

Your grief is allowed to be enormous. It is allowed to weep in the street and accuse and ache and refuse to be hurried. The poets did all of that. But your grief is not without a shore. There is a first letter and there is a last one. You can say everything, aleph to tav, the whole alphabet of it, and then, held by a shape older and stronger than your pain, you can begin, slowly, to lay it down.

And if you keep reading past the darkest middle of Lamentations, you find that the poet, still inside his careful alphabet, still keeping step letter by letter, arrives at a line that has held broken people up for thousands of years: that the mercies of Yahuah are new every morning, and great is His faithfulness. He did not reach it by abandoning the form. He reached it by walking through it. Letter by letter, all the way to hope.
