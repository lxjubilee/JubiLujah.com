A singer once told me, apologetically, that she could not reach the top of a song I had written. It sat too high for her; the climbing notes strained and cracked, and she was embarrassed, as if the failure were hers.

So I sat down at the keyboard and moved the whole thing down. Not a little, a long way down, into a register her voice could live in comfortably. And here is the thing I want you to notice, the thing so ordinary that we forget how strange it is: *every single note changed.* Not one pitch from the original remained. The song she sang was, note for note, a completely different set of sounds than the song I had written. And she knew it instantly. She started singing on the first chord, no music in front of her, because it was, obviously, unmistakably, without a shadow of doubt, the *same song.*

I have done this ten thousand times. It is the most routine thing a musician does. And I still find it quietly miraculous, because it means a song is not what I always assumed a song was.

## The song is not in the notes

You would think a melody *is* its notes. That "the tune" means these particular pitches in this particular order, and change them and you have changed the song.

But that is not how it works, and almost no one has the kind of hearing that would even let them believe otherwise. A tiny handful of people have what is called perfect pitch. They hear a note and know its exact name, its precise frequency, the way you know a color on sight. But the overwhelming majority of us, nearly everyone reading this, do not hear that way at all. We have *relative* pitch. We do not recognize a melody by its absolute pitches. We recognize it by the *relationships* between the notes: this one higher than that one by a certain reach, this one falling to that one by a certain step, the shape of the rises and drops, the pattern of the intervals. The song lives in the *relationships,* not in the notes.

Which is why you can begin a familiar tune on any note in the world and everyone still knows it at once. Move the whole thing up, move it down, every pitch is now different, and as long as the *relationships between* the notes are kept, the song is perfectly, undeniably intact. You can transpose a melody into a key with not one note in common with the original, and it survives completely, because its identity was never in the pitches. Its identity was in how the notes stood in relation to one another. Change all the notes, keep the relationships, and you have not lost the song. You have only moved it.

And the day that truly landed on me, I understood something about the hardest seasons of a life that I had needed for years.

## When your whole life is transposed

Because a life gets transposed. That is what a hard season actually is.

One day you are living in a certain key, a comfortable register, the notes falling where you expected them, the melody of your days in a range you could sing without strain. And then something moves the whole thing down. A diagnosis. A loss. A door that closed. A grief that dropped your entire life into a lower, darker key overnight, and suddenly *every note is different.* The mornings feel different. The prayers sound different. The future you were humming is in a register you do not recognize, low and heavy and strange in your mouth. And it is so easy, in that transposition, to conclude that the song is *over*, that because every note has changed, the melody itself has been lost, that whatever God was playing through your life has stopped and something else, something worse, has taken its place.

But every note changing is not the same as the song ending.

If the *relationships* hold, if the shape of the thing endures, if the way the notes stand toward one another is kept even as the pitches all drop, then it is the same song. Transposed into a key you never wanted, yes. Harder to sing, lower, aching, unfamiliar under your fingers, absolutely. But the same melody God has been playing through your life all along, now sounding in a register you did not choose. The theme did not die when the key changed. It only moved. And a melody in a minor, low, sorrowing key is not a broken melody. Sometimes it is the same beloved theme finally sounding at the depth it always had in it.

## The theme He never abandoned

The Scriptures give us a whole life shaped exactly like this, and his name was Joseph.

Consider the key changes. A favored son in his father's house, then, in a single afternoon, thrown in a pit and sold, his whole life dropped into slavery. A trusted servant rising in a foreign house, then falsely accused and dropped again, lower still, into a prison with no visible bottom. Every stretch of his story is a transposition, always downward, every note changing, nothing where it was before. And if you had asked him in the pit, or in the cell, whether God was still playing his song, I do not think he could have heard it. The key had changed too many times.

And yet, at the end, standing before the very brothers who had thrown him down, he says the sentence that reveals the melody was never lost: *you meant it for evil, but God meant it for good.* The same events. He does not deny a note of the darkness. He names it plainly, *you meant it for evil.* But he had come to hear that underneath every brutal key change, one theme had been sounding the whole time, and the relationships had held, and it was, from the pit to the palace, the same song. God had never stopped playing it. He had only kept transposing it into keys Joseph could not yet sing.

There is a line late in the Scriptures that I read now as the deepest promise of all to a transposed life: that Yeshua the Messiah is the same yesterday and today and forever. The keys of our lives change constantly, up in the bright years, down in the dark ones, restless and modulating through every season. But the One the song is truly about does not change. He is the theme that holds through every transposition, the constant relationship every note in your life is measured against. And as long as He holds, the song holds, in any key, however low, however far from where it began.

## Sing it in the key you're in

So if your life has been dropped into a key you never wanted, if every note is different now and you have been grieving it as the end of the music. I want to offer you the songwriter's truest secret.

You have not lost the song. You have been transposed. Every pitch may have changed; the melody has not, because the melody was never in the pitches. It was in the relationships, and the deepest relationship. You to the God who is the same yesterday, today, and forever, has not moved an inch. The theme He has been playing through your whole life is still playing. It is only sounding, for now, in a lower and more aching key than you would have chosen, and one day you will hear how much depth that key gave it.

Do not wait for the old bright key to return before you are willing to sing. Sing it low. Sing it in the register you are actually in, with all the notes changed and your voice cracking on the reach of it. It is still the same song. He is still the one composing it. And a melody transposed into the dark and sung anyway is not a lesser music. It may be the truest performance of the theme your life was ever going to give.
