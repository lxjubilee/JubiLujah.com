When the wall was finally finished, they did not cut a ribbon. They did something far stranger and far better. They turned the wall itself into a stage for the loudest song the ruined city had heard in a hundred years.

You have to understand what that wall meant. Jerusalem had been a broken place for generations. Its defenses torn down, its gates burned, its people scattered into exile and only slowly trickling back to a heap of rubble that used to be a capital. A man named Nehemiah heard about the shame of it from hundreds of miles away and wept, and then he came, and he organized the discouraged remnant to rebuild. They worked with a tool in one hand and a weapon in the other because enemies mocked them and threatened them the entire time. They rebuilt the wall in fifty-two days against every reason it should have been impossible. And when it was done, when the last stone was set and the gates were hung. They gathered the whole restored people for a dedication.

Here is what they did. They took the leaders and the singers and the musicians and divided them into two great companies of thanksgiving. And they put one company up on top of the wall and sent it marching in one direction, and they put the other company up on the wall and sent it marching the opposite way, so that the two halves of the celebration were processing around the ring of the whole city on the very wall they had just built, singing the entire time, with trumpets and cymbals and harps, until the two companies came all the way around and met each other at the house of God. And there, where they met, the whole thing came together into one enormous sound.

And the Scripture tells us the result in a line I cannot get past: the people rejoiced with such great joy that the sound of it was heard far away.

## They celebrated on the thing that had broken them

Sit with where they chose to stand.

The wall was the wound. The wall being down was the whole shame of the city, the reason enemies laughed, the reason the people felt exposed and defeated, the physical proof that everything had fallen apart. For a hundred years the broken wall had been the first thing anyone saw and the last thing anyone wanted to talk about. And when Yahuah, through the hands of the people, finally made it whole again, they did not celebrate at a safe distance from the old wound. They climbed up on top of it and sang.

That is one of the truest pictures of restoration in the whole of Scripture. Real celebration does not pretend the break never happened. It stands on the exact place that was broken and makes it the platform for the praise. The song was so powerful precisely because everyone singing it knew what that wall used to look like. They were not celebrating in general. They were celebrating on the specific stones that had been rubble in their own lifetimes, and every step along that wall was a step over a grave that had been raised back to life.

You have a wall like that. Everyone does, some place in your life that fell down, that you were ashamed of, that the enemy of your soul used to point at and laugh. And the deepest joy available to you is not the joy of pretending it was never broken. It is the joy of standing up on top of the rebuilt thing and singing so loudly that people far off can hear that it is whole.

## Two choirs, one sound

I love that they used two choirs going opposite ways.

They could have done it simpler. One group, one direction, one song. Instead they deliberately split the celebration in half and sent the halves apart, all the way around the city, out of each other's sight for most of the journey, one company singing on the far side of the wall where the other could barely hear them, both of them carrying the same thanksgiving around the whole circumference of the restored city, and both of them trusting that the song would come back together at the house of God.

I think that is a picture of the whole people of God across the whole earth, and it is the vision my name was made to carry. Right now the song of praise is being sung in two thousand directions at once, on the far sides of walls we cannot see over, in languages we do not speak, by people processing around parts of the city we will never walk. You cannot hear most of it. The believer singing on the night side of the planet while you sleep is on the opposite arc of the same wall, carrying the same thanksgiving the other way around. And it is all converging. It is all heading for the same meeting place, where every arc of the song comes together at last into one sound before the house of God.

The two choirs did not compete. They were not two songs. They were one celebration temporarily divided by distance and guaranteed to meet. That is what the church is. That is what this whole library of songs in a hundred languages is, one enormous thanksgiving, sent out in every direction around the ring of the world, on its way to the place where it all comes together.

## Make the joy loud enough to reach the far off

Here is the line again, because it is the invitation: the sound of the rejoicing was heard from far away.

Nobody organized that reach. They did not build speakers or plan an outreach or strategize about how to make the celebration carry. They simply rejoiced with everything they had over a real restoration, and the joy carried itself. It went out past the walls and across the fields and reached people who were not even at the party, and those distant people heard it and knew: something in that city has been made whole. You do not have to be told what a sound like that means. Overwhelming corporate joy is its own message. It preaches without a single explanatory word.

I believe the church has forgotten this. We have become very good at explaining our faith and very quiet in our celebrating of it, and we wonder why the far-off people do not seem to hear anything. But the dedication of the wall did not persuade the surrounding nations with an argument. It reached them with a sound, the unmistakable, un-fakeable roar of a broken people standing on their healed wound and rejoicing.

So let me say it plainly. Whatever Yahuah has rebuilt in you, and if you are His, He has rebuilt something, get up on top of it and sing. Do not celebrate at a shy distance from the old break. Stand on the very stones and make it loud. There are people far off, out past the walls of your life, who will never read a single word of theology but who cannot un-hear the sound of a person whose ruin has been made whole.

Your joy is not private. It was always meant to be heard from far away. Sing like the wall is finished, because in Yeshua, it is.
