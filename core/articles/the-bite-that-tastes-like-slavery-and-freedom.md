There is a moment at the Passover table I think about more than almost any other in all the feasts, and it is not a song. It is a taste.

You take something bitter, sharp enough to sting your eyes and catch in your throat, and you dip it into something sweet and dark and thick, and you put them in your mouth at the same time. Not one and then the other. Together. In a single bite you taste the sting and the sweetness at once, and neither one cancels the other out.

I cannot get over the wisdom of that. Because I think it is the truest thing anyone has ever put on a plate.

## Maror: the taste that will not let you forget

The Scripture is plain about the bitter part. When Yahuah gave the instructions for the Passover meal, He said the lamb was to be eaten with unleavened bread and with *bitter herbs*. The Hebrew word is *maror*, and it comes from the same root as the word for *bitter* itself. This is not a subtle seasoning. It is a deliberate, mouth-puckering sharpness, and it is *commanded*. Written right into the feast.

Why would a God of joy build a bite of bitterness into His celebration?

Because the story the feast tells is a story that *began* in bitterness, and He will not let His people pretend otherwise. The people of Israel had lived generations in slavery, their lives, the text says elsewhere, made *bitter* with hard labor. And when they sit down every year to taste freedom, Yahuah does not want them to taste only the sweetness of the ending. He wants the bitter on their tongue, so their bodies remember what they were brought *out of*.

There is a mercy in that most of us miss. A rescue you cannot feel the cost of is a rescue you slowly forget. The bitter herb is Yahuah's way of keeping the memory of the pit alive in the mouth of the freed, not to shame them, but so that the freedom never goes flat, never becomes a thing they assume they always had.

## Charoset: the sweet the tradition set right beside it

Now for the sweet.

Alongside the maror, the tradition of the table places a paste called *charoset*, a soft, dark mixture of fruit and nuts and wine and spice. I want to be careful and honest here: unlike the bitter herbs, which Scripture commands, the charoset is part of the developed tradition of the seder, not a line in the text. But the tradition built it for a reason, and the reason is beautiful. Its color and texture are meant to call to mind the mortar the slaves once mixed for the bricks of Pharaoh's cities.

Sit with that. The very thing that looks like the mud of slavery is made *sweet*. The tradition takes the picture of the worst labor and renders it in honey and fruit. And then, here is the moment, the bitter herb is dipped into that sweetness, and the two go into your mouth together.

> You are not asked to taste the sorrow first and the joy later. You are asked to taste them in the same bite, on the same tongue, in the same breath.

## The flavor the whole gospel is made of

I have spent my life around music, and if there is one thing songs have taught me it is this: the sweetest melody is almost always the one with an ache buried inside it. Pure sugar with no sorrow underneath goes thin and forgettable. But joy that has passed *through* grief and come out singing, that is the sound that undoes grown men. That is the note that rearranges you.

The Passover bite is that note, made edible.

And it is the exact flavor of the whole gospel. Because our whole faith refuses to hand us a joy that skips the sorrow. We do not have a Savior who avoided the bitter and went straight to the glory. We have a Savior who *drank* the bitter, a cup He asked, in a garden, if it might pass, and then took anyway, so that the sweetness on the far side of it would be real and not pretend. The cross and the empty tomb are not two separate flavors we taste in sequence and move on from. They are one bite. The Lamb was slain *and* the Lamb is risen, and you taste them together, and neither one cancels the other out.

This is why the people who have suffered most often worship the deepest. They are not faking a shallow happiness. They have tasted the maror. They know exactly how bitter the slavery was. And that is precisely why the sweetness in their mouths is so strong, because it is dipped in the memory of the pit, and it holds.

## Do not spit out the bitter

Here is what I want you to hear, tender as I can say it.

There is a pressure, everywhere around us, to build a life and a faith with the bitter removed. To only ever taste the sweet. To skip the herb, dodge the sorrow, curate a table where nothing stings. And it always produces the same thing: a sweetness so thin it cannot nourish anyone, a joy that evaporates the moment real trouble walks in the door.

The Passover will not let you do that. It hands you the bitter *on purpose*, and it sets the sweet right beside it, and it tells you to taste them at once, because that is the only kind of joy strong enough to carry a freed slave through the wilderness. The kind that remembers the pit. The kind dipped in what it was rescued from.

If your mouth is bitter right now, if there is a grief on your tongue you cannot seem to swallow. I am not going to tell you to hurry past it. The feast doesn't. The feast tells you to dip it. To take the bitterness you are carrying and press it down into the deep sweetness of a God who has already gone into the pit ahead of you and come up singing. Not to erase the sting. To hold it in the same bite as the joy, until you taste, all at once, both what you were saved from and Who saved you.

Slavery and freedom, on the same tongue. Grief and glory, in the same breath. That is the taste of Passover. And it turns out that is the taste of being loved by Yeshua all the way through, bitter dipped in sweet, and the sweet winning, and the bitter never wasted.

Taste and see. Both of it. That is where the joy that lasts is hiding.
