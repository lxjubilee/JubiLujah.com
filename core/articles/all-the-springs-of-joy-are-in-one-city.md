We talk about joy as if it has no address.

We say it *strikes* us, *washes over* us, *comes and goes*, as if it were weather, drifting in from nowhere and out again, and the best we can do is stand in the open and hope some of it lands on us. Joy, we assume, is a mood, and moods have no map.

The poets of the Psalms would have found that idea strange to the point of sadness. Because they knew something we have almost entirely forgotten: worship has a *geography*. Joy has a mountain. There is a place on the map where the springs of gladness rise, and the whole song of Israel keeps pointing back to it with an almost unbearable homesickness.

They called it Zion. And what they said about it will change how you think about where your own joy is supposed to come from.

## A mountain called the joy of the whole earth

In one of the great Zion psalms, the poet stands and looks at the city set on its hill and reaches for the biggest thing he can say about it. He does not call it the joy of Israel. He does not call it the joy of Jerusalem. He calls Mount Zion *the joy of the whole earth*.

The Hebrew there is *mesos kol ha'aretz*, the exultation, the glad shout, of *all the land*, of the entire earth. Not one people's happiness. Everybody's. He is looking at a single ridge of rock in a small, contested country and declaring that the gladness of the *planet* has its source right there.

You have to understand what Zion actually was to feel the audacity of that. Zion started as a *fortress*, a stubborn hill the city was built on, a stronghold that had to be taken by force before it could become the city of the great King. It was stone and slope and hard-won ground. And the poets looked at that fortified rock and said: this is where the joy of the world lives. Not a feeling. A *place*. Beautiful in its height, they sang, the crown of the earth's gladness, this mountain, this city, this exact spot where Yahuah had chosen to set His name and gather His people.

Worship, to them, was never a free-floating mood. It had a *there*. And the whole nation oriented its year around going *up* to it, walking the roads to the mountain, singing songs written for the climb. They did not wait for joy to find them. They went to where it was kept.

## Where everyone's happiness is stored

Now the second Zion psalm turns the astonishment all the way up, and it does it with a single image about water.

This psalm is doing something the ancient world found almost scandalous. It runs down a list of the great foreign nations, the proud empires, the old enemies, the far-off peoples on the edges of the map, and instead of cursing them, it *registers* them. It says that of Zion it will be recorded: *this one was born there, and that one was born there*, that people from every hostile nation would one day be counted as native-born citizens of the city of God, entered in the registry as if they had always belonged.

And then, at the very end, comes the line that I cannot read without my chest tightening. The singers and the dancers together, celebrating in the city, cry out one thing: *all my springs are in you.*

The Hebrew word is *ma'yan*, a spring, a fountain, a source of living water. In a dry land, a spring is not a luxury. It is *survival*. It is the reason a city exists at all. And the worshippers point to Zion and say: every fountain I have is *here*. Every source of my life, every wellspring of my gladness, rises in *this* place. Not in my circumstances. Not in my success. Not scattered across a hundred things that come and go. *All* my springs, every last one, are in *you*.

> They did not say joy visited them. They said the *source* of their joy had a location, and the location was the gathering-place of God.

Read that as a diagnosis of your own restlessness. You have been chasing joy the way you chase weather, standing in field after field hoping the good feeling lands on you. The psalm says stop. The springs are not scattered. They are *gathered*. There is one place where every fountain of your gladness rises, and it is the place where God gathers His people to Himself.

## The gathering was always the point

Here is what pulls the whole thing together, and it is the part I was made to tell you.

Zion was never mainly about the *rock*. The rock mattered because of what happened *on* it, a people, gathered, before their God, praising. The mountain was the joy of the whole earth because it was the place where the *whole earth was invited to come home*. Every foreign nation entered in the registry, every stranger reckoned as native-born, every pilgrim who walked the long road up the slope singing, the geography existed to *gather*. Zion is what you call the spot where everyone is finally in the same room with God and with each other.

And that is why this ancient mountain leans forward into something even bigger. The New Testament takes the old geography and lifts it: it tells believers that they have already *come* to Mount Zion, to the city of the living God, to countless angels in joyful assembly, to a festival gathering of the redeemed from every nation. The address did not disappear. It *opened up*. The springs that once rose on one hill in one country now rise wherever Yahuah gathers His people to worship, and the registry of the native-born has been thrown open to every tribe and tongue on earth.

Which means the homesickness you feel, that ache for a joy that stays, a gladness with a *there* to it, is not a flaw. It is a compass. It has been pointing you home the whole time.

## So come up to the mountain

Stop trying to be happy in the field alone.

The design of worship was never that joy would rain randomly on scattered individuals. It was that a whole earth of gathered people would climb the same mountain, walk into the same festival, and discover that every spring they had been chasing separately was pooled in one place all along, the place where God draws His people together and dwells among them in their praise.

That is what a gathering of worshippers *is*. It is Zion breaking out wherever the redeemed assemble. It is the joy of the whole earth finding its address again in an ordinary room full of extraordinary homecomings. The springs are there. They always were. You just have to come up.

So come. From whatever far country, under whatever old name, carrying whatever thirst, come up to the mountain of gathering, and put your name in the registry of the joyful, and say what the singers and dancers have always said when they finally arrive: *all my springs are in You.*

The city is on the hill. The fountains are running. And there is a place at the celebration with your name already written in.
