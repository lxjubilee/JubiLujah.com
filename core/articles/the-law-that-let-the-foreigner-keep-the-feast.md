If you wanted to build a feast that shut foreigners out, Passover is exactly the feast you would build.

Think about it. This is the meal that remembers the night God chose *this* family and not that one, the night He set a difference between Egypt and Israel, the night He carried His own people out of a nation that was not theirs. It is the most tribal, most in-house, most *ours-and-not-yours* moment in Israel's whole calendar. A birthday of a single people. You would expect its instructions to draw the circle tight and keep it that way.

And that is what makes the small print of Passover so astonishing. Because right there, tucked into the very laws for keeping the feast, the Torah stops and does something the ancient world almost never did. It makes a doorway for the outsider. It says, in effect: if a foreigner among you wants to keep this feast, here is how he may. There is a way in.

Most of us read straight past it. But it is one of the most tender sentences in the Law of Moses, and I want to slow all the way down on it.

## The stranger who wanted in

The Hebrew word running through this passage is *ger* (גר), the sojourner, the foreigner who lives among you, the resident outsider with a different birth and a different past. And the instructions about Passover deal with him directly. A foreigner, the Torah says, may keep the Passover.

There is a condition, and I will not hide it from you, because the Word does not hide it. The *ger* who wanted to eat the Passover had to enter the covenant fully, the males were to be circumcised, the sign that marked a man as belonging to Abraham's household. This was not a low fence. It was the real thing: not a guest pass, but full covenant membership. But look closely at what that condition actually says. It does not say the foreigner may *never* come in. It says the opposite. It says the door is real, the way is open, and once he has come all the way in, the Torah's own words are these, *he shall be as one born in the land.* As a native. As family. No lesser tier. No side table.

And then, as if that were not enough, comes the line that I think the ancient world could not have written on its own:

> One Torah shall be for the native and for the stranger who sojourns among you.

*One law.* Ezrach and ger, the homeborn and the foreigner, under the same instruction, at the same table, keeping the same feast, with the same standing before the same God. In a world where every people group ranked itself above the peoples around it, where the foreigner was by definition a lesser being with fewer rights, the God of Israel wrote into the founding feast of His nation that there would be *one* standard, and the outsider who came in would live under it as an equal.

## A place was always set

I have eaten at a lot of tables in a lot of countries, in guarded places where an outsider is watched at the door, and I know the difference between a table that has a chair for you and a table that merely lets you stand near it.

What moves me about the Passover law is that it does not merely permit the foreigner to exist at the edge of the feast. It sets him a place. It hands him a way to move from the doorway to the table, from *watching* the family remember its rescue to *being* one of the rescued. The circumcision was not a barrier meant to keep him out; it was the covenant meant to bring him all the way in, to make sure that when he sat down, he sat down as a son of the house and not as a tolerated guest who might be asked to leave when the real family got serious.

Do you feel how deliberate that is? Yahuah did not have to say any of this. He could have made Passover a closed door. Instead, on the way *in* to the story of His chosen people, He wrote a standing invitation to the un-chosen, a place always set, a law that made no distinction, a table where the stranger who came in was simply *one of us.* The feast of a particular people carried, from the start, the heart of a God who was never only after one people.

## The table where the outsider becomes kin

Now carry this to the upper room, because that is where the whole provision finally opens all the way.

Yeshua keeps this same feast on the last night before the cross, and He takes the ancient Passover and stretches it to the size it was always straining toward. He lifts the bread and the cup and makes them the sign of a new covenant in His own blood, and the sign of entry is no longer a knife. It is faith. The covenant that once asked the *ger* to be marked in the flesh now asks the outsider to be marked in the heart, circumcised inwardly, brought near not by the blood of his own body but by the blood of the Lamb. And the promise on the far side of that entry is the very promise the Torah made at the first Passover: you shall be *as one born in the land.* No lesser tier. No side table. Kin.

This is why the message of Yeshua could walk out to every nation without betraying a word of the Torah. The Passover always had a door for the foreigner. Yeshua simply flung it as wide as it was built to open. The stranger who once had to travel the whole distance into a single nation is now brought near, in Him, from wherever on earth he stands, and seated, as family, at a table that always had a place set.

## For the one still standing at the door

So hear this, you who have been hovering at the edge of the room, sure that the real feast belongs to people with a longer history and a purer pedigree than yours.

The most in-house feast in all of Scripture wrote you a doorway on its very first page. The God who set apart one people also declared, in the same breath, *one law for the native and the stranger*, one welcome, one standing, one table. He never intended the outsider to press his face to the window and watch a family he could not join. He intended the outsider to come in and become the family.

The place has been set for longer than you have been alive. The Lamb has already been given, and the only entry He asks now is your trust. So come in from the door. Sit down. There has been a chair at this table with your name on it since the very first night the blood went up over the frame, and the God of this feast has been keeping it for you.
