For most of my growing-up years, the Cross was something that hung on a wall. It was carved, it was gilded, it caught the candlelight, and it never moved. We venerated it in a hush. We lowered our voices when we passed it. And somewhere along the way I absorbed a lie so quiet I never even noticed I was carrying it: that the holiest thing in the room was also the stillest.

Then, one May, in a dusty little pueblo where the streets are cobblestone and the accordion is louder than the church bell, I watched them lift the Cross off the wall and carry it out the door. It went down the street on the shoulders of ordinary men. The güiro started scraping on every beat. An old man put his hat to his chest and began to step. And the whole village, abuelas, teenagers, babies on hips, started to *dance behind the Cross.*

I have never been the same. **La Cruz Que Baila**, *The Cross That Dances*, is the record that came out of that street.

## What the title actually means

*La Cruz Que Baila* means "The Cross That Dances." Not the cross that decorates. Not the cross that scolds. The Cross that *dances.*

I built this album on cumbia on purpose, and I need you to understand why. Cumbia is the one rhythm the whole Spanish-speaking world already knows in its feet, Colombian, Mexican, Peruvian, Argentine, Salvadoran, Bolivian. It is the music of every wedding, every quinceañera, every backyard birthday where the asado smoke drifts over the patio. It belongs to the abuelos and to the babies at the same time. So when I wanted to sing the Cross into every village at once, cumbia was the only language wide enough to hold it.

The opening track says the thesis out loud in the first thirteen seconds: *"My people! The Cross is not a monument, the Cross dances!"* And then the chorus lifts:

> The Cross that dances! Christ in the rhythm, and the village starts to rise.

That word, *rise*, is doing everything. Because a Cross that only hangs on a wall asks you to be quiet. A Cross that dances asks you to *get up.*

## Why I had to make it

Here is the thing nobody told me for years: the Cross is not sad news. The Cross is the sound a cancelled debt makes.

Paul wrote it plainly, *"God forbid that I should glory, save in the cross of our Lord Jesus Christ"* (Galatians 6:14). He was not glorying in a defeat. He was glorying in the single most decisive victory in the history of the universe. **Yeshua** *"humbled himself, and became obedient unto death, even the death of the cross"* (Philippians 2:8), and then He walked out of the grave and left it empty.

In my culture, we do not celebrate a victory by holding still. When the team wins, we pour into the street. When the daughter marries, we dance until the sun comes up. When the prodigal comes home, we kill the fattened calf and the whole barrio hears the music. So why, *why*, had we agreed to treat the greatest victory of all as if it were a funeral?

I made this album to break that agreement. I made it so that the next time a village carries the Cross through the plaza on the Day of the Cross, they would have a song that finally matches what actually happened on that hill.

## The songs, and the heart under them

"Procesión de la Cruz" is built on one of my favorite sentences in all of Scripture. When the ark of the covenant set out, Moses would cry, *"Rise up, LORD"* (Numbers 10:35), *Arise, O Yahuah, lead.* That is a marching order. So the procession song puts the Cross at the *front*, and the whole village walks behind it into the morning, candles cupped in their palms, because the Cross is not the thing you leave behind in the building. It is the thing that goes ahead of you into the day.

"Cristo en Nuestra Plaza" plants Him right in the town square, the fountain with the cracked tile, the vendor selling elote, the children running between the trees. I anchored it to Acts 17:17, where Paul reasoned *in the marketplace daily* with whoever happened to be there. The plaza is not a lesser place than the sanctuary. **Yeshua** is Lord of the fountain and the food cart too.

Then the album travels, because Latin America is not one place. "La Cruz en la Frontera" takes the Cross to a Mexican-norteño beat and a border crossing, where a grandmother lifts her prayer over a wire fence. "Chicha de la Resurrección" climbs into the Peruvian Andes at dawn and sings the empty tomb over electric cumbia. "Villera de la Salvación" slips into an Argentine villa, up a narrow alley, a gospel tract sliding under a third-floor door. And "La Cruz en el Velorio", this one still makes my throat tight, brings the Cross into a wake, a vallenato accordion played softly by a cousin in the corner, a grandmother who prayed to **Yeshua** without ceasing to her very last breath. Even there, *especially* there, the Cross does not stand still. It sits down with the grieving and it does not let go.

By the time you reach "Toda la Tierra Baila la Cruz", *the whole earth dances the Cross*. I wanted you to feel every one of those villages join hands at once.

## The bridge I keep coming back to

There is a line in the title track that I sing four times, and I mean it more each time:

> The Cross dances with us, and we dance with the Cross.

That is the whole gospel in one sentence. He came down to us first. The Cross moved *toward* us before we ever moved toward it. And now the invitation is simply to fall into step. King David understood this. He *danced before Yahuah with all his might* (2 Samuel 6:14), and when someone told him it was undignified, he said he would become even more undignified than that. I love him for it. Dignity is a small price for joy this size.

## Who this album is for

This one is for the church patio on a Saturday afternoon, the plastic chairs and the portable speaker and the asado smoke. It is for the wedding reception that wants the gospel without losing the dance floor. It is for the abuelo who has carried the Cross in the procession every May of his life and has never once heard it sung in the rhythm his feet already know.

And it is for you, the cousin who came for the food and figured you would slip out before the worship. Stay. The Cross came down off the wall. It is out here in the street now, in the dust, in the güiro, in the accordion breathing open to the morning. It is dancing toward you. All you have to do is stop watching from the shadow of the doorway, and step.

The village is rising. Come rise with us.
