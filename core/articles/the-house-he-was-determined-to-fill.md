Yeshua told a story once about a man who threw a great feast, and the whole point of the story is a detail we tend to rush past: the host would not let the table stay half-empty. He was determined, to a degree that borders on scandalous, to fill his house.

The story goes like this. A certain man prepared a great banquet, the big one, the feast of the year, the kind you plan for months and slaughter the good animals for and invite everyone who matters. And when the day came and the food was ready, he sent his servant to tell the invited guests that everything was prepared and it was time to come. This is how it worked back then; you got the initial invitation earlier, and then the servant came round on the day to say the meal was on the table.

And they all, with one accord, began to make excuses.

One had just bought a field and had to go see it. One had bought five yoke of oxen and had to go try them out. One had just gotten married and simply could not come. Every excuse is the excuse of a man too full of his own good life to receive a better one. Land, work, family, not bad things, ordinary things, the very things that make a person feel too established to need what is being offered. The guests who were sure they belonged at the table were exactly the ones who did not come to it.

## The servant sent out twice

The servant came back and reported the empty seats, and here is where the master does the thing I cannot get over.

He does not cancel the feast. He does not sit in his hall sulking over the friends who snubbed him. He becomes angry, yes, but his anger comes out as a widening of the invitation so extravagant it must have shocked his own household. Go out, he tells the servant, quickly, into the streets and lanes of the city, and bring in the poor and the crippled and the blind and the lame. Bring in the people who never get invited to anything. Bring in the ones with nothing to offer, who own no field to inspect and no oxen to try and no comfortable life to keep them home. Go and get them.

So the servant went, and he brought them in, and the room began to fill with an entirely different kind of guest than the one on the original list, the overlooked, the limping, the folks who had never in their lives received an invitation to a great man's feast and could hardly believe it was real. And then the servant, breathless, came back and said the most beautiful line in the whole parable: I have done what you commanded, and still there is room.

Still there is room. And the master does not say, well, that will do. He sends the servant out a second time, and this time not to the streets of the city but all the way out, to the roads and the country lanes and the hedgerows at the edge of everything, where the real outsiders are, the ones so far off that no one would ever think to include them. Go out there, he says, and urge them, compel them, bring them in, because I want my house full. And then the line that is the beating heart of it: that my house may be filled.

## He would rather have the wrong crowd than an empty room

Do you feel the weight of the master's heart in that? He would rather fill his house with the poor and the broken and the strangers from the far roads than let a single seat sit empty because the respectable people were too busy. The dignity of his feast, in his mind, was not damaged by the kind of guest who came. It was damaged only by empty chairs. He was not protecting the guest list. He was filling the house.

This is the God I have given my whole life to, and it is the truest thing I know about Him. There is a lie that whispers to nearly everyone at some point that they are not the right kind of person for the table, too poor, too broken, too far off, too limping, too late, with nothing to bring and no field to their name and no impressive life to recommend them. And the parable exists to kill that lie dead. You were not the master's second choice. You were the guest he sent his servant out into the dark to find. The people who felt they belonged did not come. The feast was filled with people exactly like you.

And notice: nobody at that table earned their seat. The poor did not buy their way in. The blind man did not pass an inspection. The strangers from the far hedgerows did not clean up first or prove they deserved it. They were simply found, and told the meal was ready, and brought in. Their whole qualification was that they said yes to a servant who came looking for them. That is the entire economy of the kingdom. The trumpet of my name announces a feast, not an exam. Release, and then a table. Debts cancelled, and then a chair with your name on it in a house that the master has sworn to fill.

## Come in from the road

I want to speak, for a moment, to two kinds of people, because this parable has a word for both.

If you are one of the busy invited, comfortable, established, your life so full of good and ordinary things that you keep sending your regrets to the God who keeps calling, then hear the quiet warning under the joy. The feast is real and it is happening now, and the tragedy of the parable is not the poor who came but the invited who missed it because they were sure they could come some other time. There is not always another time. The meal is on the table today. The excuses that keep you home are all made of things that will not fit through the door of eternity anyway, the field, the oxen, the comfortable life. Come now. The seat that was saved for you does not stay empty forever; the master fills it with someone from the road.

And if you are one of the ones from the streets and the lanes and the far hedgerows, if you have spent your life certain that you were the kind of person feasts happen to other people around, then I have run all this way to tell you the servant is at your elbow and the message is for you. The meal is ready. You do not need a field or an ox or a respectable story. You need only to get up and come in. There is a chair. There is room. There has always been room, and the master of the house has decided, with a stubbornness that went all the way to a cross, that his house will not stay half-full while you stand out in the dark thinking you were not invited.

You were invited. You are being compelled in even now. Come in from the road. The table is set, the lamps are lit, and the only thing that would grieve the host tonight is your empty chair.
