The line I am proudest of is usually the one killing the song.

I did not want this to be true, and I resisted it for a long time. When I am writing, every so often a line arrives that is just clever, a turn of phrase, an image, a bit of wordplay so satisfying that I sit back from the instrument and admire it. It is the kind of line you want to show people. And because I love it, I build the rest of the song around it, protecting it, arranging everything else to make room for its brilliance. And then, again and again, I discover that the song is not working, and I hunt for the problem everywhere else first, and the problem, when I finally admit it, is the line. The clever one. The one I love most. It is drawing all the attention to itself. It is a beautiful thing standing in the exact spot where the song needed to breathe.

And then comes the hardest ten seconds in all of songwriting: cutting it. Deleting the line I was proudest of. Not because it is bad. It is genuinely good, but because it is good in the wrong place, and its goodness is costing the whole song its life. Writers have a brutal, accurate phrase for this. They call it killing your darlings. And it is exactly as unpleasant as it sounds, because the darlings are, by definition, the parts you love.

For years I experienced this purely as loss, as a tax the craft extracted from me, a penance for my own vanity. Then I started paying attention to what Yeshua said about a gardener and a vine, and I realized the knife was not punishing me at all. It was doing the most loving thing there is.

## He cuts the branch that is already bearing

Yeshua described Himself as the true vine, and His Father as the vinedresser, the one who tends the plant. And He said something about that vinedresser that I used to read too quickly. He said that every branch that does not bear fruit, the Father takes away. That part we expect; dead wood gets cleared. But then He said the other thing, the part that stops me every time. Every branch that does bear fruit, He prunes, so that it will bear more.

Read that slowly, because it is not what we assume about pruning. We assume the vinedresser only cuts the dead and the failing. But Yeshua says the fruitful branch gets the knife too. The healthy one. The one that is already producing. The vinedresser walks up to a branch that is doing well, green, alive, heavy with grapes, and He cuts it back anyway. Not because it failed. Because it succeeded, and its very success, left unchecked, will divide the plant's strength across too much growth and shrink the harvest. The good branch is pruned precisely because it is good, so that its goodness can be concentrated instead of scattered.

That is my clever line exactly. It is not dead wood. It is a fruitful branch, genuinely alive, genuinely good, that has to be cut anyway, because if I leave it the whole vine gives a smaller harvest. The cutting is not a verdict that the branch was worthless. It is the opposite. You only bother to prune what is alive.

## Good in the wrong place is still in the way

This distinction changed how I understood the knife, and I think it changes far more than songwriting.

There is a kind of loss that comes because something in your life was bad, and it had to go. We understand that loss. It is painful but it makes sense; you can see the rot. But there is another kind of loss that is much harder to accept, the loss of something genuinely good, cut away not for any fault of its own but because, left where it was, it was quietly costing you the larger thing you were made for. A good job that was slowly crowding out your family. A good friendship that had subtly become the place you went instead of God. A good ability that had grown so central to your sense of yourself that it was strangling your dependence on the One who gave it. None of these are dead wood. They are fruitful branches. And the vinedresser, who loves the whole vine and is aiming at a harvest you cannot yet see, comes with the knife anyway.

When it happens to us we almost always misread it. We assume that if God is taking something away, it must have been bad, and we rummage around looking for the sin that got it cut off. But sometimes there is no rot to find. Sometimes the thing removed was good, and that is exactly why it hurt so much, and exactly why it had to go. Its goodness had begun to compete with your fruitfulness. A branch spending all its strength on beautiful leaves in the wrong place will not give you grapes.

## The knife is held by someone who loves the whole plant

I want to be honest about how much this hurts, because I do not think we should pretend the cutting is painless just because it is loving. When I delete the line I loved most, there is a real grief in it, every time. And when God prunes a good thing out of a life, the grief is far greater than any line. The vinedresser's knife is still a knife. It still cuts.

But here is what steadies me, and it is the whole difference. The branch is not being cut by an enemy who wants it smaller. It is being cut by the One who planted it, who tends it daily, who wants nothing in all the world except that this branch should bear more than it ever could untended. The vinedresser is not against the vine. The vinedresser is the most devoted friend the vine will ever have. He does not cut carelessly, and He does not cut what does not need it, and He never once cuts to harm. Every stroke is aimed, with total precision, at a harvest.

> When God takes away something genuinely good, He is not always removing a sin. Sometimes He is pruning a branch, because He is after a harvest larger than the good thing you were clutching.

So I have made a strange kind of peace with the knife, in my songs and in my life both. When I feel the resistance rising, the certainty that this line, this thing, this good branch simply cannot go. I have learned to ask a harder question than whether it is good. Of course it is good. The question is whether its goodness has wandered into the place where the larger thing needed to grow. And if it has, I have learned, slowly, to let it be cut. Not thrown away as worthless. Cut back, on purpose, by loving hands, so that everything left can finally give the harvest it was planted for.

The line you love most may be the one strangling the song. And the good thing you are clutching may be the very branch the vinedresser is reaching for, not to rob you, but because He has seen the harvest you were made to bear, and He loves you far too much to leave you smaller than that.
