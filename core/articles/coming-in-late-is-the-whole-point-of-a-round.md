Every child learns to sing a round before they know what one is.

You know the shape of it even if you have never named it. One group starts the tune. They sing a phrase, and just as they begin the second phrase, a second group begins the *first* phrase, the same melody, only later, deliberately behind. Then a third group comes in behind the second. And what should be a mess, what feels at first like everyone singing the wrong thing at the wrong time, resolves into something astonishing: the tune folded over on top of itself, harmonizing with its own echo, a single simple melody becoming a rich braided sound that no one group could ever make on their own.

And here is the thing about a round that I want you to hold very still and look at.

The ones who came in late are not behind. They are the harmony.

## The lie you have been singing about yourself

I have met so many people who carry a quiet, worn-down conviction that they are late. Late to faith. Late to figure life out. Late to the settledness that everyone around them seems to have found years ago. They look across the room at people who have been walking with Yahuah since they were small, people who seem to know all the words, and they feel the particular loneliness of the one who came in after the song had already started, as though the good part happened without them, and they missed their entrance, and now they are just trying to catch up to a music that got ahead of them long ago.

If that is you, I want to retune the whole way you are hearing your own life, because you have been listening to it as if lateness were a mistake. And in a round, lateness is not a mistake. It is the *design*. There is no round without the ones who come in after. If everyone started at the same moment, you would not have harmony. You would have unison, one thin line, the same note stacked on itself with no depth at all. The richness, the whole reason the thing is beautiful, comes precisely from the fact that not everyone entered at the beginning. The voices that came in late are the ones that turned a plain melody into a chord.

## The eleventh hour was never a punishment

Yeshua told a story that scandalizes our sense of fairness every single time, and it is really a story about people who came in late.

A landowner hires workers for his vineyard at dawn. Then he goes out again at midmorning and hires more. Again at noon, again in the afternoon, and finally, at the eleventh hour, with almost no daylight left. He finds people still standing around who no one had hired, and he sends even them into the field. And when evening comes and he pays them, he starts with the last, the eleventh-hour ones, the ones everybody would call *too late*, and he gives them a full day's wage. The same as the dawn workers. The whole amount. Not a reduced portion for their reduced hours. Everything.

The dawn workers grumble, of course, because they are counting by the clock, and by the clock the latecomers should get less. But the landowner has been operating on a different measure the whole time. *Are you envious because I am generous?* he asks them. The last, he says, will be first.

I have come to believe that story is not only about mercy at the edges. It is about how the Kingdom actually sounds. Yahuah is not running a race where the earliest entrance wins and every later one is a diminished version of it. He is conducting a round, and he keeps sending voices into the field at every hour precisely because the song is not finished, and the ones coming in at the eleventh hour are not the leftovers of the music. They are the last, richest layer of it, the harmony that arrives after the melody has been established, laid over the top of everything that came before, and paid in full.

## The tune you came in late to was already yours

There is one more mercy in how a round works, and it is the tenderest one.

When you come in late to a round, you do not sing a different, lesser song. You sing *the same melody*, the identical tune the first group sang, note for note. Nothing was withheld from the latecomer. The whole song is given to you exactly as it was given to the ones who started at dawn. You just enter it further along, and your voice, folding in over theirs, makes the sound bigger than it was before you got there.

So the years you think you missed were not a smaller portion of the tune. When you came in, you got the whole melody, the entire love of Yahuah, the full song of Yeshua, undiminished, not a discounted version scaled to your late arrival. And the part of you that aches over the years you spent outside the music, the ones who found him before you did, hear this gently: those are the voices you are now harmonizing *with*. You were never meant to be a copy of their entrance. You were meant to come in after them and turn their melody into a chord. That is not catching up. That is your part.

## The invitation

If you feel late, stop trying to sprint to the front of a song that has no front.

A round has no falling behind, because behind is where the harmony lives. The child who comes in a phrase later than the group beside her is not failing at the song. She is completing it, laying her voice over theirs until the plain tune blooms into something neither group could make alone. That is you. Your late entrance is not the evidence that you missed the music. It is the reason the music got richer.

Come in now, wherever you are in the song. At the eleventh hour if that is what it is. You will not be handed a smaller melody for your lateness. You will be given the whole tune, the same one the dawn voices got, and paid the full wage, and set down exactly where your voice was always meant to fold in, a beat behind the ones ahead of you, on purpose, making the harmony that could not exist without the ones who came in late.

The last, he said, will be first. That is not just mercy.

That is how the round was written.
