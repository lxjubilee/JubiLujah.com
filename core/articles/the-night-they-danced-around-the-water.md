There is a feast most of us have never been taught, and it may have been the happiest night of the whole ancient year.

Picture it. A great stone courtyard, packed with people. Enormous golden lampstands lifted so high that men have to climb ladders to fill their bowls with oil, and when the wicks catch, the light pours out over the whole city, the tradition says there was not a courtyard in Jerusalem that did not glow from it. Flutes playing. Harps and lyres and cymbals. And down in the middle of it, the most respected, most dignified elders of the nation, the wise men, the heads of the courts, the men who spent their days bent over the sacred scrolls, are dancing. Juggling burning torches. Leaping. Singing at the top of their lungs while a whole city claps them on through the night, hour after hour, until the sky begins to gray in the east.

And the strangest thing of all is what the party was about. It was about a pitcher of water.

Let me tell you the feast, and then let me tell you what happened one year in the middle of it, because it is one of the most beautiful things in all of Scripture.

## The feast of booths

The feast is called Sukkot, the Feast of Tabernacles, or Booths. It comes in the autumn, after the harvest is gathered in, and the Word lays it out plainly: for seven days the people were to live in temporary shelters, little huts of branches, to remember that their ancestors had once lived in tents in the wilderness while Yahuah carried them home. It was a remembering of the fragile years, kept from inside the fat, settled, harvest-full years.

And here is the part I want you to feel. When Scripture gives the command for this feast, it does something it does for no other. It commands *joy*. Over and over, the instruction is that the people are to rejoice before Yahuah, and in one place the Word piles the words up almost impatiently and tells them to be *altogether* joyful, nothing held back. It is the only feast in the calendar where gladness itself is legislated. You were commanded to be happy. The tradition that grew up around it even came to call Sukkot, simply, "the season of our joy."

So the people obeyed the command to rejoice the way you obey a command to rejoice: they threw the greatest celebration they knew how to throw.

## The water they drew with shouting

Now I have to be careful and honest with you, the way I always try to be, because here Scripture and tradition part ways and it matters which is which.

The Word commands the feast, the booths, and the joy. But the specific ceremony I am about to describe, the torches, the all-night dancing, the water, the Bible does not spell out. It comes to us from the later Jewish tradition, from the teachers who recorded how the feast was actually kept when the Temple still stood. So I offer it to you as history and tradition, not as a command of Scripture. But it is very old, and very well-attested, and once you know it you will never read a certain Gospel scene the same way again.

Each morning of the feast, a priest went down with a golden pitcher to the pool of Siloam, drew water, and carried it back up through the city in a procession while the shofar sounded and the people sang. At the altar, that water was poured out, a libation, an offering of plain water lifted up to God at the season when the whole land was praying for the rains that would either make or break the coming year. Water poured out to the God who sends water.

And every night, the courtyard became the celebration I described, the great lamps, the flutes, the dancing sages, the crowds. The tradition gave it a name that means, roughly, *the Rejoicing of the House of the Water-Drawing.* And one line the old teachers left about it has never left me: they said that whoever had not seen this rejoicing had never in all his life seen rejoicing at all. That is how they remembered it. Not as one celebration among many. As the definition of the word.

Why water? Because tucked in the prophets is a line the tradition tied to this very ceremony, a promise that with joy you would draw water out of the wells of salvation. The water was never only about rain. It was a picture. A whole nation, at the height of its gladness, drawing water and pouring it out and singing about a salvation that runs like a spring.

## The man who stood up in the middle of it

Now come with me to one particular year.

The Gospels tell us that Yeshua went up to Jerusalem for this feast, the Feast of Booths, and taught in its crowded, festive, torchlit days. And then Scripture marks a specific moment: on the last day of the feast, the great day, the climax of all that water-drawing and pouring and singing, Yeshua stood up.

He stood up, and He *cried out*, the word is a shout, a raised voice pitched over a crowd. And into the middle of a feast that was, at that very hour, obsessed with drawn water and poured water and thirst and the God who sends the rain, He shouted an invitation: if anyone is thirsty, let him come to *me* and drink. And He said that the one who trusts Him would become a source himself, that out of the very center of such a person, rivers of living water would flow.

Sit with the timing. He did not say it in a quiet room. He said it standing in the loudest, brightest, most water-drenched celebration of the entire year, at the moment the whole city was pouring out water and singing about the wells of salvation. It is as if He waited for the feast to build to its full roar, and then stepped into the exact center of the picture and said: *this. All of this pouring and singing and thirst. It was pointing at me. I am the well. Come and drink.*

## What the party knew that we forget

Here is what I want you to carry away from a night you were never taught.

We have quietly absorbed the idea that reverence is solemn and joy is shallow, that the holiest thing you can do is go still and serious, and that dancing, shouting, torchlit, all-night gladness is somehow beneath the dignity of worship. The people who built the House of the Water-Drawing believed the opposite, and I think Scripture is on their side. They believed that the highest response to a saving God was not a hush but a *feast*, that when your barns are full and your God has carried you all the way home from the wilderness, the fitting thing, the *commanded* thing, is to light every lamp you own and dance until the sun comes up.

And they believed that this uncontainable joy was, at its root, about thirst and water, about a salvation you draw up like a spring and cannot stop pouring out. They just did not yet know the face of the well.

You do. He stood up in the middle of the party and told you plainly.

So let the old joy teach you something. You do not have to choose between reverence and gladness; the deepest reverence the ancient world ever staged was also its wildest joy. And you do not have to draw your own water from a dry place and hope it holds. The One the whole feast was reaching for is here, still standing in the middle of every thirst, still shouting the same six words over the noise of your life: if you are thirsty, come to me.

Draw the water with singing. There is Someone worth dancing around until dawn.
