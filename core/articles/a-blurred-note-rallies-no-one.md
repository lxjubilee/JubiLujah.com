I once wrote a song I was sure would be the best thing I had ever made, and it did nothing at all.

It had everything. A melody that turned in surprising places, chords that I was quietly proud of, a structure so intricate I could have taught a class on it. I played it for a room of people who loved me and wanted to be moved, and I watched their faces, and their faces were polite. Nothing happened. The song was, by every measure I could name, excellent, and it left the room exactly as it found it. Not one person could have hummed it back to me on the way out. It was a firework that went off too high to see.

It took me an embarrassingly long time to understand what had gone wrong, because the problem was hidden inside the thing I was proudest of. The song was impressive. And impressive was the disease. I had written it, though I would never have admitted this at the time, to be *admired*, to display what I could do, and a song built to be admired asks the listener to watch the writer. It never opens a door they can walk through. It is a locked room with a very beautiful door.

The passage that finally diagnosed me was one I had read many times without feeling it. It is Paul, writing to a church in Corinth that had fallen in love with the flashiest gifts, and he makes his argument, of all things, with musical instruments.

## The flute that plays no tune

Paul says, in effect: even lifeless instruments make his point for him. Take a flute, or a harp. If it does not sound distinct notes, if the pitches blur together into an impressive wash of sound, how will anyone know what tune is being played? You can be technically producing music, fingers flying, breath pouring through the pipe, and if the notes do not come out *distinct*, there is no song. There is only noise that happens to be coming from an instrument.

Then he sharpens it with a trumpet. If the trumpet gives an uncertain, indistinct call, who is going to get ready for the battle? A trumpet on the field is not there to be beautiful. It is there to be *understood*. It tells the whole army when to rise, when to advance, when to hold. And a trumpeter who blew a dazzling, complicated, blurred fanfare that no one could read would not rally a single soldier. He would simply stand there sounding magnificent while the line fell apart around him, because a blurred note rallies no one.

I read that and felt it land on my clever, admired, useless song. I had blown a magnificent blurred note. And Paul drives it home for the people, not just the instruments: unless you speak words that can actually be understood, he says, how will anyone know what you meant? You will only be speaking into the air. There are many languages in the world, and none of them is meaningless, but if I do not know what your sounds mean, I am a foreigner to you and you to me, no matter how sincere the sound.

## The five words that beat ten thousand

Here is the line from that chapter I have written where I can see it while I work.

Paul says that in the gathering, in the room where people have come together to be built up. He would rather speak five words that people can understand and be instructed by, than ten thousand words in a tongue no one can follow. Ten thousand to five. He is not against the ten thousand because they are too many. He is against them because in that room they do nobody any good, and five plain words that land do more than ten thousand dazzling ones that don't.

That ratio undid my whole theory of what makes a song good. I had assumed the goal was to give the room the most, the most craft, the most surprise, the most of *me*. Paul says the goal in the gathering is not the most. It is the clearest. It is the five words they can carry, not the ten thousand they will forget. And the reason is not that art is bad, or that beauty is suspect, or that simplicity is holier than skill. The reason is *love*. The whole chapter turns on one command he keeps circling back to: since you are so eager for these gifts, he says, then strive to excel at the thing that actually builds other people up. Aim your excellence at *them*, not at your own reflection.

That is the sentence that changed how I write. Clarity is not the opposite of art. Clarity is where the art starts loving the listener instead of the maker. A blurred note serves the trumpeter's vanity. A distinct one serves the whole army. And I had spent years perfecting blurred notes and calling it depth.

## Sing with the spirit and the mind

I do not want to be misheard, so let me say what Paul is careful to say. He is not telling us to stop making beautiful things, and he is not telling us to stop pouring our spirits out. In the very same passage he says he will sing with his spirit, and he will also sing with his mind. Both. The overflow of the heart *and* the discipline of being understood, held together. He is not choosing feeling over clarity or clarity over feeling. He is refusing to let a real gift become a private performance that leaves the room untouched.

That is the discipline I had been missing. It is much easier to be impressive than to be clear, because impressive lets you hide, and clear leaves you nowhere to hide. When I strip a song down until the note is distinct and the meaning lands and a tired person at the back can follow it on the first hearing, there is nothing left to dazzle anyone with. There is only whether the thing is true. That is terrifying, and it is the whole job.

So now, when I finish a song, I no longer ask first whether it is impressive. I ask whether the notes are distinct, whether someone who has never met me could tell what tune is being played, could get ready for the battle, could carry five clear words out of the room and into the week. If the answer is no, then it does not matter how proud of it I am. It is a blurred trumpet. It will rally no one.

Whatever you make, whatever you are trying to say to the people in front of you. I want to hand you Paul's gentle, ruthless test. Do not aim to be admired. Aim to be understood, because being understood is how you love them. Five clear words that reach them will outlast ten thousand dazzling ones that don't. Blow the note distinct. Let them hear the tune. That is not the lesser art. That is the art finally pointed at somebody other than yourself.
