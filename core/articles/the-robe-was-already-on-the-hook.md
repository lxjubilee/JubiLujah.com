## We Tell the Story Backwards

We have told the prodigal story wrong for so long that we stopped noticing. We make it about the son, his rebellion, his ruin, his long walk of shame back up the road, his rehearsed apology. We turn Luke 15 into a lesson about the sinner getting his act together.

But go back and actually read it. The hinge of the whole parable is one sentence: *while he was yet a great way off, his father saw him, and ran.* The Father was watching the road. The Father moved first. And in that culture, a dignified man did not run. He would have gathered up his robes and exposed his legs and looked like a fool doing it. **Yahuah** looked like a fool for joy before His son ever got a word out.

I made an entire album to move the spotlight off the son's walk and onto the Father's run. Because if you think the homecoming depends on the quality of your apology, you have never actually met this Father.

## The Speech He Would Not Let Me Finish

The tender heart of the record is a testimony about a man who had been gone eleven years. Eleven. He had rehearsed the apology a hundred times, *Father, I have sinned, I am no longer worthy*, polished it like a stone in his pocket. He walked up the driveway slowly, bracing for the lecture he was sure he had earned.

The Father was on the porch. A coffee mug fell off the step. And the old man was already running before the son could clear his throat.

> I rehearsed the words for eleven years. He never let me reach the first tear.

That is the gospel in one image. The son had a speech about worthiness, and the Father interrupted it with an embrace. He got out *Father, I have sinned*, and the arms came around him and ended every plan he had made to earn his way back in. **Yeshua** told this story precisely so that you would stop trying to negotiate a re-entry that has already been granted. The embrace comes *before the word.* You do not apologize your way to the Father. You get run at.

### Coming to Yourself in the Far Country

Before the running, there is a turning, and I did not want to skip it. Luke says the son *came to himself* in the far country, sitting in the swine-pen, counting what he had lost, the coins and the covenants and the whole cost of his wandering. That phrase has always struck me. He did not come to a theology first. He came to *himself.* He remembered whose he was.

> I came to myself in the far country dust. Father, I have sinned, and I need You to trust.

There is a tender song on the album about exactly that moment, the pig-pen days ending, the journey home beginning, every step made of grace. And I wanted it there because the turning is real and it matters. But here is what the album refuses to let the turning become: a transaction. The son's turning did not *earn* the embrace. It just pointed him toward the One who had been facing the road the whole time. Repentance is not the price of the homecoming. It is the moment you finally aim your feet at a welcome that was already prepared.

## The Detail That Undid Me

Now here is the twist I built the whole album to arrive at, and it is buried in a detail most people read past.

When the Father calls for the celebration, he says *bring the best robe, and put it on him; and put a ring on his hand.* And when I sat with that, a question stopped me cold: *where was the robe?* Where was the ring? You do not have a best robe and a signet ring lying loose in the yard. They were kept. Prepared. Set aside. Which means, and this is almost too much to hold, the Father had them *ready before the son came home.*

> The robe was on the hook. The ring was in the drawer. The Father had them ready before I turned to look.

The fatted calf had been fed for this day. The sandals had been kept by the door. Every gift the Father intended to lavish on the returning child was prepared *in advance*, before the boy repented, before he turned, before he was even a speck on the horizon. **Yahuah** was not caught off guard by your homecoming. He has been getting the house ready the entire time you were gone.

## The Chair That Was Never Filled

I put that same truth into a quieter song about a kept place at the table. Every Sunday dinner, an extra chair. Every Tuesday breakfast, a plate set out for someone who was not there. You may have assumed the Father moved on, filled your seat, reassigned your place. He did not.

> The chair has been kept. The plate has been set.

This is the difference between the God we fear and the God who actually is. The God of our anxiety keeps a ledger of our absence. The real Father keeps a *place setting.* And it is not merely restored proximity, Ephesians 1 says we were *predestined for adoption*, and Romans 8 calls it the Spirit of adoption by which we cry *Abba.* You do not come back as a hired servant on probation, which is all the son dared hope for. You come back a son. A daughter. Named, sealed, seated.

## Lift Up Your Eyes

The album does not stay at one father and one son. It widens out to Isaiah 60, *lift up your eyes and look around; your sons come from afar, and your daughters are carried*, and to Isaiah 43, where the Father says *bring My sons from afar, bring My daughters from the ends of the earth.* Because the homecoming was never meant to be a private reunion. It is a global gathering, a whole family reassembling from every far country, every century, every land.

And the last song makes clear the door does not have office hours. The homecoming is not a one-time weekend event. It is *forever*, today, tomorrow, every dawn, the Father's door standing open and the table standing set.

I made this for the one who is standing at the end of the driveway right now, rehearsing. Put the speech down. You will not get to finish it anyway. The robe is already on the hook. The ring is already polished. And the Father. I promise you, is already running. You were never as far from home as the years made you feel. Come on. Come on home.
