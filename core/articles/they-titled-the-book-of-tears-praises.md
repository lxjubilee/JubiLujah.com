I want to tell you the real name of the book you think you know.

We call it Psalms, an old Greek word for songs played on strings. A fine name. But it is not what the people who wrote it called it. In Hebrew the book is *Tehillim*. And *Tehillim* does not mean "songs." It means, flatly and unmistakably, **Praises**. The Book of Praises. That is the title Israel gave it, the sign over the door.

Now here is what should stop you in your tracks. Walk into that book expecting praise, and you will spend most of your time inside weeping.

You will find a man asking Yahuah, out loud, why He has forgotten him. You will find *how long* asked so many times it becomes a refrain. You will find someone whose only companion, he says, is the darkness. You will find betrayal, and sickness, and enemies, and the specific loneliness of praying into what feels like an empty sky. Scholars who count these things will tell you the single largest category in the Book of Praises is not the hymn. It is the *lament*. The book named for praise is, by volume, a book of tears.

Israel was not careless. Israel was not confused about which songs were happy. They looked at their collected grief, the raw, the furious, the inconsolable, and they wrote **Praises** over the top of all of it on purpose.

I think that is one of the bravest things any people has ever done. And I think it will change how you worship if you let it.

## What the word underneath is actually saying

The word *tehillah*, praise, comes from a root you already know without knowing it: *halal*. It is the *hal* in *hallelujah*. And its oldest sense is not "to compliment." It is closer to *to shine*, to flash out light, to make a thing radiant and seen.

To praise, in that root, is to make Yahuah *visible*, to lift Him up where the whole room can see how bright He is.

Hold that next to the tears, and something quietly enormous happens.

> A lament, sung to His face, is not the absence of praise. It is praise refusing to shine a false light. It is the radiance of a God trusted enough to be told the truth.

Because think about what a lament in that book actually is. It is not a complaint muttered *about* Yahuah to someone else. It is a cry brought *to* Him, addressed to His face, in His hearing. And you do not pour out your worst hour to a God you believe has left the room. You only cry *how long* to Someone you are certain is still listening. The lament is soaked in an unspoken faith the griever may not even notice he is confessing: *You are here. You can do something. You are the One I bring this to.*

That is why they could file the tears under Praises and not be lying. The tears *are* a form of shining. They make visible a God so trustworthy that His people will hand Him their unedited anguish and expect to be held.

## A praise big enough to carry sorrow without lying

Here is what we have often done instead, and I say it with tenderness, because I have done it too.

We have built a kind of praise that can only hold good weather. Bright, quick, resolved, a praise that needs you to feel fine first, or at least to fake it convincingly by the second chorus. And so the moment real sorrow arrives, that praise has no room for you. You have to leave your grief in the car and put on a face at the door, because the only worship on offer cannot carry what you are actually holding.

The Book of Praises will not allow that. It is *praise built wide enough to walk your grief straight through the front door.* It hands you words for the valley of the shadow and never once demands you pretend the valley is a garden. It lets you say *my tears have been my food day and night*, and counts that, too, as praise, because it was sung to His face and not away from it.

This is the mercy of it: you do not have to lie to worship the God of the Bible. You never did. The most honest thing you can do with your sorrow is not to hide it from Him until it clears up. It is to *bring it to Him as a song*, to let even the ache be a way of making Him visible, a way of saying, without saying it, *I still believe You are the One who hears.*

## Notice how the tears travel

Read those grieving songs to their end and watch what happens, because they are teaching you the shape of a sung sorrow.

So many of them begin underwater, *why, how long, where are You*, and somewhere in the middle, without the circumstances changing at all, the language turns. *Yet I will trust.* *But You, O Yahuah.* The problem is not solved. The enemy has not left. The night is exactly as long as it was. But the griever has been *sung* somewhere. In the act of pouring it all out to a listening God, the weight redistributed. The song became the hand that reached in and held on.

That turn is not decoration. It is the whole architecture of praise that tells the truth. It does not leap over the grief to get to the light. It carries the grief *toward* the light, at grief's own pace, and lets the light find it there.

And a handful of them do not turn at all. One ends in the dark, with the last word being *darkness* itself. Yahuah kept that one too. He did not require every song to arrive somewhere bright before it was allowed into the Book of Praises. He knew some nights do not end by morning, and He wanted His people to have a song even for those. Even the unresolved cry, aimed at Him, is praise.

## So bring the whole book of you

If you have been holding a sorrow you did not think you were allowed to sing. I want to lift that off you.

You are allowed. The oldest worship book in the world was named **Praises** by people who filled it mostly with tears, precisely so you would know that your grief has a place at the front of the sanctuary and not out in the parking lot. Praise, it turns out, was never the sound of a life with nothing wrong. It was the sound of a God made visible, even, and especially, by the ones who came to Him with wet faces and told Him everything.

So sing the ache. Address it to His face. Let it shine.

They titled the book of tears *Praises*, and they were not wrong. They just understood, long before we did, that a praise too small to carry sorrow was never really praise at all.
