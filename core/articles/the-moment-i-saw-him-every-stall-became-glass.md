The souk raised me to think in worth.

Walk any market in my part of the world and you are walking through a live argument about value. What is this saffron worth by the gram? What will you give me for the carpet, and what is it truly worth, and are those two numbers the same? A boy grows up in that noise learning the deepest question a market can teach: what would you trade everything for? Most people spend a lifetime in the stalls and never once meet a thing worth the whole purse. This album is about the day I did.

*Pearl of the Souk* is built on one parable of **Yeshua**, four sentences long, that has never let me go. He said the kingdom of heaven is like a merchant searching for fine pearls, who, on finding one pearl of great price, went and sold everything he had and bought it (Matthew 13). A merchant. Not a beggar who had nothing to lose. A man who knew markets, knew worth, knew exactly what he was giving up, and gave it all anyway, gladly, because he had finally found the thing the whole trade was for.

## Why the merchant, and not the beggar

I want you to notice who the hero of that parable is, because it changed how I sing.

The merchant is an expert. He has handled a thousand pearls. He is not fooled by a shiny fake; he has seen enough to know the real thing when it is finally in front of him. And that is the person I made this record for, not only the empty-handed, but the accomplished. The one who has money in the hand and options in the world and has been walking the souk of this life pricing everything, and has quietly begun to suspect that none of it is worth the whole purse.

The opening song is that man's moment of recognition: *I walked the crowded souk with coins inside my hand, past the saffron and the turquoise and the carpets rolled and grand, but the moment that I saw You every stall became as glass; You were brighter than the gold leaf, You were worth the whole bazaar.* Every stall became as glass. That is what happens when you finally see the Pearl. It is not that the market becomes ugly. It is that it becomes transparent. You can suddenly see straight through everything you were haggling over, to the one thing behind it all.

> I have sold all I had and I'm running to You. You are the Pearl, You are the Pearl of the souk.

## Selling all is not a loss, it is a running

Here is the thing the parable insists on that the world will never quite believe: the merchant sells everything *with joy.* Not grimly. Not as a grim religious duty, teeth clenched, counting what he gave up. He runs.

The second verse of the title song is the gladness of that release: *let the merchants keep their silver and the weavers keep their thread, I have found the costliest beauty in the One who rose from dead; take the silver of my striving, take the brass of all my pride, You are worth the empty purse and the riches set aside.* The silver of my striving. The brass of my pride. Those are the things I most hated to hand over, and they turned out to be the things I was most relieved to be rid of. You do not lose your life when you sell all for the Pearl. You finally put down the exhausting bag of counterfeits you had been dragging through the market.

That is why this album, though it centers on the costliest exchange a soul can make, does not sound heavy. It sounds like a man running.

I know that running from the inside. I spent years pricing my own worth by what I could hold, the respect I had earned, the songs I had written, the good name I carried through the streets. Handing that over felt, at first, like ruin. It was the opposite. The purse that had grown so heavy I could barely lift it turned out to be full of glass beads I had mistaken for pearls. The King asked for all of it and gave me Himself, and no one has ever made a poorer trade look so much like winning.

## A whole market of smaller treasures

The Pearl is the center, but a souk is full of other beauties, and I let them each have a song, because every one of them turned out to point back to Him.

"Pour the Qahwa" is the coffee of welcome, Jesus the Host who fills His house from the streets and the lanes (Luke 14). "Saffron Morning" is the dawn, and the mercy that the book of Lamentations says is new every single morning, never running out no matter how dark the night was (Lamentations 3). "Bread, Oil, and Honey" is the set table and the overflowing cup of Psalm 23. "The Lamp in the Fanous" is the lantern in the dark alley, the word that is a lamp to the feet (Psalm 119) and the Light that darkness cannot master. And "Letters of Gold" is the calligrapher's art turned toward Scripture itself, the words the Psalm calls sweeter than honey, sweeter than the honeycomb (Psalm 19).

Each of these is a real treasure. The saffron is genuinely precious. The bread is really sweet. I am not pretending the market is worthless. I love the market; it is my home. I am saying that every good thing in it is a smaller pearl, and behind them all is the one Pearl they were all rehearsing for. When you find Him, you do not despise the saffron. You finally see what the saffron was hinting at.

## The souk goes silent, then it shouts

The album ends the way the market ends its day, but not in exhaustion. "Hallelujah in the Souk" takes the confession public, out among the vendors and the buyers and the children underfoot, because Scripture promises a day when every tongue confesses that Yeshua is Lord (Philippians 2). Even the tongues that spent their whole lives calling out prices will one day call out His name. And the tune of that last song circles back to the very first, so the record does not close so much as loop, the merchant is still running, the market is still being redeemed.

I made this for the one still walking the stalls. You have coins in your hand and you are good at this. You know worth, you have priced a thousand things, and something in you is tired of never finding the thing worth the whole purse. It is here. It has a name, and the name is **Yeshua**. When you see Him, the stalls go to glass, and you will do the strangest, gladdest thing a market-wise heart can do, sell all you have, laughing, and run.
