Somewhere tonight, a man is winning an argument about God and losing something he cannot name.

He has read the books. He has his answers ready, for the ones who believe too easily, and for the ones who believe nothing at all. He can take apart a weak reason for faith in under a minute. He can take apart a weak reason against it just as fast.

He wins. He almost always wins.

And when the thread goes quiet and the screen goes dark, he is standing exactly where he started. Outside. A little colder. Right, and alone.

Maybe you know him. Maybe, some nights, you are him.

Here is the strange arithmetic of the argument about God: you can win every round and never once arrive. You can be correct at the top of your voice and still be a stranger to your own life.

Because the argument was never the door. Somewhere along the way it quietly became the wall, the thing you built so you would never have to stand at a threshold and knock.

I want to show you the one question that ends it. Not a sharper argument. Not a checkmate move that finally silences the other side. A different kind of question altogether, one that sounds less like a courtroom and more like a father's doorway.

But first, why the courtroom can never end it.

A courtroom is built to produce a winner and a loser, and it is very good at its job. Every ruling can be appealed. Every proof meets a counter-proof. Every closing argument becomes the opening of the next one. You can spend an entire life in that room, brilliant and exhausted, and the gavel never falls for good.

And notice what the courtroom quietly requires of you. It requires an opponent. It needs someone across the table to defeat. So the God at the center of the argument slowly stops being Someone you might meet and becomes a position you must defend, a flag on a hill, a point to be scored.

You cannot go home to a position. You can only go home to a Person.

Which is why the question that finally ends the argument is not aimed across the table at all. It is aimed at the door.

There is a story Yeshua once told, and even people who have argued about God their whole lives go quiet when they hear it.

A father had two sons. The younger one wanted his share of everything now, while his father was still alive, which is a way of saying, *I wish you were already gone.* The father, astonishingly, gave it. The boy took it to a far country and spent it all, and when the money was gone the far country had no more use for him. He ended up among the pigs, hungry enough to envy what they were eating.

And there, in the mud, he began to build his argument.

He rehearsed a speech. *Father, I have sinned against heaven and before you. I am no longer worthy to be called your son. Make me one of your hired servants.* He had his case ready, the confession, the demotion, the terms. He was going to negotiate his way back in as a laborer, because a son was more than he dared to ask for.

He was still a long way off when his father saw him.

Understand what that means. The father was watching the road. Every day, for who knows how long, an old man scanning the horizon for a shape he already knew by the way it walked. And when he saw it, the father, who had every right to wait with his arms crossed, ran.

He ran, and he threw his arms around his son, and here is the part that undoes people: he interrupted the speech. The boy got out the confession. He never got to the negotiation. Before *make me a servant* could leave his mouth, the father was calling for the robe, the ring, the feast. The argument the son had prepared for a hundred miles was made unnecessary by an embrace he had not dared to imagine.

The son came home hungry for one thing and braced for another. He came home wanting a place at the table and expecting a place in the barn. And the father gave him the table without letting him finish making his case.

Do you see it now?

The son never won an argument about his father's character. He simply stopped debating it and started walking toward it. The whole distance home, he was still holding a speech he thought he needed. He was wrong. What the door required was not a better argument. It only ever required that he come.

So here is the one question. Not *Am I right about God?*, the courtroom question, the one that always has one more appeal. But this:

*What if the One we have been arguing about has been watching the road for me?*

That question ends the argument, because it is not aimed at an opponent. There is no one left to defeat. It is aimed at a doorway, and it turns the whole exhausting contest into a single honest step: not to win, but to come home.

I know how much courage this asks, especially of anyone worn thin by years of debate, anyone who has defended the honor of God so long and so fiercely that laying the weapons down feels like betrayal. It is not betrayal. The father in that story was never in danger of losing. He was only ever waiting.

And I will not pretend the far country is nothing, or that the road home is short. It is not. Homesickness is a long ache, and a bolted door is a real grief. But the ache itself is a clue. You do not grow homesick for a place you never had. The longing to come home is the first evidence that there is a home to come to.

So let the argument rest tonight, just tonight. And instead of preparing your next case, try the one thing the son tried. Turn toward the door and say something true and unguarded in that direction. Not a proof. Not a position. A first honest sentence, spoken like a son who is still a long way off: *If You are there, I am tired of the far country, and I am coming.*

You may find, as he did, that the door was open before you finished the sentence, and that the One you were arguing about had already left it, and was running down the road to meet you.
