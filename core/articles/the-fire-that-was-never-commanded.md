On the day the altar was dedicated, fire came down out of the sky and everyone fell on their faces. And then, moments later, two young men who had just watched it happen reached for fire of their own.

Let me set the scene, because the timing is everything. It was the great inauguration of the worship of a whole nation. Aaron and his sons had been consecrated, the offerings made exactly as commanded, and at the climax the glory of Yahuah appeared to all the people. Fire went out from before Him and consumed the offering on the altar, real fire, His fire, sent from heaven with no human hand to strike it. And when the people saw it they shouted and fell on their faces. This was the thing they had waited for. The presence had come. The altar was lit by God Himself, and now it would burn, and all anyone had to do from that day forward was tend the flame He had given.

That is the fire that was already there when Nadab and Abihu, Aaron's two oldest sons, took their censers into their hands. They put fire in them, laid incense on top, and offered it before Yahuah. And the account tells us, with a bluntness that should stop us cold, that it was fire He had not commanded them. Strange fire. Unauthorized fire. Fire from some other source than the altar God had just kindled with His own presence. And fire went out again from before Yahuah, the same fire, from the same place, and this time it consumed them, and they died there in front of everything.

We flinch. We are meant to. But I want us to look past the flinch to the thing almost nobody names, because it is aimed straight at how we worship now.

These men were not pagans. They were not mocking God. They were ordained priests, sons of the high priest himself, standing in the holiest work in the world on the most important day of their lives, and by every appearance they were eager. That is what unsettles me most. Their sin was not indifference. It was initiative. They had energy, they had zeal, they had censers in their hands and something they very much wanted to offer, and the one thing they did not have was a command. They improvised in the presence of God. They brought Him fire He never asked for, on a day He had just shown them exactly what fire He would accept, and they died with their own flame still burning in their hands.

Sit with the awful mercy of it, because it is a parable of something we have grown very comfortable doing.

We have become a people of the sincere improvisation. We arrive at worship full of good intentions and creative energy, and we assume that because we mean it, He must want it. We build the moment, engineer the swell, strike our own matches to get the fire going, and we rarely stop to ask the question the whole scene is screaming at us: is this the fire You sent, or the fire I brought? We have quietly decided that sincerity is the same as obedience, that as long as our hearts are warm the source of the flame does not much matter. And Nadab and Abihu are the record's terrible correction to that idea. It is possible to be completely earnest and completely uninvited. It is possible to offer God, with real feeling, a worship He never asked for.

Because holiness is not a mood we generate. It is a reality we approach. And the reason the fire had to fall from heaven in the first place, the reason no priest was ever permitted to light that altar with an ordinary spark, is that the whole point of it was to say: this flame did not come from you. You did not manufacture the presence. You did not conjure the glory with your effort or your excellence or your zeal. It came down. It was given. All you can do is receive it and keep it burning, and the moment you decide you can supply your own, you have missed the entire meaning of the altar.

Hear what Moses says to Aaron in the silence afterward, standing over his own dead sons, because it is the sentence the whole thing turns on. This is what Yahuah meant, he says, when He said: among those who draw near to Me I will be treated as holy, and before all the people I will be glorified. There it is. Among those who draw near. The nearer you get, the more the holiness matters, not less. It is precisely the ones closest to the fire, the leaders, the priests, the ones whose whole lives are built around the presence, who are most tempted to grow casual with it and most accountable when they do. And Aaron's response to all of it is one of the most staggering lines in the book: Aaron held his peace. He was silent. There was nothing to say. He had seen the difference, finally and forever, between the fire God sends and the fire a man brings.

Some have wondered whether the two were drunk, since the very next thing Yahuah tells the priests is never to drink wine before entering His presence, and that may be part of it, though the text does not say so outright and I will not put words in it. But the deeper failure is plainer than any theory, and it is one a perfectly sober person commits every day. It is the failure to wait for His fire. It is the reaching for a flame of our own because His has not come fast enough, or big enough, or on the schedule our moment required.

So let me say the hope, because there is enormous hope buried in this, and it is the whole reason I am telling you a story that begins in fear.

The good news of the altar is not that God withholds His fire. It is that He sends it. He is not a reluctant God who has to be pried open with the right technique; He is the God who, on the day the altar was dedicated, lit it Himself, in full view, so that no one would ever have to strike a spark again. And He has kept doing it. When the day of Pentecost came, He did not ask His people to work up a flame. He sent one, tongues of His own fire resting on each of them, kindled from heaven exactly as it had always been. The fire He wants on your altar is fire He fully intends to supply. Your job was never to manufacture it. Your job is to build the altar, lay the offering, and wait, undistracted, unhurried, unwilling to substitute your own spark for His presence.

So before you strike your next match, stop and ask where the fire is coming from. Not because He is hard to please, but because He is holy, and the thing you are handling is His. Lay down the censer you filled yourself. Empty your hands. And then keep them open over the altar, and wait for the flame that falls, because it still falls, and when it does, you will know beyond any doubt that it was never something you could have lit on your own.
