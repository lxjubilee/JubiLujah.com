There is a song that got me through a hard year. You have one too. Mine sat in my chest like a handrail, and on the mornings I could not feel anything else, I could still reach for it and pull myself a few steps up the day.

So when a newer grief arrived, different shape, different weight. I did the natural thing. I reached for the handrail. I sat down and sang the old faithful song over the new ache, expecting it to do again what it had done before.

It didn't reach.

Not because it was a bad song. Because it was answering a question I was no longer asking. It had been sung true, once, into a wound that was now scarred over, and it kept arriving at that healed place, knocking on a door that had already opened. The fresh ache was somewhere else entirely, in a room the old song had never been shown, and the old song had no idea it was there.

I sat with that for a long time, half-annoyed, half-grieving, until it turned, the way these things do with me, into something I could not stop turning over.

Because the Scriptures are strangely insistent on this exact point, and I had read past it for years. Over and over, the old songwriters do not say *sing to Him.* They say *sing to Him a new song.* A new one. Again and again, across the psalms and into the prophets, a new song, a new song, a new song, as though the newness were not a suggestion but the whole instruction. And I had always heard it as a kind of divine restlessness, as if Heaven were a listener who had grown tired of the hits and wanted fresh material. As if the old songs had expired.

They hadn't. That was never it. And the reason it was never it turns out to be written into the way your own body is made.

## The hum you stopped hearing

Here is a small fact that runs your whole nervous system, and you have almost certainly never thanked it for anything. Your senses are built to notice *change*, and to go quiet in the presence of the constant.

You are not feeling your clothes against your skin until I mention them. Now you are. A minute from now you won't be again. You stop smelling a scent within minutes of walking into it. And sound does this most plainly of all: the refrigerator has been humming in your kitchen all day, and you have not heard it once, until it clicks off, and the sudden *absence* startles you, and you realize with a small shock that a sound was there the whole time, filling the room, and your ears had simply stopped reporting it. The hum did not get quieter. You went deaf to it, on purpose, because it never changed.

This is not a flaw. It is a mercy. A brain that reported every constant sensation at full volume forever would drown. So the body learns the steady things and lets them fall silent, and saves its attention for what is *new*, the footstep that wasn't there, the note that shifts, the change that might matter.

And the moment I understood that, I understood why God keeps asking for a new song.

Because praise that never renews becomes the refrigerator hum. Present, technically. Still running. And entirely unheard, not only by Heaven, but by *you.* You can sing the exact same words over the exact same tune for the exact same reasons for so long that your own soul stops reporting them. The song is still going. Your attention left the building years ago. You are humming a constant your own heart has learned to tune out.

A new song is the cure for that. Not because the old one failed, but because a new song is the only kind you cannot sleep through. It makes you *look* at what is actually true this morning, not the mercy from three years ago that you have long since stopped feeling, but the specific, particular, never-before mercy of *today,* which your habituated heart would otherwise let hum along unheard in the background of a life you have stopped really listening to.

## New every morning

And that is exactly where the Scriptures locate the whole idea, not in a concert hall, but in the ashes.

There is a man in the Hebrew Scriptures sitting in the rubble of a burned city. Everything he loved is gone; the poem he leaves us is one long, unflinching lament, and I love him for refusing to pretend otherwise. And then, right in the middle of the worst grief on the page, he says the thing that has anchored broken people for three thousand years: that the mercies of Yahuah are new every morning. New. Every morning. Not stockpiled. Not issued once and left to age. *New*, a fresh delivery, arriving with the light, before he has done anything to earn the next day at all.

Sit with what that means for your singing. If the mercy is genuinely new each morning, if what Yahuah is doing in your life today is not a rerun but a first, then yesterday's song, however true it was yesterday, is always going to arrive a little late. It was written to a mercy that has already passed. Today's mercy has never been sung to. It is standing there, brand new, waiting for a voice that has actually noticed it.

That is what a new song is. It is not more impressive praise. It is praise that showed up *this morning,* awake, and looked directly at what is true *now.*

## You don't need new words

Here is where I want to be tender, because I can feel some of you already tightening. *I am not a songwriter. I have no new words. The old ones are all I have, and now you are telling me even those have gone stale.*

That is not what I am telling you. Hear me carefully.

A new song does not mean a new composition. Most of the newest songs ever sung to God had no fresh words in them at all. When the newness God is after is not the vocabulary. It is the *heart that is present in it.* You can sing the oldest hymn ever written and make it new, if you bring today's actual, unrepeated self to it, this morning's real fear, this morning's real gratitude, the specific thing that is true right now and was not true the last time your mouth made those shapes. And you can write a brand-new song and have it be dead on arrival, because you assembled it out of borrowed feeling and were not really there.

The newness is you, showing up honest, to a mercy that showed up new.

So I have stopped reaching so quickly for the old handrail songs, not because I have thrown them away. I never will, but because I have learned to ask a different question first. Not *what carried me before,* but *what is true this morning, and have I actually looked at it, and what does my voice want to do about it right now.*

Sometimes the answer is the old song after all, and it lands, because I brought a new heart to it. Sometimes it is three plain sentences I would never dare call a song, sung out an open window to a God whose mercy arrived before my coffee did. Either way, something in me wakes up that had been humming along, unheard, for far too long.

The refrigerator has been running the whole time. His mercy has been arriving new the whole time. The only thing that ever goes quiet is our attention.

So do the one thing that turns it back on. Not a better song. A new one, which only ever meant *a present one.* Look, this morning, at the mercy that has never been sung to. And let it wake you up enough to sing.
