There is one word in the New Testament that the writers did not bother to translate. They left it sitting there in its original language, right in the middle of the Greek, because they knew that translating it would break it.

The word is *Abba.*

It is Aramaic, the everyday language Yeshua actually spoke, the language of the home and the street and the dinner table. And it is the word a child uses for a father. Warm, close, ordinary, the first shape a small mouth makes when it reaches for the person who is supposed to keep it safe. It is not a formal word. It is not a religious word. It is a family word. And the New Testament reaches for it precisely when it wants to show you the most intimate prayer a human being can ever pray.

## The night He used it Himself

The most famous time the word appears, Yeshua is on His face in a garden, and He is in agony.

It is the night before the cross. He knows what is coming. The Gospel of Mark tells us He fell to the ground and prayed, and the word Mark keeps, the word he refuses to smooth over into polite Greek, is *Abba.* *Abba, Father,* He prays, *everything is possible for You, take this cup from Me, yet not what I want, but what You want.*

Look at when He reached for that word. Not on the mountaintop. Not in a moment of triumph with the crowds cheering. He reached for the child's word for father in the worst hour of His life, when He was so overwhelmed He said His soul was crushed to the point of death. That is when *Abba* came out. Which tells you something the tired heart needs to hear: the small, close word for God is not the beginner's prayer you graduate from. It is the word you fall back to when everything else is stripped away and all you have left is the fact that He is your Father.

## Belonging, not performance

We turn worship into a performance so easily, and almost always without meaning to. We start measuring it. Did I pray long enough. Did I say it well enough. Did I feel enough, mean it enough, sound spiritual enough. We approach God the way an employee approaches a review, hoping the work was good enough to be accepted.

*Abba* demolishes all of that.

Because a small child crawling into a father's lap is not performing. The child is not delivering a polished speech to earn the right to be there. The child belongs there. That is the whole basis of the relationship. A child does not say "Father" to impress the father. A child says it because it is true, because this is the one who holds me, and I am the one who is held.

> Worship is not a performance you hope will be accepted. It is a child climbing into the lap of a Father who already decided to keep you.

This is what the word *Abba* does to your whole idea of coming to God. It moves the ground of the relationship off of your performance and onto His fatherhood. You do not come to Him because your prayer was excellent. You come to Him because you are His. And a tired child does not have to be impressive to be welcome home. A tired child just has to come.

## When you have no words left

Here is the part that undoes me every time, and it is the part I most want to put in your hands if you are worn down to nothing this morning.

The apostle Paul writes to believers in Rome and again to believers in Galatia, and in both places he says the astonishing thing: it is the *Spirit* who cries out *Abba, Father.* Not you, mustering up the feeling. The Spirit of God, sent into your heart, crying out that word *through* you. Paul even says, elsewhere, that we do not know what we ought to pray for, that in our weakness the Spirit Himself intercedes for us with groanings too deep for words.

Read that slowly if you are exhausted. There will be seasons. You may be in one now, when you cannot find the words to pray. When you sit down before God and nothing comes. When you are too tired, too numb, too emptied out to construct anything that sounds like a prayer. We tend to treat those seasons as failures, as evidence that our faith has gone thin.

But Paul says something else entirely. He says that in exactly those wordless seasons, the Spirit of God is not silent. The Spirit is inside you, crying *Abba* on your behalf, saying to the Father the one word you could not manage to say yourself. Your inability to pray does not cut you off. It is met, from the inside, by a Spirit who prays the child's word for you when you have none left.

## You did not earn the name

There is one more thing in what Paul says, and it is pure gift. He tells the Galatians that God sent the Spirit of His Son into their hearts *so that* they would cry *Abba*, and that this is the proof they are no longer slaves but sons and daughters, heirs of everything.

A slave does not call the master *Abba.* Only a child does. And you did not promote yourself from slave to child by working hard enough. You were adopted. The name *Abba* was put in your mouth by the Father who chose you, not earned by the servant who impressed Him. It is a gift word. It comes with the family, not with the performance.

So hear the rest in this.

If you are so tired this morning that you cannot pray a proper prayer, if all the eloquence has drained out of you and you have nothing left but a wordless ache. You are not shut out. You are exactly where the whole thing was built to hold you. You do not have to construct anything. You do not have to perform. You can do what a crushed and overwhelmed Yeshua did in the garden, what the Spirit is already doing inside you right now: you can say the one small word. *Abba.* Father. That is enough. That was always enough.

And if you cannot even say that today, if you are past words entirely, then rest, because the Spirit is saying it for you, and the Father has already heard it, and He has already leaned down toward the tired child who is His, whether the child found the word or not.

You belong to Him. Let that be the whole of your worship this morning, and let it be enough.
