I once watched a whole neighborhood save a wedding.

The family did not have much. They had put everything they had into the day, the dress borrowed, the chairs borrowed, the whole barrio pitching in the way it does, and somewhere in the late afternoon, with the dancing already going strong, the word went quietly around that the food was running short and the drink was nearly gone. It is a particular kind of quiet panic, that one. Because it is not really about the food. It is about the shame. In my culture, to run out at your own celebration, to have the joy stop dead in front of everyone because you could not provide, is a wound a family carries for years.

And I watched the neighborhood move. Somebody went home and came back with a pot. Somebody's tía appeared with more of everything. A cousin ran to the store. Quietly, without a word said out loud about what was happening, the whole street closed ranks around that family and refilled the celebration before it could die, so that the couple dancing in the middle never even knew how close their joy had come to running out. Nobody was shamed. The party went on until dawn.

I did not think of it as a holy thing at the time. I thought of it as just what you do. It was only years later, reading slowly through the account of the first miracle Yeshua ever performed, that I sat straight up in my chair. Because He did the exact same thing. And I realized we have badly, badly underestimated what it means that this, *this*, is the thing He chose to do first.

## He could have chosen anything, and He chose the wine

Think about it honestly for a moment.

The first public miracle. The opening act. The very first time He pulls back the curtain and lets a little of the glory show. Of all the things He could have chosen, a healing, a resurrection, a storm stilled, a demon cast out, some thunderous sign to announce who had arrived. He chose to keep a wedding from running out of wine.

The setting is a village celebration. A young couple, a big crowd, days of feasting the way they did it back then, the whole town packed in. And partway through, the wine runs out. Now understand what that meant. It was not an inconvenience. It was a humiliation waiting to happen, the family's joy about to collapse into public shame, the celebration about to die in front of everyone, the young couple's happiest day about to become the story people told for the wrong reason. His mother notices before almost anyone, the way mothers do, and she comes to Him. And she does not even really ask. She just tells Him the trouble: *they have no wine.*

He tells the servants to fill the great stone jars with water, ordinary water, the kind kept for washing, and when they draw it out, it has become wine. And not just enough wine to get by. Not cheap wine to quietly cover the gap. The man in charge of the feast tastes it and is astonished, and says what nobody ever says: usually you serve the good wine first and bring out the cheap stuff once everyone is too far gone to notice, but you have saved the best wine for *now,* for the end, for last. The finest wine of the whole celebration arrived after everyone thought the celebration was already over.

That is the first thing Yeshua ever chose to show the world about Himself. Not power for its own sake. Not a warning. He chose to be the one who keeps the joy from dying, and who, when He steps in, does not merely patch the gap but floods it with more and better than anyone dared hope for.

## God is not embarrassed by your celebration

I need to say this plainly, because somewhere along the way a great many sincere people got the idea that God is fundamentally suspicious of joy. That the parties, the feasting, the dancing, the abundance, the music going till dawn, that all of it is a slightly guilty thing, tolerated at best, and that the truly spiritual life is thin and quiet and a little grim, forever apologizing for any happiness it cannot fully justify.

The first miracle demolishes that. God did not send His Son to a wedding to frown at it. He sent Him there to *rescue* it. To make sure the music kept going and the cups stayed full and the young couple was not shamed. The whole of Heaven's power, unveiled for the first time in human history, went toward keeping a celebration alive.

This is the God my people have always known in their bones, even when the wider church tried to make Him respectable and sad. He is the God of the feast. His own book is full of them, the appointed festivals, the harvest joy, the fatted calf killed for the son who came home, the promise held out at the very end of everything of a wedding supper where the tears are wiped away for good. Joy is not a distraction from God. Joy is *aimed* at God. The celebration is not the opposite of the sacred. On the day He chose to first reveal Himself, the celebration was the sacred thing, and He gave His power to keep it going.

And notice how He did it. He took the water meant for washing, the plain, the functional, the ordinary substance of a working household, and He turned it into the wine of joy. That is His signature. He is forever taking the ordinary stuff of an ordinary life and turning it into gladness nobody saw coming. He does not import joy from somewhere else. He transforms what is already in the room.

## Bring Him your empty jars

So here is what I want to leave with you.

If your celebration has run dry, if the joy that once filled your life has quietly gone, and you are standing in the middle of what should be a feast, ashamed, hoping nobody notices how close to empty the cups have gotten, then you need to know exactly who this God is. He is not standing at the edge of the party with His arms crossed, waiting for it to end so the serious business can begin. He is the one who *saves* parties. His very first act was to keep the joy alive for a family that had run out and could not fix it themselves.

Bring Him your empty jars. That is all His mother did. She did not have a plan, she did not have more wine, she just brought the emptiness to Him and told Him plainly: *they have no wine.* You can do the same. You do not have to manufacture the joy back yourself, or perform a happiness you do not feel, or borrow gladness from the neighbors. You bring Him the empty. You fill the jars with whatever plain water you have left, the ordinary, the functional, the tired remains of your day, and you let Him do the thing only He can do.

And here is the promise buried in the story, and I want you to hold it close: He saves the best wine for last. The finest joy of your whole life may still be ahead of you, waiting on the far side of the moment you were sure the celebration was over. That is not how the world serves it. But it is exactly how He does.

*El vino nuevo llega tarde y llega mejor.* The new wine comes late, and it comes better. Bring Him your empty jars, and let the party go on till dawn.
