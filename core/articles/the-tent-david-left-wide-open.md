There was a curtain, and everyone knew what it meant.

In the tabernacle Moses built, and later in the temple Solomon raised, a heavy woven veil hung across the innermost room, and behind it sat the ark, the place where the presence rested. One man could pass that curtain, one day a year, and not without blood, and not without trembling. The veil was not decoration. It was a sentence, and the sentence was *not yet*. Come this far and no farther. The glory is real, and it is here, and it is not for you to walk up to.

For generations that was the shape of nearness to Yahuah: awe, distance, a curtain doing its solemn work in the dark.

And then, on a hill called Zion, a former shepherd did a thing that should have been unthinkable.

## He built a tent, not a temple

David wanted to bring the ark home. Scripture tells it plainly, after a first attempt ended in death and fear, he brought it up the second time with dancing, with the whole city shouting, with his own royal dignity thrown to the wind in front of everyone. And when it arrived, he did not build a walled sanctuary around it. He did not hire a veil-maker. He pitched a **tent**.

The word for it is *sukkah*, a booth, a temporary shelter, the same kind of fragile, open-sided structure Israel was commanded to live in once a year to remember the wilderness. A sukkah is not a fortress. It is the opposite of a fortress. It is thin walls and a roof you can see stars through, a thing that says *I am passing through, and I am not hiding.*

David set the ark inside that booth on the hilltop. And the account of it never mentions a curtain.

Sit with that. The most dangerous, most holy object in Israel, the thing men had died near, was placed in an open tent on the high ground of the city, and instead of a veil across the front, David stationed singers. He appointed Levites to minister before the ark **continually**, morning and evening and through the hours between, to thank and to praise and to call on the Name. Where the old pattern put a barrier, David put a song. Where the old pattern said *stay back*, this hilltop tent said *come and stand here and lift your voice, and keep lifting it, and do not stop.*

## Praise that never clocked out

I have kept enough night watches on enough mountains to know what it costs to keep something burning when the sensible hours are over. Fires want to die at three in the morning. So does zeal. So does a choir.

But that was the whole genius of what David built. The temple would come later, with its schedules and its seasons and its restored veil. The tent of David was something else, an unbroken sound. Praise with no closing time, going up off that hill while the city slept and while it woke, a fountain of worship that simply did not have an *off*.

And here is the part the curtain-trained heart can barely take in: it was **open**. Up on that mountain, for a season in Israel's story, there was a place where praise ran around the clock and no woven wall stood between the worshipper and the presence. The glory was not behind anything. It was out where the singing was.

> The temple taught the people to fear the nearness. The tent taught them they were invited into it.

Both were true. Both were from Yahuah. But do not miss which one the prophets would reach back for.

## The fallen tent Yahuah swore to raise

Tents fall. Kingdoms fall. That golden hilltop of ceaseless praise did not last, and the day came when the house of David lay in ruins and the sound had long gone quiet.

Then Amos, a herdsman-prophet, spoke a promise into that wreckage. In that day, he said, Yahuah would raise up the fallen *sukkah* of David, the booth, the tent, that same open shelter, and repair its breaches and rebuild it as in the days of old. He did not say Yahuah would rebuild the *fortress* of David or the *throne room* of David. He named the tent. The open one. The one with the endless song and no curtain.

And the reason given was staggering. The tent would be raised again *so that the rest of humanity might seek Yahuah, all the nations who are called by His Name.* The open-access worship was always meant to widen until it had room for everyone.

Centuries after Amos, in a crowded upper room in Jerusalem, the leaders of the young movement argued over an impossible question: could people from the nations, outsiders, non-Jews, the ones the curtain had always kept furthest out, really come in as they were? And a man named Ya'akov stood and settled it by reaching for Amos. This, he said, is what the prophet meant. Yahuah is rebuilding the fallen tent of David *right now*, in front of us, and He is doing it so the nations can come and seek Him.

The tent David pitched was being raised again, not out of cedar and stone, but out of people. Out of a family of the redeemed with the song running through it and the presence in the middle of it and, at last, no veil at all.

## The curtain is torn, sing like it

Here is what the mountain and the manuscripts say together, and it is the best news a tired heart can hear.

When Yeshua died, the temple curtain, that ancient, load-bearing symbol of *not yet*, tore from top to bottom. Not cut by a priest from below. Torn from above, by the only hand tall enough to reach the top of it. The sentence the veil had spoken for centuries was reversed in an afternoon. The way in was opened, and it was opened *toward* us, from the inside.

So the tent of David was never really about David. It was a preview. It was one hilltop, for one season, showing the whole earth what nearness to Yahuah was always going to look like once the curtain came down: praise with no closing hour, access with no barrier, a fragile open shelter full of singing where the glory is not hidden behind anything but stands right out in the middle of the worshipping people.

You do not have to earn your way past a veil this morning. There is no veil. It fell. And the song that David could only keep going with shifts of exhausted Levites is now meant to run through you and through every nation, unbroken, unhidden, wide open on the high ground.

Pitch your tent up there. Leave the door open. And do not, whatever you do, stop singing.
