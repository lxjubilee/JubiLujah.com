The first time it happened to me I thought it had rained.

I had gone up alone, the way I still do when I need the noise of my own life to stop, and I had slept the thin, cold sleep you sleep at altitude, and when I unzipped the tent at dawn the whole world was wet. The grass was bent under water. The rope I had strung between two rocks was strung with beads instead. My hair, where it had touched the fabric, was damp. And there had been no cloud all night. I had watched the stars until they blurred. Not a drop had fallen from the sky.

It was dew. Up high, the cold reaches into the air itself and pulls the water out of it, and it settles, not by falling, exactly, but by gathering, silently, on every surface willing to hold still long enough to be found. You do not hear the dew arrive. You do nothing to earn it. You go to sleep tired and wake up drenched in something you did not make and could not have stopped.

I have never read Psalm 133 the same way since.

## The shortest song about the hardest thing

It is one of the songs the old pilgrims sang going up to the feasts, and it is short, only three verses, and it is about the one thing that is nearly impossible for people to do, which is to live together without tearing each other apart. How good and how pleasant it is, the song says, when brothers dwell together in unity. Anyone who has been part of a family, or a village, or a church, or a band, knows that the song is not describing something common. It is describing a miracle. Unity is not the natural state of people. It is the rare and lovely exception, and everyone who has tasted it knows they did not manufacture it.

So the song reaches for two pictures, and both of them run downhill.

The first is oil. The precious oil poured on the head of Aaron, running down over his beard, running down onto the collar of his robes. It is a strange image to modern ears, but the point is the direction. The oil is poured at the top and travels down, covering everything below it, until the whole man is glistening and fragrant and set apart. Nobody rubs the oil upward. It comes from above and it descends.

The second picture is the one I woke up inside of on that cold morning. Like the dew of Hermon, the song says, coming down on the mountains of Zion. Hermon is the great snow-crowned mountain in the north, the tallest thing in that whole country, and its dew is famous, heavy, generous, the kind that soaks a man to the skin. And the song imagines that abundance settling all the way down onto the lower hills, as though the wealth of the high mountain were being shared with everything beneath it. There, the song says, Yahuah commanded the blessing. There, life that does not end.

## You cannot rub it upward

I have spent a lot of years around people who are exhausted from trying to generate the dew.

They want unity, and they are right to want it, because it really is good and really is pleasant, and where it is missing everything else curdles. So they organize. They strategize. They call the meeting and give the speech and draft the values and hang them on the wall. And none of it is wrong, exactly, but none of it is dew, either, because dew does not come from horizontal effort. It comes down. It is commanded from a height. It settles in the dark on whatever held still long enough to receive it.

This is the quiet, humbling thing the mountain teaches you about blessing, and about worship, and about the love between people that God calls good. You cannot climb to it and you cannot squeeze it out of the air by wanting it badly enough. You can only make yourself the kind of surface it is willing to settle on. You can hold still. You can stay out under the open sky through the cold hours when nothing seems to be happening. You can stop thrashing.

The dew does not fall on the tent that is being packed and unpacked all night by an anxious hand. It falls on the grass that stayed where it was.

## What forms in the dark

Here is what struck me most, out there, and what I keep bringing back down the mountain with me. The dew forms while you sleep. It does its whole silent work in the exact hours when you are least able to help. You contribute nothing but your presence. You wake to find that grace has been at work on you the entire time you were unconscious, gathering on you drop by drop, and by morning you are carrying more water than you knew existed in that dry, cold air.

So much of what is best in a life of worship is made this way. Not in the loud, visible hour, but in the dark and quiet one, when you have run out of things to perform and there is nobody watching and you honestly cannot tell that anything is happening at all. The love that finally holds a fractured family together. The peace that is somehow in the room after a season you thought would break you. The unity that settles, at last, on a people who had been dry to each other for years. You did not build it. You woke up inside it. It came down.

And notice where the song says the blessing is finally commanded. Not on the summit. On the lower mountains. The dew of the high place comes down to soak the ordinary hills where the ordinary people actually live. The blessing of the heights was never meant to stay up on the heights. It was always going to travel downward, to reach the collar, to reach the valley, to reach the ones who never climbed anything.

If you are dry this morning, if you are the one who has been trying to force a togetherness that will not come, working horizontally at something that only ever arrives from above, then let me tell you what the mountain told me. Stop rubbing it upward. Go out under the open sky and hold still. Keep the long, cold, unspectacular watch of a heart that stays put. The blessing that matters is not the kind you seize. It is the kind that settles on you in the night, from a height you did not climb, while you sleep.

You will wake up drenched in something you did not make.

And you will know exactly Who commanded it.
