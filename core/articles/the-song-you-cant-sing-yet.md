Everyone else in the room has their hands up, and she has her arms folded, and she is doing the math on how soon she can leave without anyone noticing.

She used to be the one singing loudest. There was a season the songs came easy, when a chorus could lift her right off the ground. That season ended in a hospital corridor, or a lawyer's office, or a phone call at 4 a.m., and now the band starts and something in her chest just closes like a door.

She has decided this means her faith is failing. That the dryness is a verdict. That everyone around her still has something with God she has misplaced.

Let me tell you what it actually is. It is not a verdict. It is grief, and grief has a sound, and the sound is often silence.

There is a psalm for exactly this, and it is one of the most honest things in the whole book. A people had been marched off into exile, everything they loved burned behind them, and they sat down by a foreign river and wept. And their captors, half-mocking, asked them to perform. Sing us one of those songs you're famous for. Sing us something happy.

And they could not do it. They said so, out loud, and it made it into Scripture: how shall we sing the Lord's song in a strange land? They took their harps down and hung them in the willow branches and sat in silence by the water.

Read that slowly, because here is what nobody tells the woman with her arms folded. That refusal to sing is in the songbook. The silence made it into the worship. God did not strike it out for being insufficiently upbeat. He kept it, harps in the willows and all, because a heart too broken to sing is not a heart He has stopped listening to.

So the first thing to hear is this: you are allowed to stand there mute. You are not required to fake it. The worst worship in the world is a performed feeling you do not have. It is a lie set to a melody, and God is not fooled by it and never asked for it.

Now let me tell you why the door in your chest closed, because it will help.

Music and grief live in almost the same room. A song does not argue with you; it walks straight past your defenses and puts its hand directly on the wound. That is exactly why worship undid you so easily in the good years, and exactly why you cannot bear it now. The very thing that used to lift you is now pressing on a bruise. Your body is not being faithless. It is being tender. It is guarding a place that is still raw.

That is not a failure of worship. That is a fresh injury behaving like a fresh injury.

And here is the turn, the thing I most want the grieving to hear from someone who has sat where words run out.

The Shepherd in the old song does not wait for you at the far side of the valley, arms crossed, tapping His foot, wondering why you are taking so long. He walks through it. With you. At your pace. The valley is not a detour off the path, the valley is the path, and He is on it, and He does not once ask you to hurry up and feel better before He will stay.

He is not standing at the front of a bright room wishing you sounded more like the person beside you. If He is anywhere, He is down in the quiet with you, where the harps are hung up, sitting by the water. And He can carry the song you cannot sing yet. That is not a poem. The whole point of a Shepherd is that He carries what the sheep cannot.

So do not force it. Do not manufacture a hallelujah you do not have to prove something to a God who already knows exactly where you are. The forced song will only teach you that worship is a performance, and it was never that.

Instead, try the small honest thing. When the band plays and your throat closes, do not sing over the grief and do not flee it. Just stand there and let it be true. If a single word comes, let it be a true one. If no word comes, let the silence be your offering. It counted for the exiles by the river, and it counts for you.

And if you can manage nothing else, borrow. Stand in a room where others are singing and let their voices carry the tune your own heart cannot hold up right now. That is not cheating. That is the body of people doing exactly what it is for, the ones who can sing holding the note for the ones who can't, until the ones who can't, can again.

Because you will. The door in your chest is not bricked shut. It is only closed while something heals behind it, and healing keeps its own clock, and the clock is gentler than you are being with yourself.

There is a song you can't sing yet. Not can't ever. Can't *yet*. And the day it comes back, quietly, in the car, or halfway through a chorus you weren't even trying to sing. You will understand that it was never being withheld from you. It was being kept for you, until you were ready to pick it back up.

Until then, hang the harp in the willow if you have to. Sit by the water. He is sitting there too, and He is in no hurry, and the silence between you is not empty.

Take the long way through this. He walked it first, and He will be there after.
