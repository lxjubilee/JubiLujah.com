There is a song at the Passover table that does not know how to end.

It is called *Dayenu*, and if you have never heard a room full of people sing it, I am sorry, because it is one of the most joyful sounds a human family makes all year. The word itself is simple. *Dai* means "enough." *-enu* means "for us." Put them together and you get the whole song in a single breath: **it would have been enough for us.**

And that is the entire trick of the thing. It is a gratitude song built like a staircase, and on every single step the family stops and sings the same three words. The tradition strings together a long list of everything Yahuah did for His people, one gift per stanza, a good long pile of them, and after each one, the whole table sings back, *dayenu, dayenu, it would have been enough.*

Had He brought us out of Egypt but done nothing else, *dayenu*, it would have been enough. Had He split the sea but not led us through on dry ground, *dayenu*. Had He fed us in the wilderness but given us nothing more, *dayenu*. Had He brought us to the mountain but never spoken, *dayenu*. On and on it climbs, stanza after stanza, gift stacked on gift, and after every one the room roars back the same impossible confession: *this one, all by itself, would already have been too much.*

## The math that makes no sense

Now listen, because I want you to feel how strange this song actually is.

*Dayenu* is not true. Not literally. If Yahuah had split the sea but left His people standing on the wrong shore of it, that would **not** have been enough. They would have died on the beach with the water in front of them and the army behind. If He had brought them to the mountain but never spoken a word from it, they would have had a pile of rock and no covenant. The song knows this. Everybody singing it knows this. That is the whole point.

The family is not doing accounting. They are doing worship. They have stopped trying to calculate what they were owed, because the second you start calculating, gratitude dies. What *Dayenu* does instead is take each individual mercy, lift it up all by itself into the light, and say: *look at just this one. Even if the story had stopped right here, even if this were the only kindness we ever got, it would already be more than we could ever deserve.*

That is a people who have completely lost their grip on the idea that God owes them anything.

And do you know what a people like that becomes? **Too thankful to stop counting.** That is the whole theology of the song. Once you truly believe you were owed nothing, then everything, *everything*, becomes a gift so outrageous you have to name it out loud. You cannot just say "thank you for everything" and move on. That is too cheap. You have to slow all the way down and go one at a time. This. *Dayenu.* And this. *Dayenu.* And also this. *Dayenu.* You keep going because you cannot bear to lump the mercies together into a blur when each one deserves its own line.

## Why the counting never runs out

Here is what breaks me open about this song, mi gente.

The reason the family cannot stop counting is not that they are performing. It is that once you start seeing gifts as gifts instead of as your due, you discover there is no bottom to the pile. You reach for what you thought were three or four big rescues, and underneath every big one you find ten small ones you had never even noticed. He brought them out, but who softened Pharaoh's heart to finally let them go? He fed them, but who kept the bread coming *every* morning, and not just the morning they remembered to be grateful? Start pulling on one mercy and a hundred more come up attached to it, like roots.

> A grateful heart does not run out of things to count. It runs out of *breath* before it runs out of reasons.

I think a lot of us pray the exact opposite of *Dayenu*. We pray a song I would call *"lo dai"*, "not enough." God, this is not enough. God, You gave me the job but not the raise. You healed the body but not the marriage. You brought me out of the old bondage but I am not in the promised land *yet*. We stand on step three of the staircase, arms crossed, refusing to sing, because we are staring up at all the steps we do not have. And the whole time, the family beside us is singing their hearts out over the step we are standing on and cannot even see.

*Dayenu* is the cure for that. It does not deny that there is more you long for. It just refuses to let the longing rob you of the joy of what is already in your hands. It takes the one gift you *do* have and says: even this. Even *only* this. *Dayenu.*

## The God who never stops at "enough"

But now here is the turn, and this is where I want you on your feet.

The whole song is the *people* saying "it would have been enough." Stanza after stanza, *we* would have been satisfied here, *we* would have called it more than fair. The family sets the bar of "enough" lower and lower, humbler and humbler.

And Yahuah blows straight past every single one of them.

That is the secret joke buried in the joy. The people keep saying "this would have been enough", and God keeps not stopping. He brings them out; that would have been enough; He does not stop. He splits the sea; that would have been enough; He does not stop. He gives the mountain, the bread, the land, the promises, and He *keeps going*. The song is a running list of all the places God could have reasonably quit being good to them, and refused to.

And then, when the fullness of time came, when a people who kept singing "it would have been enough" had every reason to expect God to finally rest. He gave His own Son. Yeshua. The gift after which there is no next stanza, because there is nothing left in heaven to give. If Yahuah had only made us. *Dayenu.* If He had only spoken to us. *Dayenu.* If He had only forgiven us. *Dayenu.* But He came down. He wore our skin. He took our death. He rose. And there is no honest way to sing *dayenu* after **that**, because "enough" is a word that finally breaks in your mouth.

So sing the old song, familia. Count your mercies one at a time and refuse to blur them. Say *dayenu* over the small bread of this ordinary morning. Say *dayenu* over the step you are standing on. And then let the song turn around on you and realize the beautiful, staggering truth it was always pointing at:

You keep saying it would have been enough. He keeps deciding it isn't. *¡Aleluya!*, the counting is never going to be finished, because the giving never was.
