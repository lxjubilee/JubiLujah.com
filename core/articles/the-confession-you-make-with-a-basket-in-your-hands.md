There is a command tucked into the feasts that I think is one of the most beautiful things Yahuah ever asked of ordinary people, and almost no one talks about it. It is not a command to feel something, or believe something, or even give something. It is a command to *say something out loud.*

Here is the scene. The harvest has come in. An Israelite takes the very first and best of what his ground produced, the *firstfruits*, puts it in a basket, and carries it up to the place where Yahuah has set His name. He hands the basket to the priest. And then, before he goes home, he has to stop and recite a speech. Out loud. In front of the priest, in front of Yahuah, in his own voice.

And the speech does not begin where you would expect. It does not begin with thanks for the fruit. It begins with the single strangest opening line in all the feasts:

*"A wandering Aramean was my father."*

I cannot stop turning that over. Of all the ways to open a thank-you, Yahuah put *that* in their mouths. Let me tell you why I think it is one of the tenderest commands in the whole Book.

## Start with the wandering, not the harvest

Think about what that first line is doing.

A man is standing there holding a basket overflowing with the goodness of his own land, his fields, his labor, his harvest, the fruit of a home that is *his*. And the very first words Yahuah requires from his mouth are a confession that he did not start here. That his father was a wanderer. Homeless. A man with no land, drifting between places, belonging to no soil at all.

The recital, as the tradition kept it, goes on from there to tell the whole arc in a few sentences: how that wandering father went down to Egypt, few in number; how his descendants became a great people there but were made slaves, worked hard and treated harshly; how they cried out to Yahuah, and He heard, and He brought them out with a strong hand and an outstretched arm, and led them to a good land, *this* land, and *now*, look, here is its first fruit.

Do you see the shape of it? Wandering to slavery to crying out to rescue to home to *harvest in my hands*. He is required to sing his entire rescue-story out loud, from homeless to home, before he is allowed to simply say thank you.

## Why the story had to be spoken and not just remembered

Yahuah could have let a grateful man simply feel grateful in his heart. But He didn't. He commanded the *speech*. He made the man put the whole story into his own mouth, in his own voice, every year.

I think about this constantly, because it is exactly what I believe about worship. There is something that happens when a truth leaves the quiet of your chest and travels out through your voice into the air that does *not* happen when it stays inside. Speaking it, singing it, makes it real in a way that merely believing it never quite does. Your own ears have to hear your own mouth say what God has done. That is not a formality. That is how the story gets pressed down deep enough to hold.

> A rescue you only remember silently will slowly shrink. A rescue you are made to say out loud, in your own voice, year after year, keeps its full size.

And notice, the man is not reciting someone else's rescue as a spectator. He says *my* father, *we* cried out, *He* brought *us* out. The story from centuries before is put in first-person present. He was not there at the Exodus. But holding that basket, he says it as if the strong hand reached down for *him*, because, in truth, it did. He is standing in the good land *because* of a rescue he personally never saw. The recital forces him to own it.

## The danger the command was guarding against

Here is the part that quietly convicts me.

Why would you need a *command* to make a man say where he came from? Because of what a full basket does to the human heart. When your hands are finally full, when the harvest is in, the home is built, the land is yours, the great temptation is to forget the wandering. To look at the abundance and quietly begin to believe you were always meant to have it. That you built it. That it is simply *yours*.

Yahuah, who knows us all the way down, guarded against that by tying the confession to the exact moment of greatest abundance. *At the height of your harvest, say out loud that your father had nothing.* You cannot hold the firstfruits and forget the wandering in the same breath. The command will not let the blessing make you forget the rescue.

I think whole Christian lives quietly stop obeying this. Not on purpose. We just get comfortable. The basket fills, and the old story, where I came from, what I was pulled out of, the hand that reached down when I was still wandering, gets softer and softer until we can barely hear it anymore. And a gratitude that forgets the pit is only ever one hard season away from bitterness.

## The Firstfruits who came with His own story

And now the line that lifts the whole thing into glory.

The feast of firstfruits was never only about grain. Firstfruits are, by their nature, a *promise*, the first sheaf that guarantees a whole harvest is coming behind it. And Scripture takes that word and hands it, tenderly, to Yeshua. Risen from the dead, He is called the *firstfruits*, the first up out of the grave, the guarantee that a whole harvest of the redeemed is coming behind Him.

So look again at the man with his basket, reciting his rescue. Wandering, slavery, the cry, the strong hand, home. It is *your* story too, in a deeper key. You were the wanderer with no land and no claim. You were in a slavery you could not free yourself from. You cried out. And a strong hand and an outstretched arm, nailed, this time, reached down and brought you out and is bringing you home. And now Yeshua stands as the Firstfruits lifted before the Father, the guarantee that where He has gone, you are going too.

## So say it out loud

Here is my whole heart in this.

Do not let your basket make you quiet. Whatever good is in your hands this season, and I hope your hands are full, the command still stands: stop, and say your rescue-story out loud. Begin where the recital begins, at the wandering. Name what you were before He reached for you. Name the slavery. Name the cry. Name the strong hand. And then, holding whatever firstfruit He has given you, say thank you, out loud, in your own voice, so your own ears can hear it.

You are not a spectator of someone else's Exodus. A wandering one was your father too, and a strong hand brought *you* out, and you are standing in the good land only because of a rescue you never earned and did not see.

Sing it. From wandering to home. That is the confession the full basket was always meant to carry.
