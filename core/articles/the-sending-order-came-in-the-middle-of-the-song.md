The most consequential travel order in the history of the church was not issued from a throne or a boardroom. It came in the middle of a worship service, while the singers were singing, and it changed the map of the world.

The city was Antioch, a loud, crowded, cosmopolitan port up in Syria, the third-largest city of the empire, a place where a dozen languages fought for room in the same market. It was there, the record notes almost in passing, that the followers of Yeshua were first called by a new name, *Christians*, a nickname pinned on them by outsiders who did not have a category for what they were seeing. Something had appeared in that city that the old labels could not hold. Something worth naming from scratch.

## A leadership that should not have worked

Before we get to the song, look hard at who was leading this church, because the list is one of the quiet miracles of the whole book and almost everyone reads past it.

There were five of them named as the prophets and teachers of Antioch, and no committee on earth would have assembled this room. There was Barnabas, a Levite from Cyprus, the generous one, the encourager. There was Simeon, who was also called Niger, a name that means *the black one*, an African, a man of the deep south of the known world. There was Lucius from Cyrene, up the North African coast. There was Manaen, who had been brought up in the household of Herod the tetrarch, a man raised in the courts of power, foster-brother to a king, aristocracy. And there was Saul, the reformed persecutor, the fierce ex-Pharisee still learning to be trusted.

Read that room again. A Levite and a African and a North African and a palace-raised nobleman and a converted zealot. Different nations, different colors, different classes, different accents, men whose fathers would never have eaten at the same table, leading one church together in one Syrian city. In a world sorted ruthlessly by blood and rank, this was not supposed to be possible. It was, all by itself, a sermon louder than anything they could have preached: that the song had begun to gather people the old world kept carefully apart, and to seat them, as equals, around one fire.

That is the thing worship does before it ever does anything else. It integrates. It puts the palace and the margins in the same choir and asks them to keep time together. And it was out of exactly that kind of room, mixed, unlikely, fused by praise into something that had no earthly right to hold, that Heaven chose to launch its reach toward the nations that had never heard.

## The Spirit spoke while they were worshipping

Here is the sentence I cannot get past, and I want you to feel the timing of it.

The record says that *while they were worshipping the Lord and fasting*, the Holy Spirit said: *set apart for me Barnabas and Saul for the work to which I have called them.*

Notice when it came. Not while they were strategizing. Not in a planning session with maps of the empire spread on a table and a debate about where the mission field most needed reinforcements. The word came while they were worshipping, mid-service, in the thick of praise and fasting, when their attention was fixed on God and not on logistics at all. The greatest mobilization order the young church had yet received arrived inside an act of worship, addressed to a room that was not asking for a project but was simply, hungrily, seeking the face of God.

I think we have the sequence backwards most of the time. We treat worship as the warm-up and the strategy as the substance, sing a few songs to soften the room, then get down to the real business of deciding what to do. Antioch shows the opposite. The worship *was* the business. The song was not the preamble to hearing from God; the song was the room He chose to speak into. When they were most absorbed in loving Him, He trusted them with the sending. The direction of the whole Gentile mission, Cyprus, Asia Minor, eventually the roads that would carry the name to the ends of the empire, dropped into a room that was mid-praise and listening.

And look at how carefully they held it. They did not seize the moment and rush. They fasted more, prayed, laid their hands on the two men, and released them. Then, the record says, being sent out by the Holy Spirit, they went down to the port and sailed. The church sent them, and the Spirit sent them, in the same breath, because in a room like that the two are not different things. Human hands on two shoulders, and the wind of God under one small ship, at the same time.

## The base was a worshipping church, not a fortress

I have spent my working years carrying songs into the guarded countries, Arabic, Turkish, Romanian, the tongues of frontiers where the church, if it exists at all, meets quietly and pays for it. And Antioch is the pattern I keep coming back to, because it tells you what actually sends a gospel across a hostile sea and what does not.

What sends it is not a fortress. It is not a wealthy, homogenous, comfortable congregation that has perfected its own service and pulled up the drawbridge. What sent the first mission was a fasting, worshipping, gloriously mismatched church that had already crossed one border inside its own leadership before it ever crossed a border by ship. They had learned to be one room out of many nations. So when the Spirit said *go to the nations*, it was not asking them to do something foreign to them. It was asking them to keep doing outbound what they had already learned to do inbound. The choir that had gathered the palace and the margins was simply told to widen.

That is why I do not believe the frontier is reached by strategy alone, however good the strategy. It is reached by worshipping communities so full and so unlikely that the sending word can find them mid-song. The mission does not begin when we finally get organized. It begins when a room of praising people, mixed past the point of comfort, gets quiet enough in the middle of the singing to hear the Spirit say a name.

## Stay in the room long enough to be sent

So here is what I would leave with you, wherever you are reading this.

If you are waiting for a burning-bush clarity about where God wants you to go, notice where the clarity came at Antioch. It came to people who were already worshipping, not people waiting to be told what to do before they would bother to praise, but people praising first and being told second. The sending order is far more likely to reach you in the middle of the song than in the middle of your planning. So stay in the room. Keep the fast. Keep loving Him with the mismatched people He has seated beside you, the ones your background would have kept at a distance. Let the worship do its first work of making you one.

And do not be surprised if, some ordinary week, in the thick of the singing, the Spirit says a name, maybe even yours, and a small ship you had not noticed turns out to be waiting at the harbor. The greatest mission the church ever launched began exactly there: in a worship service, in a room too mixed to make sense, in the middle of the song.
