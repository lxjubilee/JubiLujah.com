The most tired man I ever pastored led worship the Sunday before he disappeared for a year.

He was gifted the way that draws a crowd, and he gave everything he had, week after week, to a room that mostly took it. He ran the rehearsals and mixed the tracks and prayed with the ones who came forward and drove the van and stayed late to coil the cables no one else knew how to coil. And then one Monday he simply could not get out of the car in the church parking lot. He sat there with his hands on the wheel and wept, not from any sin and not from any crisis anyone could name, but from the plain arithmetic of a man who had been pouring out longer than he had been poured into. When he finally called me, the thing he said was the thing the exhausted always say. He said he thought maybe he was just done. Maybe God was finished with him. Maybe the flatness meant the fire had gone out for good.

I have thought about him every time I read the story of the prophet under the broom tree, because the Scriptures put those two things side by side in a way we usually rush past.

Elijah had just come off the greatest day of his life. On Mount Carmel he had stood alone against hundreds and watched the fire of Heaven fall on a soaked altar in front of a whole nation. If any man ever earned the right to feel unstoppable, it was him, that afternoon. And then a queen sent word that she would have his life by morning, and something in the prophet snapped clean through. He ran. He ran a full day into the wilderness, past the edge of anywhere, and he sat down under a lonely broom tree in the waste and asked God to kill him. It is enough, he said. Take my life. I am no better than my fathers. This is the same man who had called fire from the sky the day before. Triumph did not immunize him. If anything, it set him up for the fall, the way the biggest Sundays so often set up the emptiest Mondays.

Now watch what Heaven does, because this is the part the busy church keeps skipping.

God does not answer the death wish with a rebuke. He does not say, how dare you, after Carmel. He does not send a word of correction or a fresh assignment or a strongly worded reminder of everything the prophet still had left to do. He sends an angel. And the angel does not preach. The angel does not even talk, at first, beyond the plainest instruction. The angel lets the exhausted man sleep. And then the angel touches him, gently, and there is a cake baked on hot stones and a jar of water waiting, and the angel says only, get up and eat. And Elijah eats, and lies back down, and sleeps again. And the angel comes a second time, and touches him a second time, and the words this time are so tender I can hardly read them without stopping: get up and eat, because the journey is too great for you.

Not, the journey is too great, so try harder. Not, the mission demands more of you. Because the journey is too great for you, so here is food, and here is rest, and I will not send you one step until you have had both.

Heaven fed him before Heaven fixed him. God tended the body before God addressed the soul. Sleep first. Bread first. Water first. The still small voice, the great turning point, the fresh commission at the mountain, all of that comes later in the story, days later, after the prophet has walked in the strength of that food across a wilderness. But it comes after. First the God who commands armies knelt down in a wasteland and made sure His most useful servant got a nap and a warm meal.

I want to say something to the church about this, and I want to say it plainly, because we have built a whole culture that would have handled Elijah exactly wrong. We would have scheduled him. We would have reminded him of his platform and his gifting and the people counting on him. We would have called his collapse a problem of discipline or vision or faith, and we would have prescribed more, more prayer, more push, more output, to a man who was drowning precisely from too much of all three. We have made tiredness into a kind of failure, a thing to be ashamed of and prayed away, when the Scriptures treat it as a simple human fact that God Himself is unhurried and unbothered to meet with food and sleep.

Because here is the truth we forget in a ministry culture addicted to spectacle: you are not a machine that has stalled. You are a body that God made, with limits God built on purpose, and those limits are not the enemy of your calling. They are the shape of it. The One who made you does not resent that you need to sleep. He designed the sleep. He is not tapping His foot while your body recovers, wishing you were more like the tireless angels. He made you unlike the angels on purpose, and He sat down in the dirt beside a broken prophet and honored the difference with a meal.

So let me speak to the one this is actually for, the worship leader who cannot get out of the car, the volunteer running on fumes, the pastor who read this far already composing the reasons it does not apply to him. Maybe what you need this week is not a better strategy or a renewed vision or one more push through the wall. Maybe what you need is the thing the angel gave first. Maybe you are supposed to lay the instrument down and sleep. Maybe the most spiritual act available to you right now is a real meal and a full night and a whole day when no one needs anything from you. That is not you quitting on God. That is you letting God do for you what He did for the prophet He was not remotely finished with.

And here is the gentleness underneath all of it, the thing I most want you to carry away. When you are too empty even to sing, when the flatness has gone so deep that praise feels like a foreign language and you are half-convinced Heaven has finally lost patience with you, that is not the moment God draws back. That is the moment He draws near, and He comes not with a demand but with bread. He is the Shepherd who makes His sheep lie down in green pasture; He does not drive them till they drop. The rest is not the interruption of the work. On the days the journey is too great for you, the rest is the work, and He Himself will serve it to you with His own hand.

The man in the parking lot came back, in time, after a long season of doing almost nothing. And the first Sunday he sang again, he told me it was the quietest he had ever felt while singing, and the truest. He had learned the thing the broom tree teaches. The God he served was never after his output. He was always, all along, after him, rested, fed, and finally still enough to be found.

Get up and eat. The journey is too great for you. And the One who says so is not sending you out hungry. He is sitting down beside you first.
