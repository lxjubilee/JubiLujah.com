Most of us know the Magnificat. A young woman in an obscure town, told she will carry the Messiah, opens her mouth and pours out one of the most beautiful songs in all of Scripture, *my soul magnifies the Lord.* He scatters the proud. He topples the mighty from their thrones. He lifts the lowly and fills the hungry and sends the rich away empty.

What most of us do not know is that Mary was singing a **cover**.

A thousand years earlier, another woman stood up and sang almost the same song, the same reversals, the same toppled strong and lifted low, the same fierce, holy joy. Her name was Hannah. And when you set her song beside Mary's, side by side, the family resemblance is unmistakable. Mary, steeped in the Scriptures of her people, reached back across ten centuries and picked up a melody a heartbroken woman had left for her.

I cannot stop thinking about that. The most famous song of the New Testament was first sung by a woman the whole town had written off.

## The song that started before the answer did

Here is what you have to understand about where Hannah's song came from, because it is the whole scandal of it.

Hannah was barren, in a time and place where that word was a verdict on your worth. And she was not left in peace about it, the other wife in her household, who had children, provoked her, needled her, made a sport of the one wound Hannah could not heal. Year after year. Hannah wept until she could not eat.

She went up to the house of Yahuah at Shiloh, and she prayed the way you pray when you have run out of composure. Her lips moved, but no sound came out. She was so far into her grief that the old priest watching her assumed she was drunk and told her to sober up. She had to explain to him that she was not drunk. She was pouring out her soul before Yahuah. (It is a beautiful footnote of history that Israel later looked back at that scene, lips moving, no voice, the whole heart poured out in silence, as a picture of how prayer is meant to be prayed. That is tradition remembering her, not Scripture commanding it. But I think the tradition saw something true.)

Now here is the part that undoes me.

The song of triumph in the next chapter, the fierce one, the prophetic one, Hannah did not sing it because life had finally become easy. The child came, yes. But she sang it while handing that only son *back*, leaving him at Shiloh to be raised by the very priest who had misjudged her, walking home to a house where her rival still lived. Her circumstances were nowhere near tidy. The mockery had not been fully answered. And she stood up anyway and sang the biggest song of her life.

> Her praise did not wait for the ending. It began in the anguish, in the silence where only her lips moved, and it grew into a song too large for the room she was standing in.

## A song bigger than her own story

If Hannah's song were only about a baby, it would be sweet, and small, and we would have forgotten it. It is not small.

Listen to what she actually sings. *There is none holy like Yahuah; there is no Rock like our God.* The word she reaches for is *tsur*, a rock, a crag, an immovable place to stand. A woman who had been standing on nothing for years names Yahuah her unshakeable ground.

And then she says, *my horn is exalted in Yahuah.* The horn, *qeren*, is the ancient image for strength, for lifted-up power, the way an animal carries its head high and unafraid. The woman who came in with her head bowed by shame sings that her strength has been lifted where everyone can see it.

But she does not stop at her own reversal. She widens out, and this is where it becomes prophecy. The bows of the mighty are broken, she sings, and the stumbling are girded with strength. The full sell themselves for bread, and the hungry hunger no more. Yahuah brings down to the grave and raises up; He lifts the poor from the dust and the needy from the ash-heap and seats them with princes. One barren woman's answered prayer becomes, in her mouth, the whole pattern of how God runs His universe: **He lifts the low and He topples the high.**

And then her song does something it has no business doing. In the very last line she prophesies a *king*, *He will give strength to His king and exalt the horn of His anointed*, when Israel had no king yet. There was no throne. There was no crown. Hannah, a private woman singing over a weaning child, reaches forward into a future that did not exist and names the Anointed One, the *Messiach*, before there was anyone to wear the word.

That is why Mary could pick this song up and it fit her perfectly. Because Hannah had not been singing only about herself the whole time. She had been singing about the God who does exactly this, who chooses the overlooked woman, the empty womb, the ash-heap, the nobody, and lifts her up to announce the way the whole story is going to end. Hannah sang the shape of the gospel a thousand years early, and Mary, carrying that gospel in her own body, sang it back.

## Praise is the seed you plant in the anguish

Here is what I want you to carry out of this.

Hannah teaches us that the fiercest praise is not grown in the good season. It is grown in the barren one. The song that would echo down ten centuries and land in the mouth of the mother of the Messiah was *seeded* in a woman weeping so hard she could not speak, in a sanctuary where the only witness misread her as a drunk. The praise did not come *after* the pain as a reward. It grew *up through* the pain, like something green forcing its way through cracked ground.

And notice. She did not sing to convince herself. She sang to *testify*. She had met the God who lifts the low, in the one place she had been low the longest, and once you have met Him there, you cannot keep the song in. It gets bigger than your own story. It starts prophesying over other people's ash-heaps. It starts announcing the King.

## So sing from where you actually are

If you are in a barren season, a prayer unanswered so long you have stopped saying it out loud, a wound the mockers keep finding, do not wait for the ending to sing.

Hannah didn't. She poured out her soul in silence, and then she stood up and sang a song so true it outlived her, so wide it prophesied a King, so trustworthy that the mother of Yeshua would one day borrow it word for word. Her circumstances were not finished when she sang. But her God was already the Rock, already the Lifter of the low, already the One who was going to turn the whole world right-side up.

He still is. And the song you sing from inside your barren years, before the mockery ends, before the answer comes, may turn out to be the truest one you ever sing.

Somebody a thousand years from now might just be singing it back.
