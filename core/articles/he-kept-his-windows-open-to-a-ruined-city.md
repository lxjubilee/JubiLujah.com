Everyone remembers the lions. I want to talk about the window, because the window is where the whole thing was actually decided, long before anyone smelled the den.

Daniel is an old man by now. He has served in the courts of an empire since he was a boy carried off into exile, and he has outlasted kings. Under Darius he has risen so high, and served so cleanly, that the men below him cannot find a single crack of corruption to pry at. So they go after the one thing they know they can use against him: his God. They flatter the king into signing an unchangeable decree. For thirty days, no one in the realm may pray to any god or any man except the king himself. The penalty is the lions' den. It is a trap built precisely around Daniel's devotion, and everyone in the palace knows it.

Now here is the sentence that undoes me every time. When Daniel learned that the document had been signed, he went to his house, and the account pauses to tell us his windows were open toward Jerusalem, and he got down on his knees three times a day and prayed and gave thanks, *as he had done previously.*

As he had done previously. That is the hinge of the entire story. Daniel does not gather a resistance. He does not march on the palace or make a speech about religious freedom. He does not, on the other hand, quietly move his prayers to the cellar for a month until the decree blows over. Nobody would have blamed him, he could have kept praying in secret and kept his life. He does neither. He simply goes home and does the exact thing he has always done, at the exact times he has always done it, with the windows open exactly as wide as they always were. The law changed. Daniel did not. His defiance was not a new act of protest. His defiance was his ordinary faithfulness, continued out loud, on schedule, in plain view, as if the decree simply did not have the authority to reach the thing that mattered most.

## Windows toward a city that wasn't there

But look at what those open windows were pointed at, because this is the part that turns the story from stubbornness into something closer to a song.

The windows opened toward Jerusalem. And Jerusalem, in Daniel's lifetime, was a ruin. Burned. Broken down. The temple leveled, the walls thrown over, the city where God had put His name reduced to rubble by the very empire Daniel now served in. When Daniel knelt and faced Jerusalem three times a day, he was not facing a glorious skyline. He was facing a memory. He was aiming his whole body, morning and midday and evening, at a wrecked city hundreds of miles away that gave him nothing back to look at but the ache of it.

That is faith believing in what it cannot see. Daniel prayed toward a promise, not a picture. Solomon had prayed at the temple's dedication that when the people were carried far away, they would turn and pray toward this place, and God would hear from heaven. So Daniel turned. Toward a house that was no longer standing, on the strength of a word that said it would matter anyway. He worshiped toward the not-yet. His windows framed a broken city and he treated it like the throne room of God, because he believed the God of that city had not moved out just because the walls came down.

I know something about praying toward a home you can only see with your eyes closed. Anyone who has carried a faith across borders knows it, the way you orient your heart toward a place, a people, a promise that the visible facts have written off. Daniel's open window is the posture of every believer who keeps aiming their worship at what God said instead of what the news reports. You face the ruin and you give thanks anyway, because you are not praying to the rubble. You are praying to the One who is going to rebuild it.

## The worship they could not make him whisper

They caught him, of course. That was always the plan. They came to his house and there he was, on his knees, windows open, doing the thing that was now a crime. And they dragged the whole matter before the king, who realized far too late that he had been played, and who spent a sleepless night unable to unsign the law that condemned the best man in his kingdom.

And Daniel went into the den. And Yahuah shut the mouths of the lions, and in the morning the old man walked out without a scratch, and the king who had signed the death decree ended up signing a new one, that in all his dominion, people should tremble before the God of Daniel, the living God whose kingdom does not end. The worship they tried to outlaw ended up preached across an empire by the mouth of the very king who had banned it. That is what happens when you refuse to whisper. The song you would not take underground gets amplified by the same authorities who tried to silence it.

## For the ones tempted to close the window

Let me bring this close, because most of us will never face a den, but every one of us faces the window.

There is a constant, low pressure on a believer to make their worship quieter, more private, more manageable, to move it to the cellar where it will not cost anything. The pressure rarely comes as a dramatic decree. It comes as a thousand small suggestions that faith is something you do discreetly, on your own time, with the windows shut, where it will not embarrass anyone. And the temptation is not usually to quit. It is to keep believing while learning to hide it. To keep the flame and close the shutters.

Daniel's whole witness is one open window against that pressure. Not louder than usual, just as loud as always, unchanged, on schedule, aimed at the promise. He did not need a new act of courage the day the law was signed. He needed the ordinary courage of continuing, out loud, the thing he had always done. That is the defiance available to you and me: not a protest, but a routine of praise so settled that no decree can find the seam in it.

So keep the window open. Kneel at the times you always kneel. Face the ruined city, the broken promise, the wrecked situation, the home you can only see with your eyes closed, and give thanks toward it anyway, because you are not worshiping the rubble. You are worshiping the God who is coming to rebuild it, and He has never once been stopped by a shut mouth or an open den. Do not whisper. The One you are facing is worth being caught in the act of loving.
