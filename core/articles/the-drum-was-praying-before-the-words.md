Before I knew a single word of any prayer, I knew the beat.

I could keep the clave before I could read. My hand knew where the accent fell, that off-kilter pattern that runs like a heartbeat under so much of my people's music, years before I could have told you what it was called or why it mattered. My mother says I was patting it out on the side of the crib. I believe her, because I have watched every child in my family do the same thing: the words come late, but the rhythm is there almost from the beginning, wired in, waiting.

And I have come to believe that this is not a small or accidental thing. I think God put the beat in us before the words on purpose. I think there is a kind of praise the body can offer that the mouth simply cannot, and that a whole tradition raised on stiff, seated, neck-up worship has been quietly starving for it without knowing what was missing.

## The oldest instrument is the one you were born holding

Here is something worth sitting with. The very first rhythm any of us ever heard, we heard from the inside. Before light, before language, before a single thought, a heartbeat. Your mother's, steady and close, the metronome of the whole world you lived in for nine months. We are, all of us, rhythmic creatures before we are anything else. We were kept alive by a beat before we ever drew a breath of our own.

So when the drum starts, and something in you answers before you have decided anything, that is not a shallow response. That is the deepest and oldest part of you recognizing a language it spoke before it spoke any other. The beat is not beneath the words. The beat came *first*.

And the Scriptures, which we have somehow managed to read as though God only wanted our minds, are full of bodies. The final Psalm, the great crescendo the whole book has been climbing toward, does not say *think about God quietly in a chair*. It says praise Him with the trumpet, with the harp, with the tambourine and *dancing*, with strings and pipe, with loud clashing cymbals, with the loud crashing cymbals, as if once was not enough to say it. It ends with everything that has breath. That is not a quiet room. That is a body, all the way in, hands and feet and lungs, holding nothing back.

Go back to the sea again and you find Miriam with a tambourine in her hand and all the women behind her with tambourines *and dancing*. Go to the day the ark came home and you find the king himself leaping and whirling down the public street with all his might, so undignified his wife was ashamed of him, and God was not. God was not ashamed of the dancing king. It was the watching, sneering wife whom the account leaves empty. Heaven took the side of the body that praised over the mind that judged it.

## What the mouth cannot say, the body can

I want to be careful, because words matter and I am not against them. The mouth is a glorious instrument. But the mouth has a limit, and you have felt it.

There are things too big for a sentence. Real joy is one of them. Have you ever tried to *say* how glad you were? The words come out small and flat next to the size of the gladness. But a body does not have that problem. When the joy is too big for the sentence, the hands come up on their own. The head goes back. The feet start to move. The hips sway before the brain has signed off on any of it. And that motion says the thing the sentence could not. It says *this joy is bigger than my words, so I am going to hand it over with my whole body instead.*

This is why I refuse to be embarrassed by embodied worship, and why I grieve a little for traditions that trained it out of their people. When my grandmother swayed with her eyes shut, she was not performing and she was not out of control. She was saying, with the one instrument she had owned since before she could talk, a thing about the goodness of God that no sentence in any language could have carried. Her body was praying. It had been praying since before she had the words, and it never forgot how.

And I will tell you plainly what I think the resistance to this really is, underneath. It is not reverence. We tell ourselves it is reverence, that stillness is holier, that a moving body is somehow less serious about God. But often it is not reverence at all. It is *pride*. It is the fear of looking foolish. It is the sneering wife at the window, worried about dignity, while the king dances his heart out before the Lord. The mind that will not let the body praise is very often not being holy. It is being self-conscious. And self-consciousness is just pride that has learned to sit still.

## Let the body pray, and let it be loud

So here is my invitation, and I mean it for the stiff and the shy as much as for anyone.

Stop making your body wait for your mind to give it permission. That order is backwards. The mind is slow and cautious and worried about the neighbors. The body is honest. When the beat rises and something in you wants to move, to clap, to sway, to lift a hand, to let your feet believe the good news before your head has finished analyzing it, that is not a distraction from worship. That may be the truest worship in the room. The child patting the clave on the crib rail had it right before any of us confused ourselves with dignity.

And do not be afraid of it being loud. We have made *quiet* a synonym for *reverent*, and the Bible simply does not agree. The Bible ends its songbook with clashing cymbals and everything that has breath. There is a hush that is holy. I know it, I love it, there are nights the only right response is silence. But there is also a shout that is holy, a clap that is holy, a drum that is holy, a dance down the middle of the street that Heaven applauds while the watchers at the window sniff. Do not let anyone convince you that God only accepts the neck-up, seated, silent version. He made the heartbeat. He wired the rhythm in before the words. He is not offended by the body He designed doing exactly what He built it to do.

*Corazón latino*, the Latin heart, did not invent embodied praise. We just never let the world talk us out of it. And there is room at that party for you, whoever you are, whatever tradition trained your hands to stay in your lap. The drum was praying before you had the words. It still is. Let your body join it. And discover, maybe for the first time, how much of the praise inside you has been waiting, not for better words, but for permission to finally move.
