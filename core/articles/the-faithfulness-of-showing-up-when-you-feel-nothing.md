There is a woman in the fourth pew who has not felt much of anything in church for a long time, and she comes anyway.

I know this because she told me, one Tuesday, without any drama in her voice. She is not in crisis. She has not lost her faith. She simply said, plainly, that she cannot remember the last time a song moved her the way it used to, back when she was young and the feelings came easy and worship felt like standing under a waterfall. Now she opens the same songbook to the same songs she has sung for forty years, and mostly she feels the pew under her and the ache in her knees and a kind of quiet flatness where the fire used to be. And still, every single week, she is there in the fourth pew, on time, singing.

I have thought about her more than almost anyone in my ministry. Because I believe she has stumbled onto something about God that the people chasing the waterfall have not learned yet, and may never learn until their feelings finally fail them too.

We have made a quiet mistake in the modern church, and it is a sincere one, so it is hard to see. We have taught people, without quite meaning to, that the feeling is the worship. That if the song does not move you, something has gone wrong, with the band, with the mix, with your own dry heart, and the honest thing to do would be to wait until the feeling comes back before you bother singing again. We have made emotion the evidence and the engine of the whole thing. And so when the feeling goes flat, as every feeling eventually does, people conclude that the worship has gone flat with it, and they stay home, waiting for a fire that stays away longer the more they wait.

The woman in the fourth pew never got that memo. She belongs to an older understanding, that showing up is itself the act of worship, that the feeling was always a guest and never the host, and that the deepest thing you can say to God is not "I feel You this morning" but "I am here again, whether I feel You or not, because You are worth coming to even on the mornings You seem far off."

The Scriptures give us a portrait of exactly this, and her name was Anna. She was a widow of great age, eighty-four years, the Gospel of Luke tells us, most of them alone. And in all those decades she did one thing. She did not depart from the temple. She was there, worshiping with fasting and prayer, night and day, year after ordinary year. The Scripture does not tell us she felt something magnificent every day. It does not report waterfalls. It reports faithfulness, the sheer, unglamorous accumulation of a woman who kept showing up to worship a God who, for most of those years, gave her no spectacular sign that He had noticed.

And then came one particular day. An ordinary couple carried an eight-day-old baby into the temple to do what the law required, and Anna, who was simply there because she was always there, came up at that very hour, and she saw. She recognized the child. Decades of showing up when nothing seemed to be happening had trained her eyes to see the one moment when everything was. She began to give thanks, and to speak of Him to everyone who was waiting. If Anna had worshiped only on the days she felt like it, she would have missed it. The presence she had waited eighty-four years for walked in on a Tuesday, and she was there, because she was always there.

That is the secret hidden inside the dry years. The feeling is not the point, and it was never a reliable one. Feelings are weather. They come and they go and they answer to a hundred things that have nothing to do with God, your sleep, your blood sugar, the argument in the car on the way in, the season of life you happen to be crossing. If your worship rises and falls with your weather, you will worship a God who seems present in your good moods and absent in your hard ones, when in truth He never moved an inch. The woman in the fourth pew is not worshiping less than the young man with his hands in the air and tears on his face. She may be worshiping more, because she is doing it on no fuel but obedience and love, which is the only fuel that lasts a lifetime.

I want to be careful here, because I am not against the feeling. I have wept in worship. I have stood under the waterfall and thanked God for it, and I hope you get to as well, often. The feeling is a gift, and when it comes you should receive it with both hands. But it is a gift, not a wage. You cannot earn it and you cannot summon it, and the person whose faith depends on getting it every week has built a house on the weather. The mature believer learns to love God on the flat mornings exactly as much as on the flooded ones, and to trust that the singing done in dryness, with no reward but the doing of it, is somehow the truest offering of all, because it is the one that proves the love was never a transaction.

There is an old line about the sacrifice of praise, the praise you bring precisely when it costs you, when there is no rush of good feeling to carry it up on your behalf and you lift it up anyway, by hand, on faith. That is what the fourth pew has been doing for forty years. It is not lesser worship. It is the sacrifice kind. It is the praise that costs.

So if you are dry this morning, if you have been reading this half-convinced that your flat, feelingless faith is a failing thing you ought to fix before you dare sing again, hear the honest word your pastor most wants you to have. Do not wait for the feeling. Go anyway. Sing the same old songs with the same flat heart, and let the very act of showing up be your offering, and trust that it is received with more delight than you could possibly guess. The waterfall may come back and it may not, and either way is fine, because you were never singing to feel something. You were singing to Someone.

And like Anna, you cannot know which ordinary Tuesday will turn out to be the one He walks in on. But I can promise you this: you will only be there to see it if you keep being there. Show up. Show up dry. Show up feeling nothing. It is the most faithful thing a heart can do, and it is closer to the center of worship than any waterfall you ever stood beneath.
