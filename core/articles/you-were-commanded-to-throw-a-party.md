We are very comfortable with the idea that God commands difficult things.

Do not steal. Do not covet. Forgive the one who wronged you. Carry the burden. Love the enemy. These are the commands we expect, the ones that require us to grit our teeth and rise above ourselves. So it comes as a genuine shock to a lot of good, serious believers to discover that tucked into the calendar Yahuah gave His people are commands of an entirely different kind, commands that you keep not by denying yourself, but by *celebrating.* Where the instruction, plainly and repeatedly, is this: you shall rejoice.

Not *you may.* Not *if you feel like it.* You **shall.** Rejoice before Me. It is a joy-mandate, and it changes everything about how you understand what worship is for.

## The moedim: appointments Yahuah put on the calendar

The Scriptures speak of the *moedim*, the appointed times. The word *moed* means an appointed meeting, a set time fixed in advance. These are the great festivals Yahuah wrote into the year: sacred appointments where His people were to stop the ordinary work of survival and come before Him together at a time He Himself had chosen.

Sit with the tenderness of that word. A *moed* is an *appointment*, which means the God of all the earth put standing dates in the calendar to meet with His people. He did not leave the encounter to chance or mood. He circled specific days and said, in effect, *this one is Ours. Clear it. I will be there, and I want you there.* The maker of time built recurring reservations into it so that His family would never go a whole year without a set occasion to be together in His presence.

And when you read what was supposed to happen at these appointments, especially the great harvest festival, the week of shelters and ingathering, the instruction that keeps returning is not solemnity but gladness. Rejoice in your feast. Rejoice before Yahuah your God. The command comes with the poor and the stranger and the servant and the whole household swept into it, so that no one would be left outside the celebration. The Hebrew for this rejoicing, *samach,* is not a faint or dutiful word. It is full-bodied gladness, the joy of a harvest home, the joy of a table too crowded and a heart too full. And it is issued as a command.

> Imagine standing before God on the day of the feast, and the way you obey Him is by being happy. Imagine a disobedience that looks like refusing to celebrate.

## Joy you are required to keep

Here is where it gets glorious, and a little bit challenging, because it turns our instincts inside out.

Most of us treat joy as a *result.* Something that happens to us when circumstances line up, when the news is good, when the body is well, when the accounts balance. Joy, we assume, is the weather; you get what you get. But a commanded joy is a different creature entirely. If Yahuah can *command* rejoicing at an appointed time, then joy cannot merely be a mood that blows in and out. It must be, at least in part, something you can *choose.* Something you show up and *do,* the way you show up and keep any other appointment, whether or not you woke up feeling like it.

That is not God demanding that you fake a feeling. He does not want counterfeit gladness any more than He wants a counterfeit sacrifice. What He is doing is far kinder. He is *making room* for your joy by putting it on the calendar and calling it obedience, so that the person who has spent all year believing that celebration is frivolous, indulgent, unspiritual, or simply not for them, finally has permission from the highest authority in the universe to lay the striving down and be glad. When rejoicing is a commandment, you no longer have to justify your joy. You just have to obey it.

And notice *when* He scheduled these parties. The great festivals cluster around the harvest and the deliverance, around the memory of being rescued and the evidence of being provided for. Yahuah did not command His people to celebrate in a vacuum. He commanded them to celebrate *because of what He had done.* The joy has a reason, and the reason is Him. The feast is not a distraction from remembering God; the feast *is* the remembering. You obey by throwing a party in honor of the One who brought you out and brought you through.

## Come and keep the appointment

So let me say this to the ones who need it most, the serious, the weary, the faithful souls who have somehow come to believe that the truest worship is always the grim and heavy kind, and that gladness is a lesser thing they have not quite earned.

Beloved, you have been reading half the commandments. Yes, He commands you to deny yourself, to carry, to forgive, to endure, and that is holy, and real, and hard. But He *also* commands you to rejoice before Him, and that command is every bit as much His word as the other ones. To skip it, to treat joy as optional decoration on the serious business of faith, is not extra reverence. It is quiet disobedience wearing the costume of piety. Your Father put celebration on the calendar and told you to keep the appointment.

And here is the sweetest part. The whole biblical story is bending toward one enormous final *moed*, a feast at the end of everything, a wedding supper, a gathering so full of gladness that every earlier festival was only a rehearsal for it. Every commanded joy you obey now is a foretaste of that table. Every time you lay down the striving and let yourself be glad in His presence, you are practicing for the celebration that will never be told to end.

So clear the date. Set the table long and crowded and open, the way the command always intended, with the poor and the stranger and the ones who never get invited pulled right into the middle of it. Bring the God who rescued you the one gift He specifically asked for on these days, not a grim endurance, but a full, loud, unembarrassed gladness. You were commanded to throw a party, mi gente. Of all the things the Lord could have ordered, He ordered your joy. Do not leave that commandment unkept.
