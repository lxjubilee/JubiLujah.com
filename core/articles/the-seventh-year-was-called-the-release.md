Imagine being told, by law, to stop earning a living for a year, and to cancel every debt anyone owed you in the same season.

Not because business was bad. Not because you had failed. Precisely when the fields were fertile and the loans were finally due to come back to you, you were commanded to let all of it go. That was the seventh year in Israel, and the name it carried tells you everything. The people called it the **Shmita**, and the word means, at its heart, *release.* A letting-go. A dropping of the thing your hands were gripping. Yahuah built into the very calendar of a working nation a recurring command to open its fists, and He did it not once in a lifetime but every seven years, over and over, so that no generation could ever forget how.

We often flatten the Shmita into a nice idea about rest. But it was far more radical and far more specific than a long weekend for the soil. It was an entire economy, ordered by decree, to let go of the two things human beings clutch hardest, their work and what they are owed, and to find out, in real time, whether the God who commanded it would keep them alive.

## Two things Israel had to drop

The release fell across two things at once, and you cannot understand the seventh year without seeing both.

The first was the **land.** Yahuah told the people that for the whole seventh year they were not to sow their fields or prune their vineyards. Whatever the ground produced on its own, they were not to harvest as owners hoarding a crop. Instead the field was, in effect, thrown open. What grew wild was left for anyone, the servant, the hired worker, the stranger passing through, the poor who had no field of their own, even the wild animals. For one year in seven, the fences of ownership came down and the land fed whoever was hungry. The farmer who spent six years guarding his boundary stone spent the seventh watching strangers eat freely from ground he had always called his.

The second release was the one we forget, and it may be the more astonishing of the two: the release of **debts.** At the end of the seven-year cycle, a creditor was commanded to let go of what his neighbor owed him. The loan he had extended in a hard season, the sum he had every legal and moral right to collect. He was to release it. Cancel it. Let it drop. Yahuah went further still and warned the well-off against a very human temptation: as the seventh year drew near, do not let your heart grow stingy and refuse to lend to a brother in need just because you know the debt is about to be wiped. Give freely, He said, and lend freely, even into the teeth of the release.

> An economy was commanded to keep lending generously to people it knew it would soon be forced to forgive. That is not accounting. That is the heart of Yahuah, written into a nation's ledger.

Think of what that does to a society. It means no family in Israel could be crushed under compounding debt forever. Every seven years the slate was scheduled to clear. The poor man who had fallen behind was never more than a few seasons away from a fresh start built into the law itself, not as charity he had to beg for, but as a release he was *owed* by the rhythm of the calendar.

## The math that never worked, on paper

Now stand in the farmer's sandals and feel the panic, because the Shmita made no earthly sense.

Everything he knew about survival told him that food comes from planting and eating comes from working. A field left unsown is a field that yields nothing, and a family that does not plant is a family that does not eat next winter. And now Yahuah was telling him to leave the ground alone for a full year *and* to forgive the debts that might have carried him through. On paper, the seventh year was a recipe for starvation. The numbers simply did not close.

Yahuah knew they would ask the obvious, terrified question, *what will we eat in the seventh year if we do not plant?*, and He answered it before they asked. He promised that the sixth year would carry a blessing so heavy that the harvest would stretch to feed them through the seventh year and on into the eighth, until a new crop could come in. In other words, He did not remove the risk. He did not make the math close on its own. He told them to obey into what looked like a cliff, and He promised to be the ground beneath the fall.

That is the beating center of the Shmita. It was a national, recurring, mandatory lesson in trust, an entire economy taught, by doing, that its true provision never came from its own frantic grip in the first place. The One who feeds the wild things that grow in an unplowed field, the One who commands a triple harvest into a single year, was the source all along. The work and the loans were only ever instruments. He was the supply.

## What it costs to keep a closed fist

I want to say something tender here, because I think the seventh year exposes a wound most of us carry and have stopped naming.

We do not trust that we will be caught. We grip our work as if our whole worth were measured in what we produce, and we grip what we are owed, money, yes, but also apologies, credit, recognition, the debts of hurt that people rack up against us, as if letting any of it go would leave us with nothing. We have never learned the muscle of release, because we have never quite believed the promise that stands behind it. And a clenched fist, held long enough, cannot receive anything either. The hand that will not open to let go is the same hand that cannot open to be filled.

Yahuah gave Israel a rhythm precisely because release is a muscle, and muscles are trained by repetition. Every seven years He pried the national fist open again, *let the field go, let the debt go, and watch Me keep you*, until a people who came out of slavery gripping everything might slowly become a people who could hold their whole lives with open hands.

## The Release with a name

And all of it was leaning toward a Person.

When Yeshua came, He announced that the poor would hear good news and the debtors would be released, and the debt He came to drop was the one no seventh year could reach. Beneath the loans of silver was a deeper account, the one every one of us has run up against heaven and could never repay in a hundred lifetimes of good harvests. Yeshua did not renegotiate that debt. He did not extend its terms or reduce its balance. He released it. He let it go, absorbed it into Himself, and tore up the record entirely. He taught His people to pray *forgive us our debts,* using the very language of the seventh year, and He made the release the ground of how we live: freely you received, so freely give.

So here is what I want to leave open in your hands, the way Yahuah left the fields open.

You are allowed to let go. The work you are gripping as if it were your salvation, set it down for a season and see if the God who commands harvests will keep you. The debt you have been holding against someone, the one you have every right to collect. You are permitted, even invited, to release it, because you have been released of infinitely more. The seventh year was never a loss. It was a nation learning, one open hand at a time, that it was held by Someone who never once let it fall.

Open your hands. The Release has a name now, and He has already opened His.
