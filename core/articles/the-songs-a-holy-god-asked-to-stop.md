He was not a professional holy man. Amos kept sheep in Tekoa and tended a grove of sycamore figs, and he says so plainly, almost defensively, as if to head off the accusation that he was a religious insider working an angle. He was a working man Yahuah took by the collar and sent north, into a kingdom that was fat and comfortable and sure that God was pleased with it.

And He was not sending Amos to shut down their worship for being sparse. This was the problem. The worship in Israel was thriving. The feasts were full. The assemblies were crowded. The offerings came in generous piles. The music, especially the music, was flowing, harps and voices and skilled players filling the air with sound. By every metric a worship team measures, revival was on.

Into that thriving scene Yahuah sent a shepherd to say the last thing anyone expected. I hate your feasts. I take no delight in your solemn assemblies. Take away from Me the noise of your songs. I will not listen to the melody of your harps.

The noise of your songs. He called their worship music noise. Not because it was out of tune. Not because it was too loud or too soft or in the wrong style. He called it noise because of what was happening in the streets outside the sanctuary door.

## Excellent worship over a rotten foundation

You have to hold the two pictures side by side to feel the weight of it. Inside: harps, feasts, the fragrance of sacrifice, hands lifted, faces sincere. Outside: the poor being trampled, the needy sold for the price of a pair of sandals, honest people crushed at the city gate where justice was supposed to live, the powerful growing sleek on what they took from the weak.

And the same people were doing both. They would grind a poor man into the dust on a Tuesday and lift their hands in a beautiful song on the Sabbath, and feel, this is the terrifying part, genuinely worshipful while they did it. The music was working on them. It was giving them the warm interior sensation of being right with God, and it was completely disconnected from how they treated the human beings God had made.

That is what turned the melody into noise in the ears of Yahuah. Not the notes. The gap. He will not be sung into ignoring what our hands are doing when the song ends. He is holy, which means among other things that He cannot be flattered. You cannot buy His approval with production value. You cannot cover an unjust life with an excellent chorus and call the transaction worship.

I want to say this carefully, because it is meant to search me before it searches anyone. It is possible to have a moving, skillful, emotionally powerful worship life and have it be, to the God it claims to honor, a noise He is asking us to turn down. The sincerity of the feeling proves nothing. Amos's audience felt it deeply. That was rather the point.

## What He asked for instead

Now hear what Yahuah asked for in place of the songs He was rejecting, because it is one of the great sentences in all of prophecy. Let justice roll down like waters, He said, and righteousness like an ever-flowing stream.

Notice what He did not say. He did not say, sing less. He did not say, tear down the instruments and abandon the assemblies forever. He reached for the image of a river. He wanted the worship and the life to become one moving body of water, the song and the justice flowing together, fed from the same source, impossible to separate. The tragedy was not that they sang. The tragedy was that they had dammed the song off from the street, made a still, private pond of their worship, and let it grow stagnant while the fields of the poor stayed dry.

A river that only fills the sanctuary is not what He is after. He wants the same current that lifts your hands on the day of worship to reach the man at the gate, the widow with no advocate, the worker you could underpay and get away with it. When those are one river, the harps are welcome again. When they are two separate things, the harps are noise, however skillfully they are played.

## The prophetic song still confronts

This is the edge of the prophetic gift that we would rather file down. We love the prophetic when it comforts. We are less fond of it when it walks into a thriving worship service and asks whether the God being worshipped recognizes Himself in the lives of the people singing. But that confrontation is not the enemy of worship. It is the friend of true worship. Amos was not trying to end Israel's songs. He was trying to make them real, to reconnect the melody to the mercy so that Yahuah could bear to listen again.

And here is where the hope lives, because there is deep hope in this. The God who asked for the songs to stop did not want silence. He wanted integrity. He was not done with music; He was done with the divorce. Every worshipper Amos rattled had a way back, and it did not run through better musicianship. It ran through the streets. Let justice flow, and the song is restored. Care for the crushed, and the harp is heard again. The road back to acceptable worship went straight through the lives of the people they had been stepping over on the way to the assembly.

That road is still open. If you have ever feared that your worship might be noise, that there is a gap between the height of your singing and the ordinariness of your justice, that fear is not condemnation. It is the same shepherd's voice, and it is a mercy, because it means the river can still be made one. Take the current that lifts you on Sunday and let it reach a Monday somebody. Let the song and the life run in the same channel. Do that, and the God who once said take away the noise of your songs will lean in to listen, because at last the melody and the man will be telling Him the same true thing.
