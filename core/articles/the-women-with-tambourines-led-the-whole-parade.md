Most of us worship standing still. Feet planted, facing one direction, waiting for it to be over so we can sit back down.

So it is worth noticing that when the old songbook wants to show you worship at its most glorious, it does not show you a room full of people standing in rows. It shows you a *parade.* A procession. A whole crowd of God's people moving, going somewhere together, in step, in motion, with musicians in the mix and a beat driving the feet forward. And right in the thick of it, the psalm points its finger at a specific group and makes sure you see them: the young women in the middle, striking hand-drums, leading the celebration in.

## A procession, not a standstill

There is a psalm that pauses to describe a solemn, joyful entry into the sanctuary of God, and it reads almost like stage directions for a festival. The singers go in front. The players of instruments come behind. And in the middle of the whole moving column, the psalm places young women playing the *tof*, the frame drum, what our Bibles usually render as the timbrel or the tambourine.

Feel the shape of that scene. This is not an audience. This is a *procession*, a body of worshipers walking forward together toward the presence of Yahuah, and the walking is not incidental to the worship. The walking *is* the worship. They are not waiting in place for something to happen to them; they are going, as one, into the house of God, and they are making a joyful racket the whole way in.

I love that the psalm bothers to arrange them. Singers ahead. Players behind. Drummers in the heart of it. This is choreography. This is a people who understood that worship has a direction and a movement, that praise is something you do with your whole body walking, not just your mouth sitting. The barrio has never forgotten this. When we celebrate, we do not stay in the building. We take it into the street. We *process.* And it turns out we did not invent that either. We inherited it from a songbook that put a parade at the center of the sanctuary.

## The little drum in the celebrant's hands

Now look closely at the instrument, because the psalm goes out of its way to name it.

The *tof* was a small frame drum, a hoop of wood with a skin stretched across it, light enough to hold in one hand and strike with the other while you walked, or danced, or spun. It was not a temple fixture bolted to a platform. It was a *portable* instrument, made to travel, made for procession and celebration and the open air. You could carry your worship in your hand and beat it as you moved. That is the whole genius of the thing. The tof was built for a people who refused to hold still.

And the psalm places it, pointedly, in the hands of the young women in the middle of the parade. Throughout the Scriptures, when there is a great deliverance to celebrate, when the joy is too big to keep seated, it is very often the women who come out first with the tof in their hands and the celebration on their feet, and everyone else falls in behind them. The timbrel in a woman's hand, leading a crowd forward in gladness, is one of the oldest images of triumph the Bible knows.

> The drum did not follow the parade. The drum *led* it. The beat in the celebrant's hand is what tells the feet of a whole people which way joy is going.

Think about what that means. The instrument setting the pace of the entire procession into the house of God is not the grand and immovable one. It is the small, glad, hand-held one, carried by ordinary celebrants in the middle of the crowd. Worship is not being conducted from a distant throne. It is being *led from the inside of the party,* by the people striking little drums with all the joy in them, and the beat they keep is the beat the whole assembly marches to.

## Get in the parade

So here is what I want to press into your hands like a drum of your own.

Worship was never meant to be a spectator sport you attend and then leave unchanged, feet never moving, still facing the same direction you came in. It was designed as a procession, a people going somewhere together, in glad motion, carrying the celebration in their own hands and beating it out as they go. If your whole experience of praise is standing still and waiting for it to end, I am not scolding you, I am *inviting* you: there is a parade, and you are meant to be in it, not watching it pass.

And you do not need to be at the front to belong. The psalm shows a body of worshipers with singers ahead and players behind and drummers in the middle, and every single one of them is essential to the shape of the thing. Some of us are called to carry the tune out front. Some of us fall in at the back and simply keep walking. And some are handed the little drum and asked to set the pace for everyone around them, and if that is you, do not shrink from it. The joy you keep in your hands might be the beat that tells the tired ones which way to march.

There is a deep tenderness in this that I do not want you to miss. The God of the whole universe did not design a worship that only the composed and the seated could join. He designed a moving, drumming, dancing procession, wide enough for the young and the old, the graceful and the clumsy, the loud and the shy, all falling into step behind the small glad drums, all going the same direction, all headed into His presence together. Nobody gets left standing on the curb in this story. The whole street is meant to be walking.

So take the drum, mi gente. Or take a hand, or take a step, or take up the song, whatever the parade puts in front of you. Fall in. Keep moving. Beat the joy out loud with your whole body as you go, because the procession is already underway, the young women with the tambourines are already leading it in, and the house of God at the end of the road has room enough for the entire celebration to come dancing through the doors.
