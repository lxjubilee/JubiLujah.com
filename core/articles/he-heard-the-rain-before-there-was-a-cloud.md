There is a scene tucked just after one of the most famous days in the whole Bible, and almost nobody lingers on it, because it comes right after fire fell from heaven and it is hard to compete with fire. But I want to linger on it with you, because the quiet minutes after the fire have taught me more about faith than the fire ever did.

The land has been in drought for three and a half years. No rain, no dew, the ground cracked, the brooks gone to dust, a whole nation slowly starving under a hard bright sky. And on Mount Carmel, Yahuah has just answered the prophet Elijah with fire in front of everyone, settling the question of who is God. It is a mountaintop day in every sense. But the drought is not over. The fire proved who reigns; it did not put water in the wells. The people still need rain.

And Elijah turns to the king and says the strangest thing. He tells Ahab to go up, eat and drink, *because I can hear the sound of a heavy rain coming.*

There is not a cloud in the sky. There has not been a cloud worth the name in three and a half years. The horizon is the same merciless blue it has been all along. And the prophet says he can *hear* it. Not see it. Hear it. Somewhere below the register of the visible, in a place that has nothing to do with what the eye can confirm, Elijah has caught the sound of an ocean of water that is not there yet.

## Faith with its face in the dirt

Here is what he does next, and this is the part I have built a lot of my hope on.

He does not stand there triumphant, arms wide, declaring the rain over a dry sky and waiting to be proven right. He climbs to the top of Carmel, bends all the way down to the ground, and puts his face between his knees. The man who just called down fire folds himself into the dirt like a woman in labor and prays. The one who heard the rain does not act like he has already got it. He gets low. He travails. He asks.

And then he sends his servant to go look toward the sea. The boy goes, scans the whole flat horizon, comes back: nothing. And Elijah, still folded on the ground, says the word I want to give you tonight. *Go again.*

Seven times. The servant walks out to the edge, searches the empty sky, walks back with nothing, seven times over. And I want you to feel how that would wear on you, because I have lived inside those seven trips. You heard the sound. You are sure you heard it. You have committed yourself out loud. You told the king it was coming, and now you are on your face in the dust and the report keeps coming back *nothing, still nothing, the sky is exactly as blue as it was before.* Six times the evidence says you were wrong to hope. Six times the horizon calls you a fool. And the prophet does not get up, and does not take it back, and does not stop sending. Go again.

## A cloud the size of a hand

The seventh time, the servant comes back and says there is a little cloud rising out of the sea, small as a man's hand.

That is all. Not a storm front, not a black sky, not thunder, one small smudge of cloud the size of a fist held up at arm's length, the kind of thing you would never notice if you had not been told to look for it. And Elijah does not wait for it to grow. He knows. He sends word to the king: *harness the chariot and get down the mountain before the rain stops you.* And before long the sky goes black with clouds and wind, and a great rain comes, and a man who had been folded in the dust runs ahead of the king's chariot in the downpour with the hand of Yahuah on him.

I love that God did not answer with the whole sky at once. He answered with a hand-sized cloud. He gave Elijah just enough to run on, the smallest possible confirmation, a thing you had to be looking for to even see, and Elijah treated that fist of vapor like the flood it was about to become. He did not despise how small it was. He recognized the down payment on the roar he had already heard.

## For the ones on the seventh trip

Let me tell you why this is a musician's story, and why I carry it.

Long before a song is finished, before there is a single line anyone can sing, you can *hear* it. It arrives as a sound with no shape yet, a rain you catch below the register of the visible, and you commit yourself to it out loud with nothing on the page to show for it. And then comes the labor of it, the getting low, the sending, the going again, and trip after trip the report comes back *nothing, still nothing, this is not becoming anything.* That is the actual work of faith: to keep sending, to keep looking, to stay folded in the dust believing the sound you heard was real when the sky refuses to confirm it. Prophets do not just announce the rain. They travail for it. They hear it first, and then they pray it down, and then they wait through six empty trips for one hand-sized cloud.

So here is my word to whoever has been sent to look at the horizon one more time.

You may have heard the rain. Somewhere in a place deeper than sight, over a marriage or a child or a calling or a country, you caught the sound of an ocean that is not visible yet, and you said so, and now you are on trip number four, or five, or six, and every report comes back dry. Do not get up. Do not take it back. The blue sky is not the verdict; the sound you heard is the verdict. Send again. And when the answer finally comes, do not be surprised that it is small, a cloud the size of a hand, a thing so easy to miss you would have overlooked it if you had not been looking. That fist of cloud is not the disappointment. It is the down payment on the downpour. Harness the chariot. The One who lets you hear the rain before He shows you the cloud is not toying with you. He is teaching you to run in faith before the first drop lands, and the rain is already on its way up out of the sea.
