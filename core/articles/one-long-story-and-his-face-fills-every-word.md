For a long time I read the Scriptures the way you walk through a souk of separate stalls, a book of law here, a book of poetry there, a shelf of prophets down the lane, and the story of Yasu' waiting at the far end. Sixty-six stalls, each with its own keeper. Then one day the whole market rearranged itself in front of me, and I saw that it had never been separate stalls at all. It was one long road, and one face was waiting at every turn of it. This album is that discovery set to the oud.

*Every Page Sings Your Name* is my attempt to walk the entire library out loud, from the first page to the last, and show what I finally saw, that the Scriptures are not a collection of religious documents that happen to mention a Messiah at the end. They are one story, and **Yeshua** is the meaning the whole thing was reaching for. Remove Him and it does not merely lose a chapter. It loses its plot.

## The promise older than the prophets

The record opens in the Garden, on the oldest promise in the Book. "Seed of the Woman" goes back to the moment right after the fall, when the Lord speaks to the serpent and says the seed of the woman will crush his head, though his heel will be bruised (Genesis 3:15). The old teachers called that verse the first gospel, the whole rescue announced in a single sentence before a single prophet was born. The opening verse of my song says it plainly: *the Seed of the woman who would crush the snake, every page of Scripture was written for Your sake.*

From there the album refuses to treat the early Scriptures as a warm-up act. Adam is called a shadow of the One to come, and Yasu' the Last Adam, where the first man fell, the true Man stood and rose (that is the language of 1 Corinthians 15:45). Abraham's promised seed turns out, the apostle Paul insists, to be singular, and the singular is Christ (Galatians 3:16). I did not invent these connections in a studio. They are the reading the earliest believers were already doing.

> From the Garden to Calvary, one long story, and Your face fills every word.

## The shadows that were always Him

The middle of the album is my favorite country to travel, because it is full of the images my people already love, the desert, the lamb, the lifted standard, and each one turns out to have been a portrait all along.

"The Bronze Serpent" takes the strangest scene in the wilderness, where a snake of bronze was lifted on a pole and the dying who looked at it lived (Numbers 21), and Yasu' Himself said that just as Moses lifted up the serpent, so must the Son of Man be lifted up, that whoever looks to Him might live (John 3:14). "Passover Lamb" walks straight into the night of Exodus, the blood on the doorframe, the firstborn spared, because the apostle says outright that Christ, our Passover lamb, has been sacrificed (1 Corinthians 5:7). "Suffering Servant" sits down in Isaiah 53 and does not flinch, the man despised and acquainted with grief, wounded for our transgressions.

These are not clever overlays I pressed onto the text. They are the shadows the text was already casting, waiting for the light that would explain their shape. That is why the music modulates from a minor maqam up into major right at the heart of the record, the shadow is real, and then the sun clears it.

## The road where the reading caught fire

Everything on this album bends toward one afternoon on a dusty road, and the song at the album's peak is "Emmaus Road."

Two travelers are walking away from Jerusalem, crushed, sure the story is over, and a stranger falls into step with them. And beginning with Moses and all the Prophets, He interprets to them in all the Scriptures the things concerning Himself (Luke 24:27). They do not recognize Him until He breaks the bread, and then they say the thing this whole record is named for: *did not our hearts burn within us while He opened the Scriptures to us?* (Luke 24:32).

That is what I want this album to do. Not to win an argument about the Bible, but to reproduce that burning. To let a listener feel the sensation those two men felt, the slow, astonished realization that the book they thought they knew had been about a Person the entire time, and that the Person is now walking beside them. The single "Every Page Sings Your Name" is the whole record in a chorus: *from Genesis to Malachi, the Law and the Prophets proclaim, Jesus, You are the Word made flesh, every page sings Your Name.*

## Where the whole library was heading

The album ends where the Gospel of John begins, with "The Word Made Flesh", in the beginning was the Word, and the Word was with God, and the Word was God, and the Word became flesh and dwelt among us (John 1:1, 14). All the scrolls, all the shadows, all the promises older than the prophets, gather into a face you could look at, a voice you could hear, a Man who pitched His tent in our streets.

I made this for two kinds of hearts. One is the believer who has read the Scriptures dutifully for years and found them a set of disconnected rooms, the law heavy, the prophets strange, the poetry beautiful but far away. I want you to see the corridor that runs through every room, and Whose it is. The other is the seeker from my own part of the world who has heard the Scriptures spoken of with respect but always as someone else's book, a foreign anthology with no thread running through. There is a thread. His name is **Yeshua**, and He is not a foreign figure inserted at the end. He is the reason the first page was written.

Open the Book anywhere. Read slowly. Watch for the face that keeps appearing in the margins of every story, in the lamb and the ladder and the lifted pole and the suffering servant. It is one long story from the Garden to the empty tomb, and His face fills every word. When you finally see it, the reading stops being a duty and becomes what it was always meant to be, a fire in the chest, and a Name on the lips.
