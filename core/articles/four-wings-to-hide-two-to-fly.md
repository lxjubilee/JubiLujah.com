The prophet does something in his vision of the throne that I have come to treasure: he counts the wings. He does not have to. He could simply tell us there were glorious creatures crying holy over the throne and leave the anatomy alone. Instead he stops and gives us the arithmetic, and the arithmetic is the message. Each of these beings had six wings. With two they covered the face. With two they covered the feet. And with the last two, he says almost as an afterthought, they flew.

Do the sum. Four wings of six are given to *hiding*. Only two are spared for *moving*. The creatures who live closest to the throne, nearer than any of us have ever been, near enough to see and be seen, spend twice as much of themselves veiling as they spend flying. Their default posture is not display. It is cover.

And who are these creatures? The word is **seraph**, and it means *burning one*. From a root that has to do with fire, with burning, with being aflame. These are not shy, retiring beings by nature. They are the incandescent ones, the ones who are themselves *on fire*. If any created thing had earned the right to stare openly into the glory, it would be the burning ones, fire meeting fire, like drawing near to like. And they will not do it. Even the flame veils itself before the Source of flame.

## Reverence in the Middle of the Fire

We tend to assume reverence is what you feel at a *distance*, the caution of the outsider, the trembling of the one who has not yet come near. Get close enough, we imagine, get intimate enough, and the veils come off; familiarity replaces fear. The throne room says the opposite. The nearer you get, the *more* covered you become. The beings with the best view are the ones most committed to not presuming upon it.

This is the thing our generation most needs to hear, and it is why I tremble to write it gently rather than sternly. We have rightly recovered the nearness of God. We know we may come boldly; the blood of Yeshua tore the veil and we are welcomed in as sons, not slaves. I would never hand that back. But somewhere along the way we let nearness and reverence become enemies, as if awe were a thing you outgrow once you get comfortable. The seraphim are the most comfortable beings in the universe with the presence of Yahuah. They have never known anywhere else, and they are the most covered. Their reverence did not shrink with proximity. It grew.

> The closer they burned, the more of themselves they veiled. Nearness did not cure the awe. Nearness was made of it.

## Why Cover the Face

Consider first the face. To cover the face before Yahuah is to confess that there is a glory you were not made to look at directly and survive, not because He is cruel, but because you are small, and the truest thing your smallness can do in that presence is decline to stare. The burning ones are not being scolded into modesty. They have simply *seen*, and having seen, they know that the appropriate response to that much glory is not to gawk but to shield.

There is a wisdom in the veiled face that our unveiled culture has lost. We want to see everything, capture everything, keep our eyes open through every holy moment so we do not miss a thing. The seraphim would teach us that some of the deepest worship happens with the eyes *covered*, that there is a knowing which comes only to those humble enough not to look, a nearness reserved for those who will drop their gaze in the very presence they most long to behold.

## Why Cover the Feet

And then the feet. This is the tender one. The feet are the lowest part, the part that touches the ground, the ordinary and unglorious part of any creature. To cover the feet before the throne is to acknowledge that even the humblest, most common thing about you is exposed in that light, and to veil it not in shame but in a kind of holy modesty, an unwillingness to bring even your most ordinary parts casually before that glory.

I find enormous comfort in this. The seraphim are not covering their feet because they have something to hide from a God who sees everything anyway. They are covering them because *reverence extends all the way down*, because in that presence even the mundane deserves honoring, even the lowly is treated as though it stands on holy ground. If the burning ones veil their feet, then there is no part of you too small to bring reverently before Him. He is worthy of your face and your feet alike.

## The Two Wings That Are Left

Only after all that veiling does the prophet mention flying. Two wings. It is the smallest allocation, and I do not think that is an accident. In the economy of the throne, *movement is not the main thing*. Doing is not the main thing. Even for the most active servants in the universe, more strength goes to covering than to going. Their first vocation is not activity. It is awe.

We have that backwards. We measure our worship and our service by motion, by how much we do, how far we go, how much ground we cover. And the burning ones, who could out-fly and out-labor us a thousand to one, keep two-thirds of their wings folded over themselves in reverence and reserve only a third for the going. What they teach is not that doing is worthless. They *do* fly, they *do* carry the coal, they *do* serve. It is that the doing flows out of the covering, and never the reverse. Worship is the four wings. Service is the two. Get the ratio wrong and you become a great deal of motion with no fire underneath it.

## Learn the Arithmetic

So here is what I want you to carry out of the throne room. You are invited nearer to Yahuah than the seraphim ever expected a human being to come, near enough to call Him Father, near enough to be filled with His own Ruach, near enough that no veil stands between you and the Holy of Holies anymore. Come, then. Come boldly. Come near.

But learn the arithmetic on your way in. Let more of yourself go to reverence than to display. Let there be moments when your eyes are covered, not because you are afraid to see but because you have seen enough to know what awe is for. Let even your lowliest parts be brought before Him honored rather than flung down carelessly. And keep the flying, the doing, the serving, the going, in its proper proportion, downstream of the fire, never in place of it.

The burning ones are still there, still covered, still crying *holy* across the ages, still trembling and still glad. They are not afraid of the fire; they are made of it. And in the very middle of it, they veil themselves, not to hide from His nearness, but because that is what nearness, at its truest, has always looked like. Four wings to hide, two to fly. Come near, beloved, and learn to burn like that.
