There is a service happening this Sunday that no one will ever hear about.

Nineteen people, maybe twenty if the weather holds. A room that takes a while to warm up. A guitar that goes flat in the second verse and stays there, and a man who plays it anyway because he is the only one who can, and he learned four chords at fifty because the church needed someone to. No stage lights, because there is no stage. No cameras, because who would film it, and for whom. They will sing three or four songs the way they have sung them for years, some voices sure and some wandering, and then they will go home to their ordinary week, and nothing about the morning will be captured, counted, clipped, or shared.

By every measure our age knows how to take, that service did not happen. If a thing is not seen, not filmed, not metricked, it might as well not exist. That is the quiet gospel of the feed, and it has crept into the church so thoroughly that even faithful pastors have started to feel that the worship which cannot be broadcast is somehow a lesser grade of worship, a practice round, a holdover, a thing we do until we can do it better and bigger and where it might finally be seen.

I want to say something I have staked my whole ministry on. That cold room of nineteen is not the practice version of worship. It may well be the truest one in the county.

The prophet Zechariah spoke to a people who were sick with smallness. They had come back from exile to rebuild the temple, and when the foundation was finally laid, it was so modest, so far from the glory of the one their grandfathers remembered, that some of them wept openly at how little it was. And into that discouragement the word of the Lord came, and it asked a question I think our whole spectacle-drunk generation needs to hear: who has despised the day of small things? Not who has endured it. Who has despised it, because the God who spoke that question does not despise it at all. He was, at that very moment, delighting in a foundation the people themselves were ashamed of. The eye of Heaven and the eye of the crowd were looking at the same small thing and seeing two completely different sizes.

This is the great reversal that runs straight through the Scriptures, and we keep forgetting it because our whole world is built to teach us the opposite. God is forever caught being most attentive to exactly what the world has agreed not to look at. A shepherd boy the prophet nearly overlooked, the one his own father did not bother to call in from the field. A widow dropping two of the smallest coins into the treasury while the wealthy make their impressive gifts, and Yeshua, watching, says she has put in more than all of them, because they gave from their abundance and she gave everything she had. Heaven's accounting and the crowd's accounting are not two versions of one ledger. They are two different worlds. The widow trended nowhere. She is still being talked about two thousand years later, in a book that outlived every empire that ever ignored her.

So let me say it about your small church, your thin gathering, your handful of the faithful in a room that will never be filmed. The reason no one is looking is not the reason you fear it is. It is not that there is nothing there. It is that the thing which is there does not photograph. Presence never has. Faithfulness never has. The particular weight of nineteen people who have buried each other's parents and prayed each other's children through the hard years and shown up again this Sunday in the cold. There is no lens that catches that. It is invisible to a camera for the same reason love is: it is real in a dimension the camera cannot reach.

And I have come to believe something stronger than that, and I will say it even though it cannot be proven, because I have felt it in too many small rooms to keep it to myself. I think Heaven leans closest to exactly the worship no one is watching. I think the very absence of the crowd is part of what makes it precious, because there is no one left to perform for. When nineteen people sing to a flat guitar with no camera in the room, there is only one possible audience for the song. It cannot be for the algorithm. It cannot be for the reputation of the band. It cannot be a performance, because performance requires spectators, and there are none but God. It can only be worship. The smallness is not the flaw in it. The smallness is what purifies it.

The megachurch down the highway is not the enemy in this. I mean that. God is worshiped there too, and I will not stand in a small room and sneer at a large one; that is just envy wearing the robe of humility. But I will say that bigness carries a temptation smallness is mercifully spared. When the lights are good and the room is full and the whole thing looks like the videos, it becomes very hard to know whether you are worshiping God or enjoying the feeling of being part of something impressive. The nineteen in the cold room have no such confusion available to them. There is nothing impressive to enjoy. There is only God, and each other, and the flat guitar, and the choice to sing anyway. That is not a lesser worship. That is worship with all the flattering mirrors taken down.

So to the ones who lead the gatherings the world will never notice, the ones tempted this week to grieve the empty seats and measure your Sunday against a screen, hear the old question turned toward you as a comfort and not a rebuke. Who has despised the day of small things? Not the One you are singing to. He laid a foundation once that made grown men weep for how little it was, and He was rejoicing over it the entire time they wept. He is not waiting for your church to get big enough to be worth His attention. He is already leaning in. He always was closest to the rooms the cameras skip.

Sing your three flat songs this Sunday. Do not apologize for the size of the room. There is no such thing as a small congregation in the only accounting that finally holds, and the worship that will never trend anywhere on earth is being received, this very morning, in the one place where it will never, ever be forgotten.
