There is a particular sadness that only songwriters know, and I want to describe it to you, because I think you have felt its cousin.

It is the half-written song. Not the one you finished. Not the one you never started. The one that sits open on the piano with a great verse and a chorus that will not come, a beginning full of promise and then a cliff, a place where the music simply stops mid-thought. You walk past it for days. Every time you see it, it accuses you a little. There it is, that good beginning, going nowhere. And a quiet voice suggests the obvious: maybe it was never going to be anything. Maybe you should throw it out and stop pretending.

I have a stack of those. And I have learned, over years, that the stack is really two stacks wearing the same clothes. There are songs I truly abandoned, walked away from, gave up on, decided were not worth finishing. And there are songs I only stepped away from, set down not in defeat but because they were not ready, because I was not ready, because the ending had not arrived and forcing it would have ruined it. From the outside, on the piano, the two look identical. Both are unfinished. Both are silent. But one is a corpse and the other is a person asleep, and everything depends on which you are.

I think a great many people walk around convinced they are the first kind, an abandoned draft, a song God started and thought better of, when the truth is they are the second. Unfinished. But not abandoned. Not for one moment.

## Unfinished is not the same as failed

Somewhere along the way we absorbed the idea that a good life is a finished one, resolved, arrived, all its questions answered, its loose threads tied. And so the unfinished places in us feel like evidence against us. The relationship still broken. The habit still winning. The dream still out of reach. The version of yourself you meant to become by now, still unbuilt. You look at the open page of your life, all that promise and then the cliff, and you hear the same accusing voice I hear over the piano: maybe this was never going to be anything.

But listen to what the accusation actually assumes. It assumes the Composer has left the room. It assumes the song is being judged in its current state, as a finished object, and found wanting. And that is precisely the lie.

The Scriptures make a promise I hold onto on my worst days. Paul writes to a young church that the One who began a good work in them will carry it on and complete it, will keep at it, faithfully, all the way to the end. Read the shape of that sentence carefully. It is addressed to people mid-song. People with the verse written and the chorus missing. It does not say you are finished. It says you are being finished, that the gap between the promising beginning and the completed whole is not a verdict against you but a description of where you are in a process that has an Author who does not walk away from His drafts.

He is not standing over the piano deciding whether to keep you. He is sitting down at it. Still writing.

## The bridge is always the ugliest part on the page

I have written enough songs to tell you a secret about how they look while they are being made: the unfinished middle is almost always the ugliest part on the page. Crossed-out lines. Notes that do not resolve. A section that seems to have lost the thread entirely and wandered somewhere dark and strange. If you found that page and did not know it was a work in progress, you might conclude the writer had failed.

But that dark, wandering, unresolved middle is not the failure of the song. In the finest songs it is the most important part, the place the music leaves the safety of what it already knows and goes out into tension so that the last chorus, when it finally lands, can mean something it could not have meant before. The struggle in the middle is not a sign the song is broken. It is the reason the ending will be worth hearing.

I do not say this to make light of the hard middle you are living in. I say it because I have watched, over and over, the exact passage that felt like proof the song was ruined turn out to be the passage that made the ending land. The Composer works in tension. He is not afraid of the unresolved measure. He is, in fact, doing something there that could not be done in the easy, resolved, finished-looking parts, deepening the thing so that its resolution, when it comes, will carry a weight it could never have carried if He had let you skip straight to the last chorus.

## He does not lose the thread

Here is the difference between the songs I abandoned and the songs I only stepped away from, and it comes down to one thing: whether the writer forgets.

The songs I truly lost are the ones I forgot, set down and never thought of again, the thread of what I was reaching for gone from my mind entirely. But the songs I only stepped away from stayed with me. I would be walking, months later, and the missing chorus would surface, because some part of me had never stopped holding the shape of what that song was trying to become. I did not abandon it. I was carrying it the whole time it lay silent on the piano.

Now hear the thing I most want you to hear. God does not forget the thread of you. The Scriptures say He knit you together, that He knew you before you were born, that the days of your life were written before one of them came to be. Whatever He began in you, He is still holding the shape of what it is meant to become, not vaguely, not distantly, but the way a writer carries an unfinished song in the back of his mind through every ordinary hour, waiting for the measure that will complete it. You are not a page He walked past and forgot. You are a work He is still composing, and the reason the ending has not come is not that He lost interest. It is that He is not done.

So when you look at the open, unresolved page of your own life and the accusing voice tells you it was never going to be anything, I want you to answer it with the truth about who is holding the pen.

You are not a discarded draft.

You are an unfinished one, which means the best of you is not behind you in the crossed-out lines. It is ahead of you, in the chorus that has not landed yet, being written by a hand that has never once abandoned a song it began.
