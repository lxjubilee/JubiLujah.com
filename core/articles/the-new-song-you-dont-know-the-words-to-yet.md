A song gets stuck partway.

You know it for a few bars, enough to hum in a stairwell, or under your breath in a checkout line, and then the melody reaches for a note that isn't there, and your voice trails off, and you couldn't quite say why you were singing it in the first place.

Most of us file that away as a small failure of memory. Turn it over for a moment, though. What if it isn't a thing you half-forgot, but a thing that isn't finished being written?

There is an old instruction buried in the songbook at the center of the Scriptures, and it keeps repeating, which is usually the first clue that something matters. Not "sing the old songs well." Not "guard the anthem your people have always known." Something stranger: sing a new song. A new one. Again. As though the singing were never meant to arrive at a final version you could frame and hang on the wall.

That command has always been a little scandalous. Every culture keeps its music the way it keeps its grandparents, the lullaby in the exact words your mother used, the melody that means wedding, the one that means grief, the call that floats over a city at first light. To be told a new song is coming can sound, at first, like a threat to the old one.

It isn't. And the reason why is the thing worth staying for.

Follow that "new song" through the pages and you notice what it never does. It never announces a new tribe, a new tongue, or a replacement for anyone's homeland melody. It announces a homecoming. It keeps turning up on the far side of exile, after the long years away, after the road home was presumed closed for good, sung by people who find the gate standing open anyway. The new song is what comes out of you when you did not think you would make it back.

And in the last pages of those same Scriptures there is a scene that has kept singers awake for a very long time. A song is being sung around the throne of Elohim, the Maker of everyone, of every people without exception, and the text says something almost impossible to shake off: no one could learn that song except the ones it had been sung over. It was not a piece you could study from the outside and perform. You learned it by being brought home. You learned the words by becoming one of the words.

Which, at last, explains the tune stuck in the stairwell.

You don't know the words to the new song yet, not because your memory failed you, and not because it belongs to some other people and was never meant for you. You don't know the words because it isn't finished being sung. And one of the voices it is still waiting on is yours.

That is a strange sentence, so let me lay it down more plainly. The reason so many of us, across so many cultures, half-remember a melody of belonging we can never quite complete, the reason the most moving music on earth so often aches instead of settling, is that we were built for a homecoming we have not fully had. The ache is not a defect in the song. It is a tuning note. It is the true pitch your whole life has been quietly trying to find.

Every people already carries a fragment of it. Listen honestly to the music of the world, the call that rises over a city at dawn, the drum that gathers a village, the hymn a grandmother hums with her eyes closed, the lament that keeps saying a name no one else remembers, and underneath all of it you hear the same reach. A reach toward a home just past the last note of the melody. Nobody sat the entire human race down and taught it to long for the same thing. We simply do. That is not a coincidence to be explained away. It is a rumor of where we came from, and a rehearsal for where we are going.

The new song does not erase any of those fragments. It gathers them. When the Scriptures picture the end of the whole story, it is never one nation singing over the silence of the others. It is every language in the room at once, and not one of them flattened into sounding like its neighbor. The particular homesickness in your music turns out to have been homesickness for a table where your particular voice is finally needed to complete the chord.

So here is the part I will not oversell, because overselling it would be its own kind of lie: I cannot hand you the words tonight. No one can print the new song on a page and let you memorize it in the car on the way to work. That was never how it worked. It is learned the way a homecoming is always learned, by coming home.

But you can find out whether the ache has been telling you the truth. You can turn, just once, toward the One the old songbook keeps naming, Yahuah, and Yeshua, who came to open the gate everyone assumed was bolted, and say, in whatever words your own language gives you: I think I have been humming Your song my whole life without knowing it. Teach me the rest.

That is not a performance, and it asks nothing of your voice. It is only the first bar. The words come after, the way they always have, not memorized, but sung over you until, slowly, they are yours.

You already know more of the tune than you think. The gate ahead may look shut; stand at it anyway, this kind opens inward. And the next time a melody catches in you and trails off into a note that isn't there, don't be so quick to call it forgetting.

It may be the part that hasn't been sung yet, waiting for you to arrive, so it can.
