The first time I watched a piano tuner work, I expected him to listen for beauty. He listened for a fight.

He would strike two strings meant to sound the same note, and instead of the single clean tone I anticipated, the air would do something restless, a slow *wah, wah, wah,* a throbbing that swelled and faded and swelled again, as if the sound itself could not settle. He would turn his wrench a hair. The throbbing would slow down. Another hair, and it slowed further, the pulses stretching further apart, *wah . . . wah . . . wah,* until, at some invisible threshold, they simply stopped, and where two strings had been quarrelling a moment before, there was suddenly one still, pure, unwavering tone, so at peace it seemed to open a window in the room.

He did not tune by chasing the pretty sound. He tuned by hunting the throb and driving it out.

I have never forgotten it, because that throb turns out to be one of the most honest things in all of music, and once you understand what causes it, you start hearing it everywhere, including in yourself.

## The sound of almost

Here is what is actually happening in that *wah, wah, wah.*

When two notes are pitched at exactly the same frequency, their waves rise and fall together, perfectly in step, and you hear one smooth tone. But when they are *almost* the same, close, but off by a hair, something strange occurs. The two waves drift slowly in and out of alignment with each other. At the moments they line up, they reinforce, and the sound swells louder. At the moments they oppose, they cancel, and the sound thins to nearly nothing. Loud, soft, loud, soft, a slow pulse that is not in either note by itself. Musicians call it a *beat,* and the closer the two pitches get to each other, the slower the beating goes, until at true unison it disappears entirely.

Sit with what that means, because it is quietly profound. That restless throbbing is not the sound of two notes that are *far apart.* Two notes far apart do not beat at all; they simply clash, or they make a chord, and each keeps its own life. The beat only happens when two notes are *nearly the same and not yet the same.* It is the specific, physical signature of *almost.* The throb is the sound of closeness that has not yet become oneness, of two things reaching for the same pitch and not quite arriving.

And the moment I understood that, a whole kind of spiritual restlessness I had felt for years, and misread for years, finally made sense.

## The ache is not distance

There is a certain unrest that the closest people to God often carry, and almost always misdiagnose.

It is not the numbness of someone far off. It is not the cold indifference of a heart that has stopped caring. It is something more agitated and more tender than that, a person who prays and still feels a pull, who has surrendered and senses there is more surrender being asked, who loves God genuinely and yet cannot shake the low, throbbing sense that they are not quite *there,* not yet fully at rest, not yet all the way in tune. And that unrest torments them, because they read it as evidence of distance. *If I were really close to Him,* they think, *I would feel settled. This restlessness must mean I am far.*

But the beat only happens when you are near.

The soul that is genuinely far from God does not throb. It clashes, or it goes quiet, or it hums along contentedly in its own key, undisturbed. The throb, that restless, swelling, will-not-settle ache for a rest you can almost taste, is not the signature of distance at all. It is the signature of *almost.* It is what closeness sounds like before it becomes union. The very unrest you have been taking as proof that God is far may be the surest evidence that He is near, and getting nearer, two pitches sliding toward each other, the beats slowing, the gap closing, the stillness just ahead.

A man who lived long ago and thought hard about God, and here I am telling you a piece of Christian tradition, not a line of Scripture, is remembered for saying that God has made us for Himself, and our hearts are restless until they find their rest in Him. I used to hear that as a diagnosis of the far. Now I hear it as a description of the *almost.* The restlessness is not the absence of God. It is the sound a heart makes while it is being tuned toward Him and has not yet come to rest.

## Rest for your souls

And here is the mercy that makes the whole picture bearable: you do not close the gap by straining.

Watch the tuner again. The two strings do not come into unison by trying harder, by throbbing more sincerely, by wanting it more. Someone turns the peg. The pitch is *brought,* gently and from outside, until it matches, and at the moment it matches, the effort ends, and there is nothing left to do but sound. The stillness is not achieved by the string. It is received.

Yeshua said something to the restless that I now hear in exactly this key. He said, Come to Me, all of you who are weary and heavy-laden, and I will give you rest. And then He said the strangest, tenderest thing, that if we would take His yoke and learn from Him, we would find rest for our souls. Not rest *from* Him. Rest *in* Him. The unrest that has been throbbing in you was never going to be silenced by you generating a purer note through willpower. It goes still the way two strings go still, when the distance closes, when the pitch is brought home, when *almost* finally becomes *one.* And that closing is His work, offered to you, not a performance demanded of you.

So if you are carrying that restless throb tonight, the ache of a faith that is real and yet not at rest, close and yet not settled, wanting God and somehow still swelling and fading and swelling with an unrest you cannot name. I want to hand you the tuner's secret.

Do not despair of the throb. It is not the sound of a soul that has missed Him. It is the sound of a soul that is *nearly there.* The beating you feel is the beating of *almost,* and *almost* is only ever a hair away from unison. You are not far. You are close enough to hear the pulse of your own nearness. Stop straining to manufacture the stillness, and simply come, near enough, and often enough, and honest enough, that the One who tunes can turn the peg. The throb will slow. The pulses will stretch further and further apart. And one day, at a threshold you will not see coming, they will simply stop, and where there was quarrelling there will be one still, pure, unwavering tone, the sound your whole restless life was reaching for, finally, and at last, in tune.
