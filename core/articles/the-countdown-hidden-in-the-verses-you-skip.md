Watch a congregation the moment the reader begins Matthew chapter one. Something happens to the room. Shoulders soften, eyes drift, a page rustles ahead to the "good part." Begat, begat, begat, forty-two links of ancient names, and the whole pew goes flat.

I have watched it my entire teaching life, and I finally got tired of it. So I made a record whose only argument is this: the list you skip is not a phone book. It is a countdown. Every one of those names is **Yahuah** ticking off the generations toward a manger, and He is not bored, even if we are. *The Line That Leads To Yeshua* is twelve songs built to make you lean back in on the exact verse you were about to abandon.

## The clock nobody reads is still ticking

The opening track, *Every Name Sings*, starts inside the moment I just described, a Sunday morning, a pastor reading Matthew one, a whole room zoning out. And then the narrator catches himself on a name, Salmon, then Boaz, then Obed, and the list stops sounding like a clock and starts sounding like a countdown. That's the hinge of the whole album.

Because Matthew tells you it's a countdown. He organizes the line into three sets of fourteen generations, Abraham to David, David to the exile, the exile to the Messiah (Matthew 1:17). That is not an accident of history. That is an author showing you a design. Fourteen, fourteen, fourteen, and then the One the whole structure was reaching for. **Yahuah** was keeping time across two thousand years, and He did not drop a single generation on the way to Bethlehem.

So the chorus of that first song is not clever. It's just true:

> Every name sings, the line that leads to **Yeshua**, Yahuah planned the whole way.

## The scandals He refused to delete

Here is where the album stops being a history lesson and starts being the gospel.

If a family were assembling a royal genealogy to impress the neighbors, there are names you would quietly lose. Matthew keeps them. On purpose. And several of them are women, which was already unusual, but look at *which* women.

There's Tamar, whose story in Genesis 38 is one anyone would call a scandal, a widow twice over, wronged by the family, who takes desperate measures and ends up bearing Judah's sons (Matthew 1:3). The song *Judah and Tamar* sits in that tension without flinching, and its whole point is one line I keep coming back to: He did not airbrush the story. He wrote the broken into the gold.

There's Ruth, a Moabite, an outsider by birth, a woman from a nation Israel was taught to keep at arm's length. And she says the most loyal sentence in the Bible: "Where you go I will go... your people shall be my people, and your God my God" (Ruth 1:16). *Ruth Said Yes* is the track, and it ends where her yes ends, as the great-grandmother of King David (Matthew 1:5, Ruth 4:17).

And there's the one Matthew names most carefully of all. He does not write "Bathsheba." He writes that David was the father of Solomon "by the wife of Uriah" (Matthew 1:6). He names the husband. He names the wound. He will not let the reader pretend the worst chapter of David's life, the sin laid bare in 2 Samuel 11 and wept over in Psalm 51, did not happen. *Mercy in the Bathsheba Line* is built entirely on that refusal. **Yahuah** did not erase her name; He named her in.

Do you feel the argument forming? The genealogy is doing gospel work. It is saying, in the driest possible form, a list, that grace is not a mood. It is a fact you can trace by name.

## The night the whole album turned on one king

The cinematic peak of the record is track eight, *The Manger Was The Mountain*, and it hangs on a name most people couldn't place: Manasseh.

Read 2 Kings 21 and Manasseh is arguably the worst king Judah ever had. He filled Jerusalem with innocent blood and burned his own sons in the fire. By any human accounting, that name gets struck from the royal line. And yet there he is, right in Matthew's list (Matthew 1:10). Why? Because 2 Chronicles 33 tells the rest of it: in captivity, Manasseh humbled himself, bent the knee, and **Yahuah** heard him and restored him (2 Chronicles 33:12-13).

That is the whole song. The manger, the lowest, smallest place, was the mountain the Messiah climbed to reach us, and He climbed it over the name of a man everyone had written off. And in the second verse I let an ordinary person stumble onto the same truth: if Manasseh made it into the line, then so can the one whose hiding isn't done. The genealogy becomes an altar call. Grace as a genealogical fact.

## The line that lands on you

The record does not end at Bethlehem. That would leave you a spectator.

*Mary's Quiet Yes* and *Joseph the Carpenter* carry the line into the first century, a young woman's "let it be to me" (Luke 1:38) and a working man who obeyed a dream, took Mary in, and gave the boy His name, **Yeshua**, which means "Yahuah saves" (Matthew 1:21). And then the finale, *The Line Lands Here*, does the thing I most wanted an album about a genealogy to do. It reaches Galatians 3:29, "if you are Christ's, then you are Abraham's offspring, heirs according to promise." The line does not stop at the manger. It runs on, and it lands on us. Every nation grafted into the same chain of names.

That's why the last chorus is not about ancient Israel at all. It's about the person listening. The line lands here.

## Who I made this for

I made *The Line That Leads To Yeshua* for two people.

The first is the one who has quietly decided that parts of the Bible are just filler, the lists, the "begats," the chapters that feel like scaffolding. I want you to hear that the scaffolding is where the tenderness hides. **Yahuah** kept those names because He keeps names. He is the God who tracks every generation through exile and famine and scandal and never loses one.

The second is the one who is sure their own name has been struck off some list. That the file is too thick, the hiding too long, the failure too public to belong in anything called a family tree of God. Tamar is in the line. Ruth is in the line. The wife of Uriah is in the line. Manasseh, Manasseh, is in the line. If those names made the countdown to the manger, hear me: there is room in the story of **Yeshua** for yours.

So next Sunday, when the reader hits the "begats" and the room goes quiet, lean in. Somebody's whole redemption is hiding in a verse you were about to skip. It might be yours.
