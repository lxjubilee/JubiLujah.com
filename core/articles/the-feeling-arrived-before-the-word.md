Something arrived in me one day, and the dictionary went gray.

I don't have a better way to say it, which is fitting, because that was exactly the problem. There was a real thing in my chest, a weight, a color, a weather, and every word I reached for slid off it. I opened the notes app and typed nothing. I stared at the blank page like it owed me an answer. I kept grabbing for the line and the line kept moving with my mind. And the culture I live in has no patience for that. It wants you to name it, brand it, caption it, and move on. But some things do not come with a name attached. They come first, and the word comes later, if it comes at all.

That gap is where the whole album lives. I wrote the first line as a confession and a small act of defiance: *I got no word for it yet, but I'm gonna dance til I do.*

## What "No Word for It Yet" Actually Means

The key word in the title is not "no." It is "yet."

I did not want to write an album about being lost. I wanted to write one about being *in process*, which is a completely different thing that looks almost identical from the outside. Lost says the thing will never resolve. *Yet* says it hasn't resolved so far, and that is allowed. So the record is a permission slip for the season when you cannot explain yourself, not even to yourself. When someone asks how you are and the honest answer is a shape with no name. The songs give that shape somewhere to stand. "The Feeling Has No Door" is about knocking on your own inside wall and finding no frame to it, *no door, no name, but it's here in the hallway just the same.* "Color With No Number" is the paint chip the chart forgot to print, honored anyway: *don't need a name to honor what I see.*

> The feeling is often older than the language we use for it. Not knowing the word is not the same as not being real.

## The Album Is a Slow Turn From Chasing to Receiving

The middle of the record is where I stopped fighting the fog and started trusting it.

"Half-Time Heart" is about keeping your own slow pulse inside a world that counts double-time, a whole song about Sabbath pace disguised as a drum-and-bass track, learning to *slow down inside the storm* until the storm becomes something you can stand in. "The Almost Word" is the fox in the fog, the thing on the tip of your tongue that runs when you chase it, and the surrender that finally catches it: *I'll catch it when I let it go.* And then the hinge of the whole album, a song called "Stop Asking, Start Listening." It is about a year of hurling questions at the ceiling, journaling pages of question marks, asking until my mouth was sore, and the night I finally sat down on the kitchen floor at midnight and heard the answer in the silence between two breaths. *I stopped chasing the word, and the word started walking toward me.*

The tenderest song is the peak, "The In-Between." It is a funeral program still in a wallet six months later, a 2 a.m. mirror, a season with no name because grief doesn't hand you one. And the line I most needed to sing: *it's not the end, it's not the start. It's the place that re-shapes the heart.* Some seasons are not detours between two named things. They are the workshop where you are quietly being remade.

## The Verse in the Wordless Place

Here is the Scripture the whole album is leaning on, and it is the one that made me brave enough to write it.

I grew up thinking prayer required the right words, that if I couldn't articulate it, I couldn't bring it. And then I really sat with what Paul wrote to the believers in Rome: that the Spirit helps us in our weakness, because *we do not know what to pray for as we ought*, and that the Spirit Himself intercedes for us with groanings too deep for words. Sit with that. The Bible names a category of prayer that has no vocabulary. Wordless groans. The exact thing I was ashamed of, that I had a feeling with no language, is not a failure of faith. It is a described, honored, Spirit-carried form of prayer. Heaven has a channel for the prayers you cannot spell.

That is why the record trusts the pause instead of panicking in it. There is a line in an old psalm, *be still, and know that I am God*, and stillness there is not passivity, it is a kind of knowing that comes only when you stop striving to name everything. **Yahuah** is not waiting for you to compose the perfect sentence before He'll listen. He was already listening in the fog, already translating the groan, already forming the word inside you before you could hear it. The last song is called "I'll Name It Tomorrow," and it is a trust-fall: *the word will arrive. I trust the trance.* The vocabulary is coming. It was already in you, waiting to appear.

> The prayer with no words is still a prayer. He hears the groan and knows the whole of it, long before you find the sentence.

## Who I Made This For

I made *No Word for It Yet* for the person in a season they cannot explain.

For the one typing nothing into a blank note at midnight. For anyone grieving something with no clean name, living in the in-between and afraid the fog means they are broken. For the person who thinks a prayer doesn't count unless it's articulate. For everyone who has been told to have it all figured out and just, quietly, does not.

You are allowed to not know the word yet. The feeling arrived before the language, and that does not make it less true. It might make it more true, older and deeper than anything you could caption. So slow your heart inside the storm. Stop chasing the word and let it walk toward you. And know that the One who searches the fog is already holding the thing you can't name, already interceding with groans too deep for words, already forming tomorrow's vocabulary in the quiet of your chest. You are not lost. You are being re-shaped in the in-between.

No word for it yet. I'll name it tomorrow.
