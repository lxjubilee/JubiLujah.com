It happens in delivery rooms, and it happens in the room next door where the machine has just gone quiet.

A woman who has run clean out of breath finds one last thing to do with the little she has left, and she sings it, low, wordless, barely a tune. And a floor below her, in a room that has gone terribly still, someone who cannot find a single word to say to the person in the bed stops trying to find one, and begins, without ever deciding to, to hum.

Two rooms. Opposite ends of a life. And the same strange thing coming out of the same strange instrument, the throat, the oldest instrument a human being owns, the one nobody had to buy or learn. Notice what it does. At the exact two moments you would expect it to fall silent, too much joy, too much grief. It does the opposite. It reaches for melody.

Why?

Hold that question, because it is older than you think, and the answer underneath it is better than you think.

Here is what I want you to see first: this is not a habit some culture invented and passed around. Dig through every people who ever lived, in every language, on every continent, and you will not find one that did not sing at its weddings and sing at its graves. Some had no written word. All of them had song. When a thing shows up everywhere, with no one teaching it to anyone, you are not looking at a custom. You are looking at wiring. You are looking at something built in.

And what it is built for is the edge.

Language is a magnificent thing, but it has a shoreline. Words are how we hand each other the manageable middle of life, directions, prices, opinions, the day's small news. But the middle is not where a life is actually decided. A life is decided out at the edges, and the edges are precisely where the words give out. Stand at a graveside and try to say what you mean. Stand at an altar, look at the one person, and try to say what you mean. You will find the sentence in your mouth is a cup, and what you are trying to pour is an ocean, and the cup will not hold it.

That is the moment. That is the exact place. And right there, where speech admits it cannot carry the weight, the human being does not go silent. The human being sings. Song is what the heart does when it is too full or too broken for prose. It is the overflow. It is what runs over the rim of the cup when the cup was never going to be enough.

So the trembling you feel when a melody undoes you in the car and you cannot explain it, that is not a weakness in you. That is the design working. You were made with a channel that opens only when words close, and music is the thing that fits it.

Now here is the turn, and I have been holding it on purpose.

If we only sang at the edges of joy, you could call it celebration. If we only sang at the edges of grief, you could call it comfort. But we do both, and that is the clue that neither one is the real reason. Watch where the singing actually goes, in the accounts that formed us.

When the sea stood up in two walls and a company of freed slaves walked out the far side onto dry ground, the very first thing the rescued did, before the shelters, before the plans, before the food, was sing. And a woman named Miriam took a tambourine in her hand, and the women went out after her with tambourines, and the desert heard a sound it had never heard. That is joy overflowing. Nobody had to organize it. It broke out.

But then follow the same instinct into the dark. There is a prison at midnight, two men with their backs torn open and their feet in wooden stocks, and what comes out of that cell into the black is not a groan. It is singing. And every other prisoner lay awake listening, because who does that? Who sings there? And there is a night, the worst night, when a man who knew exactly what the next hours held gathered his friends, and before they walked out into the dark toward the garden and the soldiers, they sang a hymn together. Into the teeth of it. On the way to it.

Do you see it now? The joy-song and the grief-song are not two different reflexes. They are one reflex, pointed in the same direction. In the light, the human heart overflows upward. In the dark, the human heart reaches upward. And it is the same direction because it is the same thing the heart has always suspected and cannot say in prose: that there is Someone at the top of the reach.

That is the secret the singing has been keeping. Praise did not come from us being happy. Praise came first, and joy is what happens when the reach connects. The Psalms are one long proof of it, a book with as many tears in it as songs, a man crying out from the pit in one breath and lifting a shout in the next, and both of them sung. The same voice said both. Because worship was never a report on your circumstances. It was a direction your heart faces regardless of them.

And here is the mercy in it, the part I most want you to take with you. You do not have to feel it to mean it. There are seasons. I know them from the inside, when praise goes flat in you and every note feels like a costume you are wearing over an empty room. Sing anyway. The obedient song and the overflowing song are both true worship, and Yahuah has never once been able to tell them apart, because He hears the direction and not the volume. He gives songs in the night. He does not wait for the morning to hand them out. He gives them at night, in the night, for the night.

So the next time you are standing at one of the edges, the good one or the hard one, and you feel the words run out and something rise up behind them that has no sentence to live in, do not swallow it. That is not nothing. That is the oldest, truest part of you finding the door that words could never open, and leaning on it.

Let it out. See where the reach lands. And if you would like to know the Name your heart has been aiming at this whole time, that is a very short conversation, and it starts the same way every song does, with one breath, offered up.
