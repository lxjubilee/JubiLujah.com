Somewhere in the world tonight, a language is dying. Linguists tell us one goes silent every couple of weeks, the last fluent speaker takes a breath, and a whole way of naming the world goes with it. Every human anthem ever written is trapped inside a tongue that someone, somewhere, cannot understand. Translate it and something leaks out. There has never been a song with words that every person alive could receive without a translator standing in between.

Except one.

The nineteenth psalm insists there is an anthem being performed right now, over your head, that every nation has already heard in full, and it contains not a single word.

## The speech that is not speech

Listen to how the psalm-writer tries to hold it, because he is clearly wrestling with a paradox and refuses to resolve it.

The heavens, he says, are *recounting* the glory of God, the word carries the sense of a scribe telling out a story, day handing the tale to day, night whispering it on to the next night. It is relentless, this telling. It never pauses. And then, in the very next breath, he says the strangest thing: there is no speech, there are no words, their voice is not heard. He has just told you creation is *speaking,* and now he tells you it makes no sound you could ever transcribe. Both at once. A telling with no language. An announcement with no audible word.

And then the line that has haunted me for years: their voice, or their measuring-line, the Hebrew hovers between the two, has gone out through all the earth, their utterance to the end of the world.

> No words. No language. No voice you could record. And yet it has already reached everyone, everywhere, with nothing lost in translation.

I used to think that was just gorgeous ancient poetry, the kind of thing you nod at and move past. But sit with the actual claim. The psalm is describing the only truly universal anthem in existence, a praise that needs no words *because* words are the very thing that would limit it. And once I understood what is physically happening in the sky, I could not read it as mere metaphor anymore.

## Why a wordless anthem travels farther

Here is what struck me, and it is simply true.

Everything that carries a word is local. Speech is sound, and sound is a slow, fragile thing. It fades within yards, it dies in a vacuum, it stops at the first wall, and even where it arrives it means nothing unless the hearer happens to know that particular language. A word is a beautiful, narrow, breakable little vessel.

But the light of the heavens is bound by none of that. The same starlight falls on every roof of every nation without asking what anyone speaks. It crosses distances that words could never survive. It requires no shared vocabulary, no interpreter, no schooling, a child in one hemisphere and an elder in the other look up and receive the *identical* transmission. The sky is broadcasting continuously, in a medium so universal that having "no words" is not a limitation on its reach. It is the *reason* for its reach. The moment you added words, you would shrink it to the size of the people who spoke that tongue.

The psalm-writer understood, three thousand years before we could describe it, that the most universal anthem possible would have to be a *wordless* one. That silence was not the absence of the song. Silence was the only way the song could belong to everyone at once.

## The praise that was already going before you woke

And this reframes something about worship that I think we badly need.

We are so verbal about praise. We measure it in words, the right lyrics, the correct doctrine in the bridge, the sentence prayed out loud. And words are a genuine gift; I would not surrender one of them. But if all praise were verbal, then praise would be rare, and human, and dependent on someone being awake and articulate and in the mood to say something.

The nineteenth psalm blows that open. It tells you that before you formed a single word this morning, an anthem of praise was already in full performance over your head, and it had been going all night, and it did not need you, and it never once stopped. The skies were recounting glory while you slept. The wordless voice was already out to the ends of the earth before your alarm went off. You did not begin the worship. You woke up *inside* it, a latecomer to an anthem that has been playing since the fourth day of the world.

I find that unspeakably steadying on the days when I have no words. And there are such days. Grief takes your words. Exhaustion takes them. Sometimes you stand in the room where everyone is singing and you cannot get a single lyric past the lump in your throat, and you feel, in that muteness, like you have dropped out of the worship entirely.

You have not. The wordless anthem is still going, and it was never asking you to narrate it. There is a praise that does not run on your vocabulary, and on your muted days it holds the room up without you.

## What the sky is teaching your soul

And I think the sky is doing something to us on purpose.

Because here is the tender turn the psalm makes. It spends its opening on this wordless cosmic anthem, the sun striding out like a runner, the heavens telling and telling, and then it pivots, without warning, inward. It leaves the sky and comes to *you:* to the law of the Lord that revives the soul, to words that are sweeter than honey, to a heart that longs to be found blameless and asks to be kept from its own hidden faults. The psalm walks you from the wordless anthem of the stars all the way down to a whispered, wordy, deeply personal prayer.

I do not think that is a swerve. I think it is the whole point. The wordless praise of the heavens is meant to *tune* you, to get the vastness of God ringing in you first, at a frequency far beyond language, so that by the time you open your mouth, your small words come out pitched to something enormous and true. Creation sings the anthem with no words so that we, who cannot help using words, will finally use them in the right key.

So tonight, before you say anything, go outside. Look up. Let the anthem that needs no translation, the one already reaching every nation without a single word, wash over you the way it is washing over a stranger on the other side of the planet at this very moment. You do not have to understand it. You could not translate it if you tried; that is the glory of it. You only have to stand under it, and let the wordless song do to your soul what it was always sent to do, get you in tune with a praise so large it had to leave language behind to hold it all.
