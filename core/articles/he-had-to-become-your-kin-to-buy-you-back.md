There is a word buried in the Hebrew Scriptures that most of us walk right past. We reach for the big theological words — *salvation*, *atonement*, *grace* — and they are true and they are glorious. But under them, older and warmer, sits a family word. In Hebrew it is **go'el**, and its verb is **ga'al**. It does not first mean *savior* in the grand cosmic sense. It means something a village would understand. It means *the relative whose job it is to get you back*.

Let me set the scene the way an ancient Israelite would have heard it, because once you hear it their way, you will never read the cross the same again.

## The Rescuer With a Requirement

In the Law given through Moshe, a family could fall apart in slow motion. A man could lose his field to debt. He could sink so low he sold himself into servitude to survive the year. A widow could be left with nothing — no husband, no heir, no future, her family line about to blink out of the record forever.

And Yahuah, in His mercy, built a rescuer into the very structure of the covenant. That rescuer was the **go'el** — usually rendered *kinsman-redeemer*. The go'el had the right to step in and buy the field back. He could pay the price to bring an enslaved relative out of bondage. He could take a childless widow and raise up a name where there was going to be silence.

But notice the requirement, because everything hangs on it. The go'el could not be a kind stranger. He could not be a wealthy benefactor moved by pity from across the country. The Law was specific and it was stubborn about this: the redeemer had to be **near kin**. Blood. Family. The word itself carries the meaning of *nearness* — the near one, the close one, the relative with the right.

A sympathetic outsider, no matter how rich, no matter how willing, had no standing to redeem you. Compassion was not enough. Ability was not enough. You had to be *related* to the one you were buying back.

Sit with that, because it is about to become the whole gospel.

## Two Men at the Gate

You know this word already, even if you did not know its name, because it runs like a river through the little book of Ruth.

Here is a widow named Naomi, empty and bitter, come limping home to Bethlehem after burying a husband and two sons in a foreign land. With her is Ruth, a Moabite daughter-in-law who had every reason to walk away and instead bound her whole life to a grieving old woman and to Yahuah. Two widows. No field. No heir. No future.

And there is Boaz — a man of standing who could have simply been generous. He could have handed Ruth some grain and gone back to his harvest. Charity would have been easy. Instead he does the harder, costlier thing: he moves to become their **go'el**. He will not just help them; he will take their emptiness into his own house and make it his own.

But there is a snag, and I love that Scripture does not hide it. There is another man — a nearer relative, first in line by law. And at the town gate, in front of the elders, Boaz gives that man the first right to redeem. The nearer kinsman is interested at first — until he learns that redeeming the field means taking Ruth, means raising up an heir who will carry *another man's* name and inherit *his* portion. And he backs out. His reason is honest and it is chilling:

> "I cannot redeem it for myself, lest I ruin my own inheritance."

There it is. He had the *right*. He may even have had the *means*. What he did not have was the *willingness to be diminished*. He would not spend himself. He would not let the redeeming cost him his own future. So he took off his sandal — the old sign of releasing a claim — and he walked away clean, and forgotten.

And Boaz stepped in and paid, and took Ruth as his own, and that Moabite widow who thought her line was finished became the great-grandmother of David. And down that same line, generations on, came One born in that very town of Bethlehem.

Two men stood at that gate. One had the right and would not be diminished. One had the right and gladly was.

## The Redeemer Who Had to Be Born

Now hold the requirement of the go'el up against Christmas, and watch what happens.

Humanity is the man who lost the field. We sold ourselves — not for a year's wages, but into the bondage of sin, and the debt was one we could never work off. We needed a redeemer. And the Law's own logic says a stranger cannot redeem us. No angel qualifies. No distant, untouched deity qualifies. Power alone does not qualify. To buy us back, the redeemer had to be **kin**.

This is why the eternal Son did not rescue us from a safe distance. He could not — not because He lacked power, but because He would not violate the very pattern He wrote into the Law. The go'el must be near. So He came *near*. He was born of a woman. He took on flesh and blood — our flesh, our blood — and became bone of our bone. The Scriptures say plainly that He had to be made like His brothers in every way. **He became our relative so He could become our Redeemer.** The manger is not sentiment. The manger is the go'el qualifying Himself.

And here is where **Yeshua** stands where the nearer kinsman would not. The man at the gate said, *I cannot redeem, lest I ruin my own inheritance.* Yeshua said the opposite with His whole life. He redeemed *knowing* it would cost Him everything — He let Himself be diminished, poured out, spent to the last drop. He did not pay the redemption price out of His surplus. He paid it with Himself. The go'el of the old villages bought back a field with silver. Ours bought back a people with His own blood.

## I Know That My Redeemer Lives

This is why, long before Bethlehem, a broken man named Job — sitting in ashes, scraped raw by loss — could say something that had no business coming out of that much pain:

> "I know that my Redeemer lives."

He did not say *I know a rescuer might come*. He used the family word — *go'el*. He was saying: I have a near kinsman with the right and the will to get me back, and He is *alive*, and He will stand upon this earth. Job was reaching, in the dark, for the very thing that would one day walk out of a tomb.

And the prophets took up the same word for Yahuah Himself — again and again He names Himself the **Go'el** of His people. Israel's Redeemer. Not a hired deliverer. The Relative who will not let His own stay lost.

So hear this, you who feel too far gone, too deep in debt, too foreign to belong at the family table. The good news is not merely that Someone strong took pity on you. It is better and closer than that. Someone made Himself your kin. He came near enough to bleed. He walked into the gate of your poverty, and while the strong of the world took off their sandals and walked away rather than be diminished, He signed His name to your debt and paid it with His life.

You were not merely helped. You were *redeemed* — bought back by a Relative who wanted you home. Naomi came back empty and Boaz filled her house. Ruth came in a stranger and went out a mother in Israel.

Come home. Your Go'el lives. And He is not ashamed to call you family.
