Sit in a concert hall five minutes before the music and you will hear something most people forget is even happening.

It is not silence. It is not the piece yet. It is a strange, patient, unglamorous sound, the whole orchestra warming up over itself, a hundred instruments each running their own scales and fragments, nothing agreeing with anything. And then one small thing happens that changes the entire room. The oboe stands, or simply lifts, and plays a single note. One note. An A. And every other musician stops their private practicing and bends toward it, the violins tightening a peg a hair's turn, the cellos leaning in, the flutes, the brass, all of them privately, quietly, adjusting until their own A lies flat against the oboe's A with no waver between them.

That is the whole ritual. Before anyone is allowed to be beautiful, everyone has to agree on one note.

I want to stay here longer than seems reasonable, because I think this small moment carries something the church has been trying to say for two thousand years and keeps saying badly.

We think the problem with unity is difference. We look around a room, a congregation, a family, a choir, a marriage, and we see how unalike everyone is, and we assume the work of belonging is to sand the differences down until we all match. Same words, same style, same volume, same taste in music, same everything. And so we spend enormous energy trying to make people identical, and we call the exhaustion of that "community," and we cannot understand why it never quite holds.

But look again at the orchestra. Nobody tuned the oboe to the violin. Nobody made the trombone play the flute's part. After the A, the cello is still a cello. The piccolo is still shrill and high and nothing like the bass. They did not become the same instrument. Not one of them surrendered what it was. What they surrendered was their private pitch, the slightly-off, self-referenced note each had drifted to on their own, and they all bent it toward one shared thing outside themselves.

That is not sameness. That is tuning. And tuning is the only thing that lets difference sound like anything but noise.

Here is the part worth carrying home: the two are opposites that get confused constantly. Sameness erases the instruments so there is nothing left to blend. Tuning keeps every instrument fully itself and aligns them to one source so that all that difference finally rings true. A room full of identical voices is not harmony. It is a single voice made loud. Harmony requires that you stay unlike me. It just also requires that we both be honest about the note.

And the note is not each other.

This is the thing the oboe understands that we forget in the pews. The violins do not tune to the violins. The section does not vote on a pitch. If everyone tuned to whoever was nearest, you would get a slow, sociable drift, each one matching the neighbor who matched the neighbor, until the whole ensemble had wandered somewhere warm and agreeable and completely wrong, and no two halls would ever play the same music. The A has to come from outside the argument. It has to be a fixed thing that no one in the room gets to bend. That is the entire point of it. You do not negotiate with the tuning note. You conform to it, gladly, because it is the one thing making all of you possible at once.

Scripture keeps reaching for exactly this. When the ancient house of worship was dedicated, the account does not describe a hundred musicians finding a groove together by feel. It describes trumpeters and singers laboring to become, as the text puts it, one sound, deliberately fused until many throats could be heard as a single voice, and it was in that oneness, not in anyone's individual brilliance, that the glory was said to fill the house. They were not identical. They had different instruments, different parts, different ranges. But they were tuned to one thing, and the tuning is what the room could not survive without.

The early church used a phrase for this that I love: they were "of one accord." We tend to hear that as one opinion, everyone agreeing about everything, which anyone who has sat through a single business meeting knows never happened and never will. But the older sense is nearer to the concert hall. One accord. One chord. Many distinct notes, tuned to one thing, sounding as one. It does not mean they were the same. It means they had stopped tuning to themselves.

Because that is the real drift, isn't it. Not that we are too different from each other. That each of us, left alone, tunes to our own pitch, our preference, our wound, our certainty, the note we happened to land on by ourselves, and calls it true because it is the only note we can hear from the inside. And two people each tuned to their own private A will scrape against each other forever and both be sure the other one is flat. Neither is willing to be the one who bends, because bending feels like losing. And a whole room can be full of sincere, gifted, devoted people, every one of them playing beautifully, and the sound is unbearable, because there is no shared note under any of it.

The gift of a fixed reference is that it ends that particular war. When there is one true A outside all of us, then bending is no longer surrender to each other. It is alignment to the same source, and the moment I stop insisting on my private pitch, I am not losing to you. I am joining you, in the only way two different instruments ever can.

So if you are worn out by the work of belonging, if community keeps feeling like a demand that you become someone else, or a slow contest over whose note the room will match. I want to hand you the oboe's quiet correction. You were never asked to become the same. You were asked to tune. To let your own drifted, self-referenced pitch bend toward the one note that is not you and not the person beside you either, but the fixed thing both of you were made to sound against. Stay fully yourself. Keep every strange and particular thing about the instrument God made you. And bend it, gladly, toward the A.

Because the second you do, the second a roomful of unalike people stop tuning to themselves and turn together toward one true note, something happens that not one of you could make alone. The difference stops being noise. The blend arrives. And the glory, the old account promises, fills the house.

Find the note. Bend toward it. And listen to what all of us become when we finally stop insisting on our own.
