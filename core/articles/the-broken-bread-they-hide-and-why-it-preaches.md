Let me be honest with you from the first sentence, because honesty is the only way this will mean anything. What I am about to describe is *not* a command from the Torah. It is a custom, a later tradition that grew up at the Passover table over many centuries, long after Moses. I will not dress a tradition in the clothes of Scripture. But some traditions, without any authority to bind you, still *preach*, and this one preaches so plainly that once you see it, you may never break bread the same way again.

## The custom, told plainly as a custom

At the Passover *seder* as it developed in later Jewish practice, there is a plate holding three pieces of *matzah*, the flat unleavened bread, stacked together. Early in the meal comes a moment, the tradition calls it *yachatz*, the *breaking*, when the **middle** piece is taken out and broken in two. The smaller portion is returned to the stack. But the larger broken portion is wrapped in a cloth and *hidden away*, set aside out of sight.

It is called the *afikomen*. And here I must be careful again: the word is not Hebrew. Scholars generally trace it to Greek, and debate its precise sense, most take it to mean something like *that which comes after*, the dessert, the final thing eaten. I tell you the debate so you know I am not inventing a root; I am handing you what is actually known and where the edges of our knowing are.

Then, at the very end of the meal, the children are sent to *find* the hidden piece. It is searched for, discovered with delight, unwrapped, and shared out so that it is the *last* thing eaten at the table. Broken. Wrapped and buried out of sight. Brought back at the end, to joy.

That is the whole custom, and I have added nothing to it. Now watch what it cannot help but say.

## Three motions the bread can't stop preaching

Set the tradition down beside the Passover Lamb it surrounds, and three motions leap out.

*The middle piece is broken.* Of three set together, it is the center one, and it alone is broken. On the night Yeshua kept this feast, He took bread, and He *broke* it, and He said the words that split history: **this is My body, given for you.** Broken bread, a broken body. I do not need the tradition to prove that; Yeshua Himself put breaking and His body together at the table with His own mouth.

*The broken piece is wrapped and hidden.* When Yeshua died, His body was taken down, *wrapped* in linen cloths, and laid *hidden* in a tomb sealed with a stone. Wrapped in a cloth. Set out of sight. Exactly the motion of the *afikomen* laid away.

*The hidden piece is brought back, and it is the joy of the end of the meal.* On the third morning the wrappings were found empty and the hidden One was *back*, sought in the dark, found alive, and the cause of a joy that ended every mourning. The bread that was broken and buried does not stay gone. It returns, and its return is the sweetest moment of the whole table.

> Broken, wrapped, hidden, sought, found, shared out at the end as joy. Whether or not anyone intended it, the custom traces the exact shape of a body given, a body buried, and a body raised.

## Why a tradition can preach without being a command

Now I want to teach you *how* to hold something like this, because the church is often clumsy here, either treating every Jewish custom as if it were binding Scripture, or dismissing every custom as if it could carry no truth at all. Both are mistakes.

Here is the careful way. The Torah commands the *unleavened bread* and the *Passover Lamb*; those are the appointed things, and they were fulfilled in Yeshua by Yahuah's own design. The *afikomen* custom is not commanded. It is a human tradition that grew up around the commanded feast. So it has *no authority to bind your conscience*. You are not obligated to it, and no one should tell you that you are.

And yet. A tradition that grows up in the soil of a feast Yahuah Himself filled with meaning can end up echoing that meaning without knowing it. The picture is not *true because the custom says so*; the picture is true because *Yeshua really was broken, really was buried, really did return*, and the custom simply happens to trace that true shape in bread. The bread does not create the gospel. The gospel was already carved into the feast, and the tradition, reaching around in the dark, laid its hand on the shape.

So we can say, with clean hands: *the afikomen is not Scripture, but it preaches Scripture.* We do not build doctrine on the custom. We build doctrine on the Lamb and the empty tomb, and we let the custom serve as a picture that makes grown believers weep, not because a tradition has authority, but because it points, unmistakably, at One who does.

## The bread He put in your hands

There is one more thing, and it is the reason this matters for your ordinary Thursday.

When Yeshua broke the bread at that Passover table and said *this is My body*, He did not leave it as a one-time moment. He said: *do this in remembrance of Me.* He took the broken, given bread and placed it, deliberately, into the recurring life of His people. The broken piece is not meant only to be admired once a year in someone else's tradition. It is meant to be *taken*, received into your own hands and your own body, again and again.

Think of the children sent to find the hidden piece so the meal can be completed. The broken bread has to be *found* and *shared* before the table is finished. And that is precisely the invitation of the gospel: the hidden One has been found, the wrappings are empty, and the last and sweetest thing at the table is the broken body *given to you*. You are not a spectator watching a custom. You are the child sent to find the bread, and the finding is meant to end in your own hands, at your own table, in remembrance of the One who was broken and buried and came back.

## So take the bread awake

Here is what I want you to carry away, holding the tradition lightly and the truth with all your strength.

The next time bread is broken before you, at a table, at a supper of remembrance, in the plainest gathering of believers, remember that there is a custom, not a command, in which the middle piece is broken, wrapped, hidden, and joyfully brought back at the end. It has no authority over you. But it traces, in flour and cloth, the very motion your salvation was made of: a body given, a body buried, a body raised. The bread that was hidden did not stay hidden. He was sought in the dark, and He was found, and He is the joy at the end of the meal.

So take the bread awake. The One who was broken for you is not lost in a tomb. He is the hidden piece brought back, found, unwrapped, and placed, alive, into your hands.
