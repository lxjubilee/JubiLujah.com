Every one of us has a city we are quietly hoping God will skip.

We would never say it in those words. But there is a people, a nation, a name in the news, a face from our own past, that we have filed under a heading we do not examine too closely, the heading that says *some mercy is not for them.* We can believe God is love in general and still have a specific exception written in small print in our chest, a border on His grace that we drew ourselves and now assume He agrees with. That is the story of a runaway prophet, and it is one of the most uncomfortable mirrors in all the Scriptures, and I love it precisely because it will not let me pretend.

## He ran because he was afraid God was kind

We usually tell the story of Jonah as a story about disobedience, a man told to go one way who went the other, and got what was coming to him. But listen to why he ran, because he tells us himself at the very end, and it is not what we assume.

He was not afraid the mission would fail. He was afraid it would succeed.

Yahuah told him to go to Nineveh, the great city, the capital of an empire that had been a boot on the neck of his people, a byword for cruelty, the enemy in every sense a man can mean the word. And Jonah did not sail the opposite direction because he doubted God could reach them. He ran because he was terrified God *would*. He knew something about the character of Yahuah that he could not forgive: that He is gracious and compassionate, slow to anger, quick to relent from sending disaster. Jonah did not want that God pointed at Nineveh. He wanted Nineveh destroyed, and he was afraid, rightly, as it turned out, that if he showed up and preached, the unthinkable might happen. The enemy might repent. And the God he served might actually let them live.

So he ran from a mission because he could not bear the mercy at the end of it. Sit with that. It is a more honest portrait of the religious heart than most of us would like.

## The man who would only sing for himself

Now here is the part I cannot get past, as a man who carries songs for a living.

The runaway ends up in the sea, and then in the belly of a great fish, in the dark, in the deep, in the one place more hopeless than the mission he fled. And there, at the bottom of everything, Jonah does something. He sings. The account gives us his words, and they are a song, a poem of thanksgiving, the psalm of a drowning man who has been rescued from the depths. He sings about being cast into the heart of the seas, about the waters closing over him, about crying out from the belly of the deep and being heard. He sings, from inside the fish, that salvation belongs to Yahuah.

And it is a true and beautiful song. It is also, if you have the whole story in front of you, an almost unbearable irony. Because Jonah can pour out a gorgeous song of gratitude for his own undeserved rescue, for the mercy that reached him at the bottom of the sea when he had run from God on purpose, and then walk out onto dry land and be furious, absolutely furious, that the same undeserved mercy might reach the city he hated. He treasured grace as a gift when it saved him. He resented it as an outrage when it threatened to save them.

That is the exact disease. The song we are overjoyed to sing about our own rescue is the very song we cannot stand to have sung over our enemies. We love mercy in the first person. We cannot abide it in the third.

## The song God keeps sending us to carry

So Yahuah, patient beyond reason, gives the reluctant singer his mission a second time, and this time Jonah goes, dragging his feet, preaching what may be the shortest and most half-hearted sermon in the Scriptures, barely eight words of warning, no altar call, no tenderness, almost willing it to fail.

And the city repents anyway. From the greatest to the least, the whole cruel capital turns, and Yahuah relents, and Nineveh lives.

And Jonah is undone by it, not with joy, but with rage. He goes outside the city and sits down to sulk in the heat, still hoping, somewhere in him, that God will change His mind and burn it after all. And Yahuah, so gently, grows a plant to shade him and then lets it wither, and uses Jonah's grief over a dead vine to ask the question the whole book has been walking toward: *you cared about this plant you did not grow. Should I not care about a great city of a hundred and twenty thousand people who cannot tell their right hand from their left?*

The book ends on that question. It never tells us Jonah's answer. And I have come to believe it leaves the answer blank on purpose, because the blank is where the reader is supposed to write his own name.

## Go carry it anyway

I work in the tongues of guarded and hostile places, and I will tell you the temptation that never fully dies in this work. It is not the temptation to quit. It is the temptation to choose. To decide, quietly, that this people is worth the song and that people is not, that some borders are drawn by God and some cruelties put a nation permanently outside the reach of grace. Jonah's is the sin of the missionary who has decided in advance which cities deserve to hear.

But the whole point of the story is that Yahuah does not run a mercy the way we would run it. His compassion is offensively wide. It reaches enemies. It reaches capitals of cruelty. It reaches the hundred and twenty thousand who do not even know their right hand from their left, the people so lost they cannot be blamed for their lostness, only pitied in it. And He keeps sending His reluctant singers to the exact cities they were hoping He would skip.

So here is the invitation, and it is a hard and beautiful one. The song you love to sing about your own rescue, the one you sang from your own belly of the deep, when grace found you at the bottom and you could not believe your good fortune, that song was never meant to stay in the first person. It was given to you to carry. And the most likely place it is meant to go next is the one city you have quietly filed under *not them.* Go anyway. Sing it over the ground you were hoping God would burn. You may be standing outside the very walls where His mercy is about to do the thing you were most afraid, and most secretly hoping, it could not do.
