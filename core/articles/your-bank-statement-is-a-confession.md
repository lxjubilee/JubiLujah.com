I used to believe my heart lived in one room of my life and my money lived in another.

The heart room was where I kept the real me, my loves, my prayers, the people I would die for. The money room was just plumbing. Boring. Numbers. Bills. I would never have said the two were connected, and I certainly would never have called a bank statement a spiritual document. Then one Sunday afternoon I opened my banking app and, half by accident, I read it the way you read a diary, top to bottom, slowly. Coffee for my sister. Books for my mind. Twenty dollars sent to my mama. And it stopped me cold, because I was reading a love letter I did not remember writing.

That afternoon became the whole album. I wrote the thesis almost as a dare to myself: *show me your receipts and I'll show you your soul.* Because it turns out the two rooms were never separate. There is only one room, and the receipt is the confession you make in it without meaning to.

## What "Where the Money Goes" Actually Means

The title is not really a question about budgeting. It is a question about worship.

Every dollar you spend is a small vote for what you believe is worth something. String a year of those votes together and you get a portrait more honest than anything you would ever post, because you can curate a feed, but you cannot curate a statement. It has already happened. It is already true. That is why one of the songs is called "Receipts Don't Lie": you can *say* you love your family, but the numbers quietly tell the story of where you actually stood. Not to condemn you. The record is careful about that, the numbers don't judge, but the numbers don't pretend. A receipt is just a mirror that already went off.

> Don't tell me you love it if you never paid for it. The heart follows the treasure. It always has.

## The Album Is a Room Full of People, Not Numbers

Here is the turn that surprised even me as I wrote it: when you read your spending as love, the money almost disappears and the *people* walk in.

"Coffee for My Sister" is four dollars at a corner shop, pumpkin spice, walked five blocks in the cold and set down at her door, because the receipt was small but the love was tall. "The Lunch I Bought You" is twenty-six dollars at a diner that I would frame if I could, because the lunch was the cheap part and the talk was the gold. And "Show Me Your Calendar" widens the whole thing, because time is a currency too, maybe the truer one. *Where the hours go is where the heart aligns.* You can audit your week the same way you audit your wallet, and it will tell you, gently, who you have actually been choosing.

Then the album reaches its quietest, holiest song, "Dad's Last Twenty." It is a memory of a father who reached into his back pocket on a prom night and pulled out a folded twenty, the only one he had, and pressed it into his daughter's palm with *"for the shoes, mija."* She did not understand until fifteen years later, when she was a parent herself, that he had an empty refrigerator on the other side of that bill. *He skipped his dinner so I could feel like a queen.* Daddy didn't preach. The receipt was the sermon.

## The Verse Written Under Every Receipt

Here is the Scripture the whole album is built on, and once you see it you cannot un-see it in your own spending.

**Yeshua** said it in one sentence that has never let me go: *where your treasure is, there your heart will be also.* Notice the direction of it. He did not say put your heart in the right place and your money will follow. He said the opposite, that your heart trails after your treasure like a body after its center of gravity. Which means your spending is not just evidence of what you love; it is one of the ways your love gets *formed*. Move the money toward a person and, over time, your heart leans that direction too. That is terrifying and it is hopeful, both at once.

And He was not impressed by the size of the gift. He watched rich men drop loud sums into the temple treasury, and then a widow put in two tiny copper coins, and He said she gave more than all of them, because she gave out of her poverty everything she had to live on. That is "Dad's Last Twenty" written two thousand years earlier. Heaven does not read the *amount* on the receipt. Heaven reads the *cost*. The folded twenty from an empty wallet weighs more in that ledger than a fortune given from the overflow.

So the album refuses to end in guilt. It ends in freedom, in a song called "Spend It Like You Love." Because the good news underneath all of this is that **Yahuah** is the most generous spender in the universe, the One who gave freely, extravagantly, His very best, and told us that what we've freely received we get to freely give. A generous heart is not a heart that has more. It is a heart that has learned where its treasure lives.

> Every dollar is a name, and every name is a kiss. Heaven doesn't read the amount. It reads the love.

## Who I Made This For

I made *Where the Money Goes* for anyone who has quietly split their life into a heart room and a money room and hoped no one would connect them.

For the person who says they value their family but can't remember the last thing they spent on them. For the one who feels guilty about money and doesn't know that guilt was never the point, alignment is. For the dad with the folded twenty and the empty fridge, who thinks nobody sees the cost. For everyone who has been managing their finances but never once let their receipts preach back to them.

Read your statement like a love letter this week. Read your calendar the same way. You will find the truth about your own heart sitting right there in the small purchases, and it is not a verdict. It is an invitation. Move a little more of your treasure toward the people and the God you say you love, and watch your heart follow it home. Pay what you love. The receipt is the confession, and grace is standing at the register.

Mira, spend it like you love.
