There is a name in nearly every congregation that no one has said out loud in a long time.

It belongs to someone who came in once. Filled out the card. Stood near the back. Maybe lifted a hand at the end of a service; maybe wept in a chair no one noticed. And then the follow-up that was supposed to happen simply didn't, because the person meant to make the call got busy, and busy is not the same as unkind, and that is almost always exactly how it happens.

No one decided to skip them. That is the quiet horror of it. They were skipped by a system that was working.

Every church builds some way to move a person from the front door toward maturity. A class. A small group. A track, a pathway, a plan. Call it whatever you like. It is good work, and for a great many people it does exactly what it promises.

But hold still with me here, because this is the part we rarely say plainly.

A pathway is built for the person who can walk it.

It serves the one who shows up on time. Who processes out loud. Who can sit in a circle of near-strangers and share on cue. Who doesn't flinch when a stranger hugs them, who isn't undone by the lights or the noise or the pressure to perform belonging before they feel any.

And the ones it quietly loses are so often the ones who needed it most.

The quiet believer, who would rather be forgotten than be a bother. The neurodiverse mind that runs on a different clock and reads a room from the outside in. The one carrying something too heavy to explain in a lobby between two songs. The person for whom a crowded room is not fellowship but noise.

They don't drop out because their faith is smaller. They drop out because the room was never shaped to hold them, and after a while a person stops offering themselves to a shape that keeps closing.

So here is the question the whole piece turns on. If our best systems are honestly built to serve the middle, the ones who fit, then what happens to everyone standing at the edges?

I want to walk you to a pool.

There is a scene near a pool ringed by five covered porches, where a crowd of sick people used to lie in the shade, the blind, the lame, the withered, all of them waiting for the water to stir. Among them was a man who had been ill for thirty-eight years. Thirty-eight. Longer than many of the people around him had been alive.

He had no one to help him into the water. Every time it moved, someone stronger, someone quicker, someone with a friend to carry them, stepped down ahead of him. He was, by every measure we use, the person the system was built to lose. He had been losing, at the same pool, for a lifetime.

And out of that entire crowd, Yeshua went to him.

Not to the front of the line. To the very back of it. To the one who had no chance of ever reaching the front.

And He asked the man a question no one had bothered to ask him in years: *Do you want to be made well?*

Sit with how strange that is. In a place full of need, He did not start with the most reachable. He went to the least reachable and knelt there.

This is not a single exception. It is the pattern of nearly everything He did.

The shepherd who leaves the ninety-nine, who are safe, accounted for, easy to keep, and goes out into the dark after the one. The short, despised tax collector up a tree, whom the whole crowd had written off, and Yeshua stops, looks up, and calls him by name. The woman drawing water alone at noon, timing her errand to miss the neighbors who had long since stopped expecting anything of her, and He is already sitting at the well, waiting, as though she were the appointment.

Even a king in the old story sends across the country for Mephibosheth, a man lame in both feet, hidden away in a town whose very name meant *no pasture, nothing here*, and seats him at the royal table, in the place of a son.

Do you see it? The One at the center of our faith has never once needed the program to find the person.

Our pipelines skip. He kneels.

And that is the thing I most need you to hear, whichever side of it you are standing on today:

> Being overlooked by people is not the same as being overlooked by Him.

The two feel identical from the inside. When every human system has passed you by, it is almost impossible not to conclude that Heaven has passed you by too. But the record is stubborn and it points the other way. The exact ones who fall through our nets are the ones He crosses the room for.

So let me speak to the two of you I imagine reading this.

If you are the one who disciples, who leads the group, runs the track, keeps the list. You don't need a better system. Systems are good. But go find the one name no one has said in a long time. Not the newcomer with promise. The person your pathway was quietly built to lose. Ask them the question at the pool. Then do the slow, unglamorous thing your programs cannot do for you: come back. Discipleship is not a pipeline anyone graduates from. It is a return, made to the same person, again, with the same patience you had the first time.

And if you are the one who was skipped, if you have read this whole way certain I was describing you, hear this and let it land somewhere deep.

You were not too much. You were not too quiet, too complicated, too slow, too odd, too broken for the plan. You were standing exactly where He has always gone first.

The water still stirs. You have simply been waiting at the wrong edge, watching a line you were never meant to fight your way to the front of.

He is not at the front of it. He never was. He is coming to the back, where you are, and He is already asking your name.
