A man said something to me once that I have never been able to put down.

We had just come off a long night of worship, the kind where the room is soaked through, where the drums have been going so long the walls have a pulse, where old women who buried husbands are up on their feet with their eyes shut and their arms open, dancing. He watched all of it from the back with his arms crossed, and afterward he came to me, and he was not unkind, but he was troubled. He said: *your people worship too joyfully for how much you have suffered. It does not add up. Where is the grief? How can a people who have lost so much dance like that?*

I did not have a clean answer for him that night. I have one now, and it took me years, and it is this: he had the arithmetic exactly backward. He thought the joy came *in spite of* the suffering, a bright coat thrown over a cold body, a denial, a way of not looking at the wound. But that is not what he was watching. He was watching something much stranger and much older.

He was watching a cross that had learned to dance.

## The two things the world keeps in separate rooms

The world I grew up in, and maybe the one you did too, is very sure that suffering and joy live in separate rooms, and that you can only be in one at a time. Grief is for the dark room with the door closed. Celebration is for the good days, when nothing is wrong. Keep them apart. Do not mix them. It is not appropriate to dance at a wound.

But my grandmother mixed them her whole life, and she was not confused. She would light a candle for the dead and then turn on the radio. She would weep at the grave and cook a feast that same afternoon and mean both. She was not being inconsistent. She knew something the man with the crossed arms did not: that in the kingdom of God, the deepest joy is not the absence of suffering. It is what suffering becomes when it is handed over to a God who raises the dead.

That is the whole scandal at the center of our faith, and we have gotten so used to it that we have stopped being scandalized. The most joyful symbol the world has ever known is an instrument of torture. A cross. A slow public killing. That is what we hang in our homes and wear around our necks and lift up over the dance floor. Think about how insane that is for a moment. It would be like a people making a party out of a gallows.

And yet. When Yeshua went to that cross, He was, the account tells us plainly, going *for the joy set before Him*. Not around the suffering. Not in spite of it. *Through* it. He endured the cross, despising its shame, for a joy He could already see on the far side of it. The suffering was real. The nails were real. And it was the very road the joy came by.

That is the cross that dances. It is not a cross that forgot it was a cross. It is a cross that went all the way down into the worst the world could do, and came up on the third day with the music still in it.

## Joy forged in the fire is a harder joy

Here is what the man in the back could not see about the old women dancing with their eyes shut.

Their joy was not cheap. That is the thing he mistook. He saw the lightness and assumed it was ignorance, that they danced because they did not know how hard life was. But those were the women who *knew*. They had buried children. They had gone hungry so the little ones could eat. They had scrubbed other people's floors and come home to sing. Their joy was not the thin, nervous happiness of people whom life has not yet found. It was the deep, unbreakable joy of people whom life found early and often and could not finish.

There is a kind of joy you can only get one way, and it is not the easy way. The Scriptures are strangely insistent on this, in a way that offends our comfort. They say to count it all joy when you meet trials, because the testing of your faith produces something that ease never could. They say that the sorrowful can be, at the very same moment, always rejoicing. They say weeping may last for the night, but joy comes in the morning, not instead of the weeping, but *after* it, on its far side, arriving in the same body that did the weeping.

This is why the songs of a suffering people hit different. There is a reason the deepest worship music on earth has so often come up out of the hardest ground, out of slavery, out of the barrio, out of the margins, out of exile. It is not a coincidence and it is not a tragedy. It is the design. Joy that has never been near a cross is a pretty thing, but it is light. It floats off in the first hard wind. Joy that has been *through* the cross and come out singing, that joy has weight. It has scars. It has, if you look closely, holes in its hands. And it cannot be talked out of the dance, because it already knows the worst and has decided to praise anyway.

## Dance at the wound, because of Who is coming for it

So I will say to you what I wish I had said to the man that night.

You are not being asked to pretend the wound is not there. That is not what the dancing means. The Gospel is not a denial. It looks the grave full in the face. It wept at the grave of a friend before it raised him, and it does not lie to you about how much it costs to be alive in a broken world. If you are in the dark room right now with the door closed, hear me: the dance is not an accusation against your grief. Grief is holy. Weeping is a form of faith. There is a time for the candle and the closed door, and God is in that room with you, and He is not asking you to fake anything.

But do not believe the man with the crossed arms when he tells you that your joy has to wait until the suffering is over. It does not. The cross did not wait. The cross went down into the very center of the suffering and turned it, from the inside, into the doorway of resurrection. That is what our God does with the worst things. He does not delete them. He raises them. He takes the instrument of death and teaches it to dance.

So when your night comes, and it will, and it may already be here, do not think you have to choose between the tears and the tambourine. My people never did. They wept *and* they danced, sometimes in the same hour, because they had staked their whole lives on the promise that the story does not end at the cross. It ends at the empty tomb. And a people who believe that can dance at a wound without lying about the wound, because they can see, past it, the One who is coming to raise them.

*La cruz que baila.* The cross that dances. Lift it up over your hardest season. And let your feet believe, before the rest of you catches up, that Sunday is coming.
