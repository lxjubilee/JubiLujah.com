I want to teach you to watch the pronouns. It sounds like the driest possible advice, pay attention to the little words, the *He* and the *You* and the *we*, but I promise it opens a door, and once you have walked through it you will never sing a worship song as a flat thing again. Because those small words are not filler. They are the wiring. They tell you who the song is actually speaking to. And the surprising answer, in nearly every good worship song, is: two people at once.

Let me show you with the oldest and most beloved song in the world, the one about the shepherd. Most people have it half-memorized without ever noticing what it does with its pronouns. It opens by talking *about* God. He is my shepherd. *He* makes me lie down in green pastures. *He* leads me beside still waters. *He* restores my soul. For four lines the singer is describing God to someone, telling of His goodness, listing what He does, the way you would describe a good father to a friend. The pronoun is *He*.

And then the path drops into the valley of the shadow of death. And watch what happens to the grammar in the exact moment the darkness closes in. The singer stops saying *He* and starts saying *You.* *I will fear no evil, for You are with me. Your rod and Your staff, they comfort me.* The description breaks off. The song turns and speaks straight to God's face. It stops talking *about* the shepherd and starts talking *to* Him, because in the valley you do not need a description of a shepherd. You need the Shepherd Himself, and you reach for Him by name, in the second person, *You are with me.*

That turn, from *He* to *You,* is one of the most moving things in all of Scripture, and it is done entirely with a pronoun. Once you have seen it, you will start seeing it everywhere.

## The vertical word and the horizontal word

Here is the structure underneath the pronouns, and it is the reason I say a worship song is always speaking in two directions.

Some of what we sing is addressed *upward*, to God, in the second person. *You are holy. You are worthy. We love You. Have mercy on us.* This is worship in its most obvious form: the creature turning its face to the Creator and speaking directly to Him. Call it the vertical word. It is prayer that happens to have a melody. When you sing *You,* you are doing the most intimate thing a soul can do, addressing the living God to His face.

But a great deal of what we sing is not addressed to God at all. It is in the third person. *He is faithful. He has done great things. Christ has died, Christ is risen. His mercy endures.* When you sing those lines, you are not, strictly speaking, talking to God. He already knows He is faithful. You are declaring it. And declaring it to whom? To the room. To yourself. To the person next to you. This is the horizontal word, worship that travels sideways, from believer to believer, teaching and reminding and building each other up in the truth. When you sing *He,* you are catechizing your neighbor.

Most of us only ever notice the vertical. We think worship is the part where we talk to God. But Scripture is startlingly explicit that worship is also the part where we talk to *each other.*

## Paul says both in a single breath

Twice the apostle Paul describes what the church is doing when it sings, and both times he says something that should reorder how you stand in the room.

He tells one church to let the word of the Messiah dwell in them richly, *teaching and admonishing one another* in psalms and hymns and spiritual songs. Teaching one another. He calls congregational singing a form of instruction, believers discipling believers by melody. And he tells another church, in almost the same breath, to *speak to one another* in psalms and hymns, *singing and making melody to the Lord.* Read that carefully. In a single sentence he says the song goes *to one another* and *to the Lord.* Both directions. At the same time. He does not make you choose. He assumes the song is doing both jobs in the same four minutes.

This is the thing the pronouns were showing us all along. When the church sings, it is simultaneously lifting the vertical word to God, *You are worthy*, and passing the horizontal word to the person beside them, *He is faithful, do not forget it.* The worshiper is a priest facing upward and a teacher facing sideways in the very same act. That is not a contradiction. That is the genius of sung worship: it multitasks in a way that no sermon and no private prayer can. A sermon is horizontal only. Private prayer is vertical only. A congregation singing is the one moment in the week when both happen in the same voice.

## Who you are singing to when you think you are only singing to God

Let me make this concrete, because it changes how you stand in the pew.

There is a person somewhere in your gathering this week who came in emptied out. They cannot pray. They cannot feel a thing. They opened their mouth to sing and nothing true would come. And then they heard the rows around them declaring *He is faithful, He has not forgotten, His mercy endures*, the horizontal word, sung sideways, from your mouth into their sinking heart. They could not say it. So you said it for them, and it held them up until they could say it again themselves. That is not a nice side effect of congregational singing. That is one of its central purposes. You were built to be someone else's voice on the day their own voice fails.

Which means your singing is never only about you and God, even in the most intimate song. When you declare who He is, you are teaching the trembling person three rows back the exact truth they will reach for at midnight when the diagnosis comes or the marriage cracks. You are laying up horizontal truth in a heart that is too tired to lay it up for itself. The vertical word rises to God. The horizontal word crosses the room and catches someone falling. Both, at once, from your one voice.

## So sing in both directions on purpose

Here is the invitation, and it will make you a different kind of worshiper starting this Sunday.

Watch the pronouns as you sing, and let them tell you which way to aim. When the song says *You*, when it turns and speaks to God's face, turn with it. Do not sing it as description. Sing it as address. Someone is listening; speak to Him. And when the song says *He*, when it declares who God is and what He has done, remember there is a whole room around you, and some of them are drowning, and your declaration is a rope. Sing it *to them.* Sing it loud enough to reach the person who has run out of faith to sing it for themselves.

The shepherd song teaches us the whole art of it in one line. It talks *about* God until the valley comes, and then it turns and talks *to* Him. Learn to make that turn, and to know which way you are aimed, and you will find that every worship song was quietly training you to do the two great works of love at once: to lift your face to Yahuah, and to hold up the person beside you, in the same breath, with the same song, in the one voice you were given.
