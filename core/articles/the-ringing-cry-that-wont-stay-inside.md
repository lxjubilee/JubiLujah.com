Some joy you can manage. You can feel it, appreciate it, keep it in a reasonable voice. And then there is the other kind, the kind that gets too big for the body that is holding it and comes *out,* whether you gave it permission or not. A laugh you could not swallow. A shout you did not plan. The sound a crowd makes when the thing they were hoping for actually happens.

The Hebrews had a word for that, and they used it for praise. The word is *ranan.*

## A word for a sound, not a decision

Most of our praise vocabulary in English names an intention. *I will worship. I choose to praise.* And there is a place for the deliberate, the willed, the disciplined offering brought when you do not feel like bringing it.

*Ranan* is not that. *Ranan* names a **sound**, specifically, a ringing, shrill, overflowing cry of joy. The lexicons reach for words like *to cry aloud, to shout for joy, to give a ringing cry, to sing out.* It is high and bright and unmistakable. It is not the low rumble of solemn reverence; it is the ringing top note of a heart that cannot contain itself.

Notice the difference from its neighbors, because the Hebrew keeps them distinct. *Halal* leans toward boasting, making a radiant show. *Shabach* is the loud, commending shout that declares who God is. *Teruah* is the blast, the alarm, the war-cry that goes up before the battle. *Ranan* is none of those exactly. *Ranan* is the **ringing overflow**, joy that has become audible because there was simply no more room for it inside.

You do not *decide* to *ranan* the way you decide to be quiet or to sing a planned song. You *ranan* the way a full cup spills. The word describes praise as a consequence of fullness.

## The command that lands on the trees

Here is where the word does something that should stop you.

The Scriptures do not only tell *people* to *ranan.* In the songs of Israel, the command lands on creation itself. The daughter of Zion is told to sing aloud, to cry out with this ringing joy. And then, in one of the most startling pictures in the whole songbook, the *trees of the forest* are summoned to do it too. The fields, the sea, the hills. The poet turns to the woods and commands them to *ring out* before Yahuah, who is coming.

Sit with that image. A God so worth rejoicing over that the created world is called to make the ringing cry, and the trees, if they could, would break into it.

> Ranan is the sound gladness makes when it stops asking whether it is allowed. It is the note a full heart hits on the way out.

Why the trees? I think because the command is not really about vocal cords. It is about *fullness meeting worthiness.* When something is truly and overwhelmingly worthy of joy, the fitting response is not measured approval. It is overflow. And if human throats fell silent, the poet insists, the sheer worthiness of Yahuah would be enough to set the forest ringing. The joy has to go *somewhere.*

## The overflow you keep corking

Now let me bring this close to home, because I think many of us have a problem *ranan* was made to cure.

We have learned to cork our joy. We feel the gladness rise, at the answered prayer, at the song that lands, at the sudden awareness of how good God has been, and we clamp it. We keep it dignified. We let a little escape, tastefully, and we press the rest back down. We have been trained to believe that maturity means keeping our worship at a moderate volume.

But a corked bottle is not more mature than an open one. It is just under pressure.

*Ranan* is the songbook's permission, no, its **command**, to take the cork out. To let the ringing joy do what ringing joy does, which is to *sound.* The word is telling us that there is a kind of praise God actually asks for that cannot be produced by trying harder to be reverent. It can only be produced by letting the fullness overflow.

And here is the tender part: you cannot fake *ranan.* You cannot manufacture a ringing cry of joy on command out of an empty heart. Which means the very existence of this word is a promise. It assumes there will be fullness. It assumes that if you stay near Yahuah long enough, honestly enough, the day will come when the joy is simply too big for the container, and the ringing cry will come out on its own, and when it does, you are not to be ashamed of it. You are to let it ring.

## When you have no ring left

I would be a poor teacher if I left it there, because you may be reading this from a season with no ringing cry in you at all. The cup is not overflowing; it feels bone dry.

Hear this gently. The Psalms that command *ranan* sit in the same book as the psalms of the pit, the tears, the *how long.* The songbook never pretends the ringing cry is always available. What it does is keep the word *there,* on the page, waiting, a memory of overflow past and a promise of overflow to come. There is a beautiful line in the Psalms about those who go out weeping, carrying seed, who will come home with this very ringing cry of joy, bringing in the harvest. (I am paraphrasing the picture, not quoting a verse number to you, but that is its shape.) The ringing cry is promised on the far side of the weeping. It is coming. The word is holding a place for it.

## Let it ring

So the next time the gladness rises in you and your first instinct is to clamp it, do not. That instinct is trained, not holy. Let the joy get loud. Let it reach the ringing top note it was always trying to reach.

You were not made to keep your best gladness at a manageable volume. You were made, like the daughter of Zion and the trees of the wood, to *ring out* before a God who is worth every decibel of it.

The cup is meant to overflow. When it does, do not apologize for the spill. Let it ring.
