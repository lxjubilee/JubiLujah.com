Here is a command in the Torah that almost no one preaches, and it is one of the quietest, strangest, most tender things God ever asked His people to do.

He told them to count.

Not to count sheep, or count their blessings as a figure of speech. To literally *count the days*, to stand up, evening after evening, for seven full weeks, and say the number out loud. Today is day one. Today is day two. All the way to forty-nine, and then, on the fiftieth, a feast. The Word is specific about it: you shall count fifty days. Number them. Do not let them blur.

I have come to love this command more than almost any other, because of *where* the counting starts and *where* it lands. And once you see the two ends of it, the fifty days in between stop looking like a chore and start looking like a road.

## From the lamb

The counting begins at Passover.

Passover is the night of the lamb, the night the whole story of the people of God turns on. Yahuah brings Israel out of Egypt with a strong hand; the blood on the doorposts turns death away; the sea opens and closes; a nation of slaves walks out into the dark as free people. It is the founding rescue, the deliverance every later song looks back to.

And then, in the days right after that Passover, the people were to bring the very first sheaf of the new barley harvest, a bundle of grain called the *omer*, and lift it up before God as the firstfruits of what the land was beginning to give. It was a small thing, one sheaf, waved in the morning air. But it was the starting gun. From the day that sheaf went up, the counting began. Seven weeks. Forty-nine days. And Hebrew has a lovely, plain name for the whole stretch: to this day it is called *the counting of the Omer*, the counting of the sheaf.

So the count starts at rescue and firstfruits. It starts the morning after freedom, with the very first handful of harvest lifted toward heaven. Day one begins in the afterglow of the lamb.

## To the fire

And where does it land? On a feast called *Shavuot*, the Feast of Weeks, named for the seven weeks you counted to reach it. You may know it better by the name that comes from the Greek word for *fiftieth*: Pentecost.

Shavuot was, first of all, a harvest feast, the day you brought the firstfruits of the *wheat*, the fuller harvest that the barley had only begun. Fifty days of counting, and at the end, a table piled with the first of the real bread. The count was never empty waiting. It was the slow filling-up of a harvest, watched day by day as it ripened toward the feast.

But there is more, and here I have to be careful and tell you which is Scripture and which is the tradition built around it. The Torah ties Shavuot to the harvest. The ancient Jewish tradition added something the text does not spell out but that generations have cherished: that Shavuot is also the anniversary of the day at Mount Sinai, the day the fire and smoke came down on the mountain and Yahuah gave His people the Torah, the instruction, the words that made them who they were. I offer that as tradition, a beautiful and very old one, not as a claim the Bible states outright. But the timing fits, and the meaning is lovely: the people counted from their rescue out of Egypt straight to the mountain where they received the Word. From freedom to the fire on the mountain. Fifty days.

And then, long after Sinai, on one particular Feast of Weeks, Scripture records something that makes the whole ancient count blaze. On the day of Pentecost, when the followers of Yeshua were gathered together, there came a sound like a rushing wind, and what looked like tongues of *fire* came to rest on each of them, and the Spirit of God filled them, and they spilled out into the streets and were understood by people from every nation under heaven. The fire that once fell on a mountain fell, this time, on ordinary people. The Word that had been carved on stone was now written on hearts.

Fifty days. That is the road. It runs from the lamb of Passover to the fire of Pentecost, from blood on the doorposts to the Spirit falling on flesh. And God asked His people to walk it one counted day at a time.

## Why count at all?

So why the counting? Why not just keep the two feasts and let the days in between take care of themselves?

Because there is something worship needs that only *counting* can teach, and we have almost entirely lost it.

To count the days toward a thing is to refuse to sleepwalk through your own life. It is to take the ordinary, unremarkable Tuesday between the mountaintop you just left and the one you have not reached yet, the flat, in-between day when nothing happens and the harvest is not in and the fire has not fallen, and to *dignify* it by naming it. Today is day twenty-three. This day counts. This day is part of the road. God did not tell His people to endure the gap between deliverance and Pentecost. He told them to *number* it, to hold each day up like the sheaf, to treat the waiting itself as sacred ground.

That is a discipline, and it is a form of worship, and most of us have never practiced it. We know how to celebrate the feast days. We do not know what to do with the fifty ordinary days in between, so we let them slide by uncounted, ungrateful, half-asleep, waiting for the next big moment. The counting of the Omer is God's gentle correction. It says: *the days between the miracles are not dead time. Count them. Watch the harvest fill. Something is ripening in you on day thirty that could not exist if you skipped straight to the feast.*

There is even a real spiritual honesty built into it. You cannot get from Passover to Pentecost in one leap. You cannot go from the night you were rescued to the day the fire falls without walking the forty-nine days in between. The Spirit came *fifty days* after the lamb, not the next morning. There was a road, and it had to be walked, and it had to be counted.

## The invitation

So let me hand you the oldest overlooked discipline of worship, and it costs you nothing but attention.

Learn to count your days. Not with dread, ticking off a sentence, but the way the ancient people counted the Omer, holding each day up as firstfruits, watching for the harvest to fill, walking the real road between where God rescued you and where His fire is going to fall. You are almost certainly living, right now, in the middle of a fifty-day stretch. Something has already delivered you; something has not yet arrived. The lamb is behind you and the fire is ahead, and in between there is a long string of ordinary days that you are tempted to waste.

Do not waste them. Number them. Say it, if only to yourself, at the end of the flat and unremarkable day: *this one counted. I am one day nearer.* The people who first walked from the lamb to the fire did not sleep through the middle. They counted every step. And when the fiftieth day came, and the fire fell, they were awake to see it.

Count the days. There is a feast at the end of them, and a road worth blessing all the way there.
