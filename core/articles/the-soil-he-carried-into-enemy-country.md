The most decorated soldier in the story asked, at the end of it, for two mule-loads of dirt. Not gold. Not a prophet to come live with him. Dirt. And when you understand why, it will change how you think about worship in the places it is not welcome.

His name was Naaman, and he was the commander of the army of Aram, the country north and east of Israel, a rival, a raider, an enemy with a long memory. He was, the record says, a great man and a valiant one, held in high honor because through him Yahuah had given victory to Aram. Sit with that a moment. The God of Israel had been quietly handing wins to Israel's enemy through a general who had never once thanked Him and did not know His name. That is how far ahead of us God already is in the places we assume He has abandoned. He was working in the enemy's war room before anyone thought to pray about it.

But Naaman had leprosy. All the honor in the world, and a body that was slowly turning against him.

## The frontier's first missionary was a stolen child

Now watch who carries the light into this house, because it is nobody a strategy would have picked.

On one of Aram's raids into Israel they had taken captive a little girl, and she had ended up as a servant to Naaman's wife. Think about what that means. This child had been torn out of her home by the very army her master commanded. She had every reason on earth to keep her mouth shut and let the great man rot. Instead she said one sentence to her mistress, *if only my master would go to the prophet who is in Samaria, he would cure him of his leprosy.*

That is it. That is the whole missionary sermon. One homesick captive girl, in the house of the enemy, saying she knew a God who could heal. No platform, no freedom, no safety. The gospel to Aram did not arrive with an army or a delegation. It arrived in the memory of a kidnapped child who could not stop believing in the God of the country she had been dragged out of. I have crossed a lot of borders carrying songs into hard places, and I have never once outrun this little girl. She got to enemy soil generations before I did, and she got there in chains, and she witnessed anyway.

## He had to be talked down from his own pride

The word travels up the chain, servant to mistress, mistress to master, master to king. And the king of Aram, being a king, does it the way power does everything: with weight. He sends Naaman off with a fortune and an official letter, as though healing were a thing you could requisition between governments. The king of Israel tears his clothes in a panic, sure it is a pretext for war.

But the prophet Elisha hears of it and sends a message: *send him to me.* And when this glittering war-hero pulls up at the prophet's door with his horses and chariots, Elisha does not even come outside. He sends a servant with instructions: go wash in the Jordan seven times, and your flesh will be restored.

Naaman is furious. He came expecting a ceremony worthy of his rank, the prophet waving his hand over the spot, calling on his God with theater. Instead he is told to go dunk himself, like a commoner, in a muddy little river of Israel, when the rivers back home in Damascus are cleaner and grander. He turns to leave in a rage, and once again it is the servants, the small people, the ones nobody was consulting, who save him. *Father,* they reason with him, *if the prophet had told you to do some great thing, would you not have done it? How much more, then, when he simply says, wash and be clean?*

So the general swallows his pride, walks down into the enemy's river, and dips himself under seven times. And when he comes up the seventh time, the record says his flesh was restored like the flesh of a little child. Clean. New. The exact language, notice, of a little child, the same kind of small, overlooked person who had started the whole thing.

## Two mule-loads of holy dirt

Here is where the soldier asks for the dirt, and here is the heart of the whole matter.

Naaman comes back to Elisha a changed man and makes a confession that outruns anything most of us have said out loud: *now I know that there is no God in all the earth, except in Israel.* The enemy commander, healed in the enemy's river, standing on the enemy's ground, declares that the God of the people his army has been raiding is the only real God there is. He tries to press a fortune on Elisha, and Elisha refuses every coin of it, because this healing was never for sale and would not be made to look like it was.

And then Naaman makes his request, and it is one of the tenderest, most practical acts of new faith anywhere in Scripture. He asks to take home two mule-loads of earth. Israelite soil. Actual dirt, dug from the ground where he now knew Yahuah was real, loaded onto animals and hauled back across the border into Aram, because, he says, from now on he will never offer sacrifices to any god but Yahuah, and he wanted a patch of true ground to build his altar on.

Understand what that soil was. It was not superstition, though it might look like it. It was a man who had met the living God in a country he was about to leave, and who could not bear to go home to a land with no house for that God, no altar, no fellow worshipper, not one, and so he carried the ground itself, so that he would have somewhere to kneel. He was going back to the capital of a pagan empire to be, quite possibly, the only worshipper of Yahuah in the entire country. And he refused to go empty. He loaded up the dirt and went to build worship where there had never been any.

He even names the hard part honestly: when his master the king leans on his arm in the temple of the Aramean god and bows, Naaman's own body will bow too, and he asks pardon for it in advance. Elisha does not lecture him. He says, simply, *go in peace.* Because God knew what we so often forget, that the man was walking back into a place with no support, no covering, no congregation, and that the mule-loads of dirt in his caravan were the first altar of a worship that had no business surviving there and would anyway.

## Where you are the only altar

I have given my life to worship in the guarded countries, carrying songs in Arabic and Turkish and Romanian into rooms where there is no church down the street, no worship night to attend, no one to hand the melody to but the four walls. And Naaman's caravan of dirt is one of the pictures I keep in front of me, because it answers the loneliest question the frontier asks: what do you do when you have met God in one place and have to live in another where nobody knows Him?

You carry the ground home. You do not wait for a building to appear or a congregation to gather or the country to change first. You build the altar out of what you were given, on the hostile soil where you actually live, and you kneel there whether or not anyone kneels beside you. The captive girl witnessed in chains. The general worshipped in the shadow of a rival temple. Neither of them had permission or company, and both of them changed the spiritual map of an entire nation with an act that looked, from the outside, absurdly small.

If you are reading this in a house, a family, a workplace, a whole country where you are the only one who knows the name. You are not too small and you are not too alone to build the altar. Load up the dirt. Carry home the patch of true ground you have been given. Kneel on it in enemy country, where worship was never supposed to grow, and watch what the God who was already winning battles in the enemy's war room does with one bold new worshipper and two mule-loads of holy dirt.
