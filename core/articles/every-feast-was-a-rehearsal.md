Every actor knows the strange holiness of a dress rehearsal.

The theater is empty. There is no audience yet. And still you play the whole thing in full costume, hitting every mark, saying every line, performing a story that has not, in any real sense, happened. You are enacting the future. You are rehearsing a night that is still to come.

I want to hand you a Hebrew phrase that has made grown teachers go quiet, because it suggests that this is exactly what Yahuah had Israel doing for fifteen centuries before the story it rehearsed ever occurred.

The phrase is *miqra kodesh* (מקרא קדש).

## Not just a "holy gathering"

Your English Bible almost certainly renders it *holy convocation*. Over each of the appointed times, Leviticus stamps that label: it shall be a *miqra kodesh* to you. And because "convocation" is a dusty word, most of us slid right past it, hearing something like "a sacred meeting", a church service with better robes.

But look at the root. *Miqra* comes from *qara* (קרא), one of the busiest verbs in the Hebrew Bible. *Qara* means to call, to summon, to cry out, and also, tellingly, to *read aloud*, to proclaim a thing so that it is heard. It is the very verb behind the reading of the Scripture in the assembly; to this day the Hebrew word for the Scriptures-to-be-read is *Miqra*.

So a *miqra kodesh* is not a passive meeting you attend. It is a **holy summoned-together**, a gathering called out by proclamation, an assembly convened for a purpose and, in that older sense of the word, a thing to be *read out loud* in front of everyone. The feast is not a day you happen to observe. It is a day on which the people are summoned to gather and *enact* something, publicly, deliberately, together.

## The word that quietly says "rehearsal"

Now here is where I have to be a careful teacher, because this is where enthusiasm sometimes outruns the text, and I will not do that to you.

Many Hebrew-roots teachers translate *miqra* as *rehearsal*. You will hear it said flatly: "the feasts are the Lord's dress rehearsals." I want you to know precisely what that is and is not. The lexicon's plain meaning of *miqra* is a called assembly, a convocation, a proclaimed gathering. *Rehearsal* is not the dictionary definition; it is a teaching emphasis, an interpretation drawn from putting two true things side by side. And I think it is a beautiful and defensible one, as long as we are honest about which is which.

Here are the two true things. First: a *miqra* is a summoned gathering convened to *do* something in the sight of all, to act it out, to perform the appointed motions, to proclaim it publicly. Second: the New Testament writers say plainly that the feasts were a *shadow* of things that were coming, and that the substance casting the shadow was the Messiah.

Lay those two truths together, and the word *rehearsal* is not a stretch. It is what you get when a summoned people enact, year after year, in full costume, a drama whose real performance has not yet arrived.

> They were not merely remembering the past. They were rehearsing a future they could not yet see, walking through the motions of a redemption that had not happened yet.

## Rehearsing a play they had never seen performed

Feel the wonder of that. On Passover, a lamb without blemish is chosen, kept, inspected, and its blood applied so that death passes over the house. The people did this for centuries, a full-dress enactment of a rescue by the blood of a spotless lamb, long before there was a Lamb of Elohim standing in the doorway of the world.

At the feast of Firstfruits, the very first sheaf of the harvest was lifted and waved before Yahuah, a promise that the whole field was coming. Israel rehearsed *resurrection*, the first of the harvest raised up as the pledge of all the rest, generations before an empty tomb made the pledge good.

Fifty days on, at Shavuot, they gathered again, and this appointed day would one day be the very date the Ruach HaKodesh fell in fire and every tongue was heard. Israel had been holding the dress rehearsal for the birth of the Spirit-filled people since before there was a Pentecost to fill.

Do you see what this does? It means Yahuah did not improvise the gospel in a corner of the Roman Empire. He had been *staging* it, in the open, in front of the whole nation, feast after feast, year after year, until the day the real thing walked onto the stage and every rehearsed motion suddenly snapped into meaning. The cast had been running the play for fifteen hundred years. Most of them never guessed what it was about. And then one Passover, the Lamb of Elohim stepped into the role, and the whole theater understood at once what they had been rehearsing all along.

## Why a rehearsal, and not just a sign

Someone will ask: why would Yahuah bother with rehearsals? Why not simply tell the story when the time came?

Because a rehearsal does something a story cannot. A story you hear; a rehearsal you *are in*. Yahuah did not want Israel to be an audience learning about redemption. He wanted them inside it, hands on the lamb, feet moving through the motions, so that the truth would be written into the muscle of a whole people before it was ever written into history. When the substance finally came, they would not be meeting a stranger. They would be meeting the One they had been playing toward their whole lives.

There is a further comfort tucked into this, and I do not want you to miss it. If Yahuah was willing to have His people rehearse the redemption for fifteen centuries before it came, then He is a God who prepares, patiently, thoroughly, over generations, for the good He intends to do. Nothing about the cross was hasty. Nothing was improvised. The whole thing was staged with the care of a Father who leaves nothing to chance where His children's rescue is concerned. And the same is true of the appointments still ahead: the fall feasts are rehearsals still running, and they are running because He is still preparing to keep them.

And that, quietly, is the invitation to you as well. The feasts are not museum pieces. They are the choreography of the gospel, still teaching anyone who will walk through them what the Messiah has done, and, in the appointments still ahead of us, what He has yet to do.

So the next time you read those two small words, *holy convocation*, do not let them lie flat. Hear the summons in them. Hear the proclamation. And hear, underneath, the astonishing thing generations of teachers have heard: a people called together to rehearse a rescue they had not yet seen.

The rehearsals ran for centuries. The curtain has already gone up on the spring. And the theater is still full, waiting for the final act.
