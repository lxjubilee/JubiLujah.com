The lie goes like this: clean yourself up first. Get your life presentable. Then walk back to the church door and hope God is still willing to take a returning mess. I have watched that lie keep people out in the cold for years. I made *Dancehall Redemption* to burn it down, because it is the exact opposite of how the rescue actually happens.

He does not wait at the steeple for you to come crawling back. He comes out to where you are, into the strobe, into the bass, onto the floor, and He pulls you off it. And here is the part that undoes me every time: He does it without shaming you.

## The Night He Walked Into the Noise

The center of this album is a song called **He Pulled Me Off the Floor**, and it is a scene I have lived and watched too many times to count. A club at two in the morning. The sub-bass kicking your rib cage. A tear in your eye you are trying to hide from friends who never notice you leave.

> He didn't wait at the church steeple. He walked onto the floor of the people. Reached through the strobe and the bass. He pulled me off, that was grace.

I anchored that on the eighth chapter of John, on the woman they threw at **Yeshua's** feet, caught and humiliated, expecting stones. What did He do? He did not pile on. He straightened up and said, *Neither do I condemn thee: go, and sin no more* (John 8:11). He got her off the ground without adding one ounce of shame to what she was already carrying. That is the God I know. He reached through the crowd and pulled her up, and the reaching was the whole gospel.

## Same Bass, Brand-New Heart

People assume an album about the dancehall must be about leaving the culture behind, turning the volume down, trading the riddim for an organ. That is not what happened to me, and it is not what this record says.

The opener, **Kingston Bass Salvation**, tells it straight: *same speakers, same bass, new heart.* When **Yeshua** found me, He did not confiscate the low frequencies. He rewrote what they were pointed at. The energy that used to run toward everything that was quietly killing me. He redirected it, whole, toward Him. That is what **Bass Drop, Soul Stand Up** is about: *same body, brand-new King.* Redemption is not amputation. It is re-aiming.

Because the chains were real, and I name them on **Chains Beneath My Feet**, the drinking chain, the lying chain, the fear chain, each one dragging at the dance. **Yeshua** walked them down to the cross. This is exactly the ministry He announced for Himself when He stood up in the synagogue and read the scroll: *He hath sent me to heal the brokenhearted, to preach deliverance to the captives... to set at liberty them that are bruised* (Luke 4:18, from Isaiah 61). Liberty to the captives. He was never squeamish about where the captives were being held.

And that liberty is not a Saturday-night novelty that fades by Sunday afternoon. **Saved Like Sunday Morning** carries the whole record into daylight, the same person who walked out of the far country now sitting in a pew with the light coming through the windows, made new. Redemption is not just a dramatic rescue in the dark. It is the ordinary Sunday that follows, and every ordinary Tuesday after that, held by the same hand that first reached through the strobe.

## The New Name Nobody at the Dance Ever Called You

There is a moment on this record I hold as tenderly as anything I have made, **New Name on the Riddim**. The old name, what the dance crew called you, what your past kept calling you, what you started to believe was the truest thing about you, goes down into the water. And a new one comes up.

That is not a metaphor I invented. **Yeshua** promises the overcomer *a white stone, and in the stone a new name written, which no man knoweth saving he that receiveth it* (Revelation 2:17). A name only He and you will ever fully know. Because when a person comes to Him, *old things are passed away; behold, all things are become new* (2 Corinthians 5:17). The bio changes. The story changes. The name the strobe lights knew you by does not get to follow you home.

> Old name washed at the cross, new name on the riddim now.

## The One at 2:47 in the Morning

The testimony I am most protective of on this album is the quietest one, **Riddim of My Repentance**. It is a real hour: a prodigal walking the long block home at 2:47 in the morning, streetlight low, first whispered prayer rising up before they even reach the door. And the line I built the whole song to deliver: *Jesus meets you where you stand, whether it's the altar or the dancehall sand. He doesn't wait for you to come.*

That is Luke 15, the father who *when he was yet a great way off... ran* (Luke 15:20). He did not stand at the gate with his arms folded, waiting for a proper apology. He ran down the road, undignified, to a son who still smelled like the far country. The running is the point. God is always the one who closes the distance.

## Who I Made This For

I made *Dancehall Redemption* for the one who thinks they are too far gone, too deep in it, too many nights into a life they cannot see a way out of. For the returner walking in with the night still warm on their skin, bracing for the church to wince. For anyone who believes the rescue is conditional on cleaning up first.

Hear me, and hear me as a pastor: you have the order backwards, and it is keeping you on the floor. He does not wait for you to get up. He comes down to where you fell, reaches through everything you are ashamed of, and lifts you, and only after you are standing in His arms does the cleaning up begin, and even that He does with you, not to you. That was grace on the dance floor. It is grace right now, wherever you are reading this.

You do not have to make it back to the door. He already walked out to meet you. Let Him pull you off the floor.
