Imagine a party thrown for a book.

Not a book launch, not a quiet reading, not a lecture with folding chairs. A *party.* Music. Circles of dancing that go on for hours. People taking the scrolls out of the cabinet where they are kept, wrapping them up carefully, holding them against their chests the way you hold a sleeping child, and then *dancing* with them. Spinning. Laughing. Passing the scrolls from arm to arm so everyone gets a turn to carry the Word around the room. Circuits and circuits of it, sometimes late into the night, on a day whose entire purpose is to be glad about the Scriptures.

The day is called *Simchat Torah*, the words mean "the joy of the Torah," or "rejoicing in the Torah." And before I say one more glorious thing about it, let me be straight with you the way I am required to be: this feast is **later tradition.** It is not one of the appointed times commanded in the Torah itself. It grew up long after, as a joyful cap on the fall festival season. So when I tell you about the dancing and the scrolls in the arms, hear me clearly. I am describing a beautiful custom, not a command from Sinai. But mi gente, sometimes a custom captures a truth so well that heaven must smile at it. And this one does.

## Finishing the book so you can start it again

Here is the reason for the whole celebration, and it is one of the most tender ideas I know.

The people who keep this feast read through the Torah, the five books of Moses, on a schedule that takes an entire year. A portion each week, faithfully, all the way through the seasons, until the very last week when they reach the end. And the end of the Torah is not a happy ending, not on the surface. It closes with the death of Moses, the greatest prophet, standing on a mountain looking at a promised land his own feet will never walk into. That is where the scroll runs out. On a grave, and a longing.

So what do they do when they reach that last, aching word?

They do not close the book and put it away for a rest. On the very same day, in the very same breath of celebration, they **roll the scroll all the way back to the beginning and start reading it again**, *"In the beginning, God created the heavens and the earth."* From the death of Moses to the making of the world, with no gap in between. They finish and they begin in the same joyful hour, so that there is never a single day when the reading of the Word has stopped.

Do you feel how gorgeous that is? They refuse to let the story ever be *over.* The last word hands you straight back to the first word. The grave hands you back to *let there be light.* And the joy of the day is the joy of a people who cannot stand the thought of a single day without the Word open in front of them, so they make sure the ending is also a beginning, and they dance in the seam between the two.

## Loving the Word with your whole body

I have to sit with what it means that they *dance* with it.

Not study it. Not merely revere it from a respectful distance. They pick the scrolls up, hold them close, and *move.* This is worship done with the feet and the arms and the sweat of the whole body, not just the head. And I love that with everything in me, because a lot of us have been quietly taught that loving the Word is a serious, seated, silent activity, something you do with a furrowed brow and a highlighter, alone.

> The Word of God was never meant to be only studied. It was meant to be *embraced*, carried, held against the chest, danced with until your feet hurt from gladness.

The tradition of the circuits, the going around and around the room with the scrolls, turns loving the Scriptures into something a child can join, something an old woman can join, something the shy man at the back can be pulled into whether he understands every verse or not. You do not have to be a scholar to dance. You just have to be glad. And there is a kind of knowing the Word that only opens up to you when you have held it in your arms and celebrated it with your whole body, that will never open up to you from a chair.

I think heaven is far more like the dancing than like the folding chairs.

## The Word you can finally hold

But now I have to tell you the thing that makes me want to jump into the circle myself, because for those of us who follow Yeshua, this feast points at something almost too good.

They dance holding the Word wrapped in their arms like a beloved child. And there came a day, mi gente, when the Word stopped being only ink on a scroll and became a Man you could actually hold. Yeshua is called the Word, the Word that was in the beginning, the Word through whom the heavens and the earth were made, the very "in the beginning" that the scroll rolls back to. The Word did not stay on the page. The Word put on skin and walked into the world, and there was a night when an old man in the temple named Simeon literally took the Word of God up into his arms, held Him, an infant, against his chest, and blessed God that his eyes had seen the promised rescue.

That is Simchat Torah made flesh. The joy of holding the Word became the joy of holding *Him.* And notice what the scroll was always telling us with its beautiful refusal to end: it closes on a death and reopens on creation, over and over, year after year, because the story of God has never once been allowed to stay at the grave. It always rolls forward into new beginning. And Yeshua is the One in whom that pattern stopped being a poem and became history, the death that turned straight into a new creation, the last word that became the first word, the tomb that opened onto *let there be light.*

So when I picture them dancing with the scrolls in their arms, I do not see a quaint old custom. I see a whole people acting out, without always knowing it, the deepest joy there is: the Word of God is not a distant thing to be feared. It is something, *Someone*. You get to hold.

## So take Him in your arms and dance

Here is what I want you to carry away from the gladdest day on the calendar.

Stop keeping the Word at arm's length. Stop treating it like a stern examiner you have to satisfy. The people who dance with the scrolls have understood something the anxious student never will: the Word is not against you. It is *for* you, it is *yours*, and it wants to be held close and celebrated, not filed away and feared.

And when you get to the hard endings, the pages that close on a grave and a longing, the seasons where the story seems to run out on a mountain looking at a land you will not reach, do what they do. Do not close the book. Roll it forward. Trust that in the God of Yeshua the last word is always secretly the first word of something new, and let the ending hand you straight back to the beginning.

Then get up. Take the Word into your arms. And dance, familia, dance until your feet hurt, because the Word came near enough to hold, and that is worth every circle you can make around the room.
