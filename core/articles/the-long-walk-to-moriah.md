We always tell this story as if it happened in an instant, and it did not. It took three days.

You know the shape of it. An old man is asked to give up the one thing his whole life had been aimed at, the son of the promise, the boy he waited a hundred years for, and to give him up on a mountain, as an offering. It is the hardest command in all of Scripture, and we tend to fast-forward through it to the knife and the ram, because the ending is where the relief is. But I want to slow the account down to the pace it actually moved at, because between the command and the mountain there is a road, and the road is three days long, and I think the road is where the real thing happened.

The account says Abraham rose early in the morning. He split the wood for the offering himself, an old man, at dawn, cutting the very wood his son would be laid on, with his own hands. He saddled the donkey. He took two servants and the boy, and he set out toward the place God had told him about. And then comes the line that undoes me every time I sit with it: on the third day, Abraham looked up and saw the place in the distance.

The third day. Which means the first day, and the second day, he woke up with it. He walked all day with it. He lay down at night still carrying it, and got up in the morning and it was still true. Whatever he had settled in his heart back home in the first terrible hour of obedience, he had to re-settle it every single mile, for seventy-two hours, with the mountain getting slowly closer the whole time.

## Worship is mostly the walk

Here is what I have learned climbing real mountains, and it is exactly the same thing: the summit is a moment, but the climb is the mountain. You do not become the kind of person who can stand at the top by standing at the top. You become that person on day two, on the switchback nobody photographs, when the peak is still a far grey shape and every part of you is negotiating whether to keep going. The mountain is not the last hundred feet. The mountain is all of it.

We have almost the opposite idea about worship. We think worship is the moment, the altar, the raised hands, the surrender that finally breaks. And it is that. But before Abraham ever built a single altar on Moriah, he called what he was doing by its name. He turned to the servants at the foot of the mountain and told them to wait, and said, in effect: *the boy and I are going over there to worship, and we will come back to you.*

Sit with the word he used. Not "I am going to go through the worst thing a father can do." He called it *worship*. He had been calling it worship for three days of walking. Which means the worship was not only the knife on the altar. The worship was the whole obedient trudge toward a thing he could not understand and did not want and would not stop walking toward. The surrender was already happening at three miles an hour, in the dust, with a boy asking questions.

And notice what else he said to those servants: *we* will come back. Not "I will come back." He was climbing toward what looked like the end of everything, and somehow, out of a faith I can barely comprehend, he had already decided that the God who gave the promise would keep the promise on the far side of the altar, that he would come down the mountain with his son somehow, some way. He did not have the answer. He had the God. And he started walking, and he told the truth about the God before he had any evidence for it.

## The provision was already on the mountain

Here is the mercy hidden in the long walk, and I do not want you to miss it.

The whole way up, Abraham did not know how it ended. The boy carried the wood. Abraham carried the fire and the knife, the dangerous things, the things a father would never let a son carry. And the boy, no fool, finally asked the question that must have gone through the old man like a blade: *father, here is the fire and the wood, but where is the lamb?* And Abraham said the truest thing he knew: *God will provide the lamb, my son.* He did not know that yet. He believed it. He spoke it uphill into the silence, and kept climbing.

And then, at the very top, after the altar was built and the boy was bound and the hand was raised and Heaven itself stepped in to stop it, Abraham looked up, and there in a thicket was a ram, caught by its horns. The provision was not sent from somewhere far away in the nick of time. The ram was already on the mountain. It had been up there, in the brush, the entire three days Abraham had been walking toward the place, terrified and obedient and not knowing. The answer was ahead of him on the trail the whole time. He just could not see it until he had climbed all the way up to where it was waiting.

He named that place a name that God's people have carried ever since: *Yahuah will provide.* Or more literally, *Yahuah will see to it*, on the mountain of Yahuah, it will be seen. And the name is not really about a ram. It is about a discovery you can only make by walking the whole road: that the God who asks you to climb toward the impossible has already gone up ahead of you and put the provision in the thicket, out of sight, waiting for the exact altitude where you will finally need it and finally find it.

## For the one on day two

So let me speak to whoever is somewhere in the middle of their own three-day walk, not at the command anymore, and not at the altar yet, but out on the road in between, where it is hardest.

You said yes to God about something a while ago. You surrendered it in a rush of grace, and you meant it. And now you are on day two, and the mountain is still far off, and you have discovered the thing nobody warned you about: that you have to keep surrendering it. Every morning it is still true. Every mile you carry the fire again. The obedience did not finish when you decided; it just began, and now it is a long walk, and part of you wonders whether all this trudging even counts, because nothing dramatic is happening. You are just putting one foot in front of the other toward a thing you do not fully understand.

Hear me as a pastor who trusts this metaphor with his life: the walk counts. The walk is the worship. What you are doing on the ordinary, undramatic, third-day road, getting up, carrying it again, speaking the truth about God's faithfulness before you can see it, keeping your feet moving toward the mountain, that is not the delay before the real surrender. That *is* the surrender, stretched out to the size of your actual life so that it can go all the way down into you.

And the provision you cannot see yet is already up ahead. The God who asked you to climb did not send you toward an empty summit. He went up first, and He has put in the thicket, at the exact altitude of your need, the thing you will require when you get there, which you will not find one step sooner and do not need to. Your job is not to see the ram. Your job is to keep walking, and to tell the truth about Him on the way up, and to trust that on the mountain of Yahuah, it will be seen.

Keep climbing. Say it out loud like Abraham did, into the silence, before you have any proof: *we will worship, and we will come back down.* Then take the next mile. The ram is already there.
