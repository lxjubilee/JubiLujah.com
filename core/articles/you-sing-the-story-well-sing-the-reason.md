There is a psalm in the middle of the songbook that looks, at first glance, like a mistake.

Twenty-six lines. And every single one of them ends with the exact same words: *for his mercy endures forever.* Line after line after line, the same phrase, until a modern reader starts to skim, the way you skim a form letter, and wonders why the editor did not trim it down.

But it was never meant to be read down a page by one set of eyes. It was meant to be *sung across a room by two.* And the moment you understand that, this psalm stops looking like a stutter and starts looking like the most beautiful thing in the whole collection.

## The oldest antiphon we have

In the Jewish tradition this psalm has a name. They call it the *Hallel HaGadol*, the Great Hallel, the Great Praise. And *hallel* is the very root that stands underneath the word most of us think we already know: *hallelujah*, praise Yah. So we are standing at the headwaters of the loudest word in worship.

Here is how the tradition understands it was carried, and the shape of the psalm itself all but demands it. One voice sings the deed. The whole assembly answers with the reason.

The leader lifts up the line: *Give thanks to the One who made the heavens.* And the room comes back, every throat in it, *for his mercy endures forever.* The leader again: *who spread the earth upon the waters.* And back it rolls: *for his mercy endures forever.* Who split the sea. *For his mercy endures forever.* Who brought a people through on dry ground. *For his mercy endures forever.* Who remembered us in our low estate. *For his mercy endures forever.*

Do you feel what that is? It is not repetition for its own sake. It is *dialogue.* It is a conversation with a part written for you.

> One voice cannot sing this psalm. It was composed for a soloist who tells the story and a multitude that keeps answering why.

## The word the whole room says back

Look at what the room is given to say, because it is not a throwaway. The phrase the assembly repeats is built on the Hebrew word *chesed*, and *chesed* is not thin. It is Yahuah's steadfast, loyal, covenant love; the kindness that has bound itself by promise and will not let go. When the leader recounts what Yahuah *did*, made, split, struck, delivered, remembered, the people are not answering with applause. They are answering with the *why underneath the what.* He did it because of *chesed.* And that *chesed*, the room insists, twenty-six times, until the truth is worn smooth as a river stone, *endures forever.*

Notice the division of labor, because it is tender and it is deliberate. The leader carries the events. The crowd carries the meaning. No one person is asked to hold both the whole history and the whole reason at once. The story is told by one. The reason is sung by everyone. And the two halves only become a psalm when they meet in the middle of the room.

Some teachers of Israel have long noticed that the refrain lands twenty-six times, and that twenty-six is the number that spells the sacred Name. I hold that gently, as their observation and not as something the text explains of itself. But even held gently, it says something lovely: that the answer the people give is stamped, all the way through, with the Name of the One they are answering.

## Why you need the other half of the room

Here is what this ancient form knows about you that our private, headphone-shaped worship has forgotten.

There are days you can sing the story. You can rehearse what Yahuah has done, the sea he split for you, the low place he found you in, the enemy he brought you out from under. On those days the verses come easily and the deeds are fresh.

But there are other days when you cannot get the *reason* out. When you can recite what he did and still not feel, in your own chest, that his mercy endures *forever*, because your evidence, this week, seems to argue the opposite. On those days you are meant to open your mouth and let the *rest of the room* sing the refrain over you. Their *for his mercy endures forever* becomes the floor you stand on until your own voice comes back. That is not weakness. That is the design. The psalm was built so that no one is ever asked to hold both halves alone.

And it runs the other way, too. On the day your faith in his *chesed* is unshakable, you become the wall of sound that answers over the person three seats down who has gone quiet, the one telling the story with their lips while their heart cannot yet reach the reason. You do not know which of you that is on any given Sunday. You are not supposed to know. You are only supposed to sing your line loud enough that it reaches whoever needs it, and trust that when your line falters, someone will be singing it back to you.

## Harmony is a conversation

We have made worship into a solo we all happen to perform at the same time. Everyone facing forward, everyone privately expressing an inner feeling, the crowd an accident of scheduling. And then we wonder why it can feel so lonely to be surrounded by singing.

The Great Hallel remembers something better. Harmony, at its root, is not many people making the same sound at the same instant. It is *call and answer*, one voice reaching out and another reaching back, a question and its response, a story and its reason, until the reaching itself becomes the music. The beauty is not in the leader's line. It is not in the crowd's refrain. It is in the *turning*, the handoff, the moment the sound crosses the open air between them and becomes a single thing that neither half could make on its own.

That is why the psalm can afford to say the same six words twenty-six times without ever growing stale. Because it is not being *repeated.* It is being *answered.* And an answer, given again and again to a love that has proven itself again and again, is not monotony. It is a marriage.

So the next time you stand in a room full of voices, listen for the shape of it. Somewhere a leader is telling the story. And your part, the part the whole design has been holding open for you, is to sing back the reason, at full volume, whether you feel it that morning or not.

You sing the story. We will sing the reason. And between us, over and over, for as long as there is breath in the room, we will keep insisting the truest thing there is: that his mercy endures forever.

Sing it back. Someone across the room is waiting to hear their own faith returned to them in your voice.
