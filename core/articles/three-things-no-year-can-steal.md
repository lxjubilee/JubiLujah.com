I have sat at a great many bedsides in my life, and I have learned to watch what a person reaches for when everything else has already been taken. Because the years are thieves. They come slow and polite, and they take a little at a time, the eyesight, the hearing, the strength in the hands, the names of the streets you grew up on. And if you live long enough, the years will eventually walk right into the room and take the one thing you swore they never could.

I made **All That Remains** for the hour when the subtraction is nearly finished, and a soul is trying to find out what, if anything, is left. And I built the whole record on the most examined verse the church owns: *"So now faith, hope, and love abide, these three; but the greatest of these is love"* (1 Corinthians 13:13). Abide. That is a word for what stays when everything else has gone home.

## What the years cannot take

The song I want you to sit with is "What the Years Cannot Take," and I will tell you plainly where it comes from, because I do not build songs on air. It follows a woman watching the long disease take her husband, watching him forget her own face across the kitchen table. And the line that breaks me every time I sing it is not a complaint. It is a discovery: *I have watched my husband forget my face in the long disease, but I have watched the Lord still walk into the room with ease.* The years take memory, the years take strength, the years take everything. *But the Lord remains, and the soul remembers what to sing.*

That is doctrine, sung from the far edge of a life. The anchor under it is Hebrews 13:8, *"Jesus Christ is the same yesterday and today and forever."* Everything else in your life is on a moving belt, sliding away a little each year. He is the one fixed point in the room. So when the song says *what the years cannot take is the Name above every name*, it is not sentiment. It is the only sane place left to set your feet when the floor is being pulled up plank by plank.

> The husband forgets, the children move away, the body fails, the doctor has nothing left to say. But the Lord of yesterday, today, forever is the same, and what the years cannot take is the Name above every name.

## Faith and hope will one day be out of a job

Now let me teach, because there is a glory hidden in Paul's little word *abide* that most people rush past.

Faith and hope are magnificent, and this album spends its first songs on them, "Faith Remains," "Hope Remains." Hebrews 11:1 calls faith *"the assurance of things hoped for, the conviction of things not seen,"* and Hebrews 6:19 calls hope *"a sure and steadfast anchor of the soul."* An anchor. A conviction. These are the muscles that carry you through every dark season you will ever cross. But here is the wonder: faith and hope have an expiration date, and it is a happy one. Faith is for what you cannot yet see, and one day you will see. Hope is for what has not yet come, and one day it will come. On that day, faith becomes sight and hope becomes possession, and the two of them, their work finished, quietly retire.

Love does not retire. Love is the one of the three that is already doing on earth exactly what it will do forever in heaven. That is why Paul says the greatest of these is love, not because faith and hope are small, but because love is the only one of the three that never has to stop being what it is. When the album's final song lands on *"All That Remains Is Love,"* it is making a theological claim, not a greeting-card one: at the far end of everything, when faith has become sight and hope has become home, love is still standing in the room, because love was the thing God was the whole time.

## Where the treasure actually is

The middle of the record turns from the losing to the keeping, and I want to be exact about it. "The Treasure Untaken," "Incorruptible Inheritance," "What Lasts Forever", these are not vague comforts. They are **Yeshua's** own economics.

He told us where to put what matters: *"Do not lay up for yourselves treasures on earth, where moth and rust destroy and where thieves break in and steal, but lay up for yourselves treasures in heaven... For where your treasure is, there your heart will be also"* (Matthew 6:19-21). Notice He does not deny that earth's treasures get stolen. He assumes it. Moth, rust, thief, the years, by other names. His counsel is not to grip the perishable tighter but to move your treasure to a vault the thieves cannot reach. Peter names what is stored there: *"an inheritance that is imperishable, undefiled, and unfading, kept in heaven for you"* (1 Peter 1:4). And Paul gives the whole album its sightline: *"the things that are seen are transient, but the things that are unseen are eternal"* (2 Corinthians 4:18). We spend our lives staring at the transient and calling it real. The eternal things have been the real ones all along.

## Who this is for

This album is for the caregiver in the long disease, sitting beside a face that no longer knows hers. It is for the widow who has already buried most of what she loved and wonders what is even left to hold. It is for the aging saint taking inventory of a body that keeps handing things back to the years. And it is, quietly, for the young and strong who have not yet been robbed of anything, because the time to move your treasure is *before* the thieves arrive, not after.

Here is the whole record in one sentence: the years are going to take nearly everything, and they cannot touch the three things that were the point the entire time. Faith will carry you home and then rest. Hope will bring you to the door and then be filled. But love, the love **Yahuah** poured out at the cross, the love He has set in you toward Him and toward every soul at your table, that one goes all the way through, out the far side of the grave, and keeps on going.

So hold what remains. The husband may forget your face. **Yeshua** never has, and He never will. And when the years are finally finished taking, you will find that what is left in your hands was the treasure the entire time, and the greatest of it is love.
