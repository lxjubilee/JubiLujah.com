I want to tell you about the loudest, brightest, most joyful music the ancient world ever made, and about the strange, small thing it was all made *over*.

It was water. A single pitcher of water.

Once a year, during the feast that gathered the whole harvest home, there was a celebration so overwhelming that the old teachers who remembered it said a thing I have never forgotten: that anyone who had not seen this joy had never in their life seen joy at all. Not a poem, a claim from people who were *there*. Whatever it was, it set the bar for gladness so high that everything else looked pale beside it.

Let me tell you what happened, and let me be honest about which parts are Scripture and which parts are the memory of the tradition, because both matter here.

## The all-night music

The feast is the one Scripture calls the feast of *booths*, the week Yahuah told His people to move outside, live in little shelters, and rejoice before Him over the ingathered harvest. The command to *rejoice* is in the text. It is one of the only feasts where joy itself is the instruction.

But the celebration I am describing, the one the old teachers could not stop marveling at, belongs to the memory of the Second Temple. So I want to be careful and say: this all-night ceremony is *tradition*, the remembered practice of the people, not a command written in the Book. Hold it as tradition. But hear what the tradition remembered.

They remembered great lampstands lifted so high that men climbed ladders to fill their bowls, and when the wicks caught, the light poured out over the whole city until, they said, there was not a courtyard that did not glow. They remembered flutes and harps and lyres and cymbals. They remembered the most dignified, most respected elders of the nation, the wise men, the ones who spent their days bent over the sacred scrolls, dancing. Juggling burning torches. Leaping and singing at the top of their lungs while a whole city clapped them through the night, hour after hour, until the eastern sky began to gray.

And at the center of all that light and music was one act, repeated each day: a pitcher of water, drawn and carried up in a great glad procession, and poured out. The tradition gave the whole celebration a name that means, tenderly, *the rejoicing of the house of the water-drawing*.

## Why in the world would anyone dance over water?

Here is what moves me most. Of all the things to throw the greatest party in the ancient world over, not a military victory, not a coronation, not a harvest of gold. They danced over *water*.

But of course they did. Because they were a people of the dry land, and they knew, in their bones, what most of us have forgotten in our plumbing and our ease: water is *life*. In a land where the rains could come or fail, where a dry season could mean death, water was never ordinary. To pour it out in worship was to say, with your whole body, *every drop of life we have comes from Yahuah, and we are so glad, and we are asking Him for more.*

So the loudest joy the ancient world ever made was, underneath, a thirst. A whole people singing and dancing out a longing, *give us water, give us life, we cannot make it ourselves*, and rejoicing in advance over the God who gives it.

> The most exuberant celebration ever remembered was, at its heart, a nation admitting it was thirsty and trusting Someone to satisfy it.

## The voice that rose above the music

Now set one particular year inside all of that light and noise.

There was a day the tradition marked as the great and climactic day of the feast, the culmination of all that water-pouring and dancing. Picture the crowd, exhausted and radiant, the torches, the flutes, the water carried up one more time. And in the middle of it, the Gospel of John tells us, a man stood up and *cried out*. He raised His voice over the whole celebration so it could be heard above the music.

Yeshua stood up in the middle of the feast of water and shouted: *If anyone thirsts, let him come to Me and drink.* And He went on, whoever believes in Him, out of that person's innermost being would flow rivers of living water.

Do you feel what He did? He waited until the whole city was singing and dancing out its thirst, until the longing under all that joy was at its very loudest, and then He stood up and said, in effect: *the thing you are all dancing for, the living water your whole heart is aching after. It is standing here. Come to Me. Drink.*

He did not scold the joy. He did not stand outside the party. He stepped into the middle of the loudest gladness His people had, and He revealed that He was the fountain it had been dancing toward the whole time. All those torches, all those years, all that water poured out, a people rejoicing over a promise, and the promise had a face now, and it was raising its voice over the flutes to say *come*.

## The joy that has an inside to it

I have spent my life around music, and I have learned there are two kinds of loud.

There is a loud that is trying to cover something, a noise you make so you don't have to feel the emptiness underneath. And there is a loud that is *overflowing*, a joy so full it cannot stay quiet, a gladness with something real on the inside of it. The water-drawing was the second kind. And Yeshua's invitation is the key to why.

Because He did not offer a cup to drink and be thirsty again in an hour. He offered a spring, living water that would rise up *inside* a person and keep flowing. That is the difference between a joy you have to keep refilling from outside and a joy that has its own source within you. The whole feast poured water *out* over the altar. Yeshua promised water that flows *out* of you, the party moving from something you attend to something you *become*.

## Come and drink

So here is my invitation, which is only His.

If you are thirsty, and I think everyone reading this knows exactly what I mean, the dryness underneath even our happiest days. You do not have to manufacture joy to be allowed near the fountain. You just have to be thirsty, and come. That is the whole condition. *If anyone thirsts.*

The loudest, brightest joy the ancient world ever knew was a people dancing out their thirst under torchlight, trusting God for water. And the God they were dancing toward stood up in the middle of it and told them the fountain had come, and its name was Yeshua, and it was open.

Come to Him and drink. And do not be surprised if the joy that rises in you is loud, the overflowing kind, with a spring on the inside, because that is exactly the kind He pours. Rivers, He said. Not a sip. Rivers.
