I want to introduce you to a woman most people read right past, because she gets only three sentences in the whole Bible, and because by the time we meet her she is very old and there is nothing left about her that looks like a headline.

Her name is Anna. Luke tells us she was a prophetess, the daughter of Phanuel, from the tribe of Asher, and if you know your history, that tribe alone is a quiet heartbreak. Asher was one of the northern tribes, the scattered ones, the ones history had swept off into other nations generations before. For a daughter of Asher to be standing in the temple in Jerusalem at all is already a small miracle of a faith that refused to die when the map said it should have.

She married young. And after seven years, her husband died. We are not told how. We are only told the arithmetic of the decades that followed: she lived on as a widow into deep old age, and Luke says she did not depart from the temple, worshiping night and day with fasting and prayer. Some read the text to mean she was eighty-four years old; some read it to mean she had been a widow for eighty-four years. I do not know which, and I will not pretend to. But either way you land, you are looking at a woman who took an early grief and turned it into a lifetime of showing up in the house of Yahuah to worship a God who, for years and years and years, did not visibly do the one thing she was waiting for Him to do.

She was waiting for the redemption of Jerusalem. She was waiting for the consolation of Israel. She was waiting, in plainer words, for the promise, the Anointed One her whole nation had been aching after for centuries. And she waited by worshiping. Every dawn. Every dusk. Through decades in which nothing arrived.

## The vigil no one applauds

I need to sit here with you, because this is where most of us actually live, and it is the part of the story nobody paints.

We love the moment she finally sees Him. But look at what came first, an ocean of ordinary mornings in which she got up, went to the temple, and worshiped over an empty promise. No angel visited her each day to keep her going. No crowd gathered to admire the faithful old widow at prayer. There was, as far as we can tell, no evidence at all that her vigil was accomplishing anything. Just her, the incense, the same stone courts, the same held hope, the same silence where the answer was supposed to be.

That is not a dramatic faith. That is a durable one. And I have come to believe the durable kind is rarer and costs more.

Because anyone can worship on the morning the answer comes. What Anna did, what she did for the better part of a human life, was worship on all the mornings it did not. She kept a post that no one was checking. She sang, if you will let me call her prayers a song, into a room that gave her nothing back, on nothing but the character of the God she was singing to. Year after year after year, she agreed out loud with a promise she had never once seen kept.

Some of you know exactly what that costs. You have been keeping a post like that. A prayer you have prayed so long you have stopped expecting the phone to ring. A hope for a child, a healing, a homecoming, a nation, that you have carried past the point where anyone around you still expects it. And somewhere in the long middle, a voice started telling you that the silence was a verdict, that if it were ever going to come, it would have come by now, and your faithfulness has just been a slow way of talking to a wall.

Hear me, from a prophet who has kept some long posts of her own: the vigil is not the wall talking back. The vigil is worship. Yahuah was not less pleased with Anna on year forty than He was on the morning she finally held the answer. Her worship was complete the whole time. The seeing was a gift; it was never the grade.

## The morning it walked in

And then, one day, it came. Not with trumpets. Not with an army. It came in the arms of a tired young mother and a bewildered carpenter, a poor couple bringing an eight-day-old baby to be presented, indistinguishable from a hundred other poor couples who came through that gate every week.

Luke is very precise about the timing. He says that at that very hour Anna came up. That very hour. As the old man Simeon was still holding the child and blessing Him, Anna arrived, and she saw, and she knew, and she began to give thanks to Yahuah and to speak of the child to everyone who was waiting for the redemption of Jerusalem.

Do you feel the knife-edge of that? *At that very hour.* If she had not been there, if, on that particular unremarkable morning, she had finally decided she was too old and too tired and too disappointed to make the walk one more time. She would have missed by minutes the thing she had given her life to. She did not miss it because she was always there. The faithfulness of the long dark is what put her in the room when the light walked in.

And notice what the seeing turned her into. She had been, for decades, a woman of the quiet vigil, fasting, praying, worshiping mostly unseen. The moment she saw the promise, she became an evangelist. She could not hold it in. She went and told everyone still waiting. The long silent worship did not make her small and bitter. It made her, at the very end, the first woman in the Gospel to go out and announce the Redeemer to a waiting people.

## The honest word, and the invitation

Now I owe you the truth, and I will not soften it, because a prophet who only tells the sweet half is not worth listening to on the hard mornings.

Anna saw the promise before she died. Many of the faithful do not. The great cloud of witnesses is full of people who kept their post for a lifetime and died still waiting, having only greeted the promise from a distance and welcomed it from afar. I will not stand here and guarantee you that your particular ache resolves inside your particular lifetime, because that is not a thing any honest servant of Yahuah can promise, and the moment I turn your worship into a lever that forces the answer, I have lied to every believer whose answer never came on this side.

So let me tell you what I *can* promise, because it is the thing Anna's whole life is shouting. Your vigil is not wasted, whether or not you are the one who gets to hold the answer. The worship was the point, not the transaction. And the ones who keep their post are the ones standing in the room when the light finally comes, sometimes to hold it themselves, and sometimes to hand it, still warm, to the generation coming up behind them who will.

So go back to your post today. Worship in the empty court one more morning. Keep the vigil over the promise that has gone long. You do not know the hour, and neither did she. She only knew Whom she was waiting for, and she decided He was worth a lifetime of dawns.

Be there when the light walks in. It has a way of coming through the gate on the most ordinary morning of all.
