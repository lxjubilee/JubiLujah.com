Before the voice from the fire told Moses a single thing about his future, it told him something about his feet.

He had gone up the back of the mountain that day as a shepherd, not a prophet, a runaway with a price on his head, tending someone else's sheep at the far edge of the wilderness, his great deeds long behind him and his failures more recent. And then he saw it: a bush on fire that would not burn up. The flames were real, but the branches stayed whole, and Moses did what any curious man would do. He turned aside to look. He walked toward the strangest thing he had ever seen, closing the distance, until a voice came out of the fire and stopped him cold before he could take one more step. Do not come any closer, it said. Take the sandals off your feet, because the place where you are standing is holy ground.

Not: I have a mission for you. Not: I have seen the misery of My people. All of that comes, the sending, the burden, the great commission that will split a sea and free a nation. But not yet. First the feet. First the threshold. Before Yahuah will entrust this man with the largest assignment in the book, He addresses the way he is standing on the ground, and He does it by having him take something off.

I have come to believe that command is the most overlooked instruction in the whole scene, and that it is aimed straight at the way we worship now.

Because we do not take anything off. We arrive at the presence of God the way we arrive everywhere else, shod, hurried, still wearing the whole day. We carry our opinions in with us, our distraction, our low-grade familiarity, the sandals of a life that has walked across a thousand ordinary surfaces and no longer notices when one of them is different. We stride right up to the fire the way Moses almost did, curious, casual, ready to get a good look. And we start singing with our shoes on. We have never learned that there is a threshold at all, that the ground where Yahuah is actually present is not the same ground as the parking lot, and that you do not cross from the one to the other still wearing what you wore on the way in.

The sandals are the point, so let me stay on them. In that world, your sandals were what stood between the sole of your foot and the earth, the layer of ordinary protection, the thing that let you walk over anything without feeling it. To take them off was to make yourself defenseless and unhurried at once. It was to feel the ground directly, to be unable to just breeze across it, to acknowledge with your own bare skin that this place is not like the others. That is why Yahuah asked for the feet before He asked for anything else. He was not being fussy about footwear. He was teaching a runaway shepherd the first thing a worshipper ever has to learn: that you cannot approach the holy the same way you approach everything else, and that reverence begins not with what you say but with what you are willing to remove.

And notice what Moses does the moment he understands where he is standing. He hides his face, because he is afraid to look at Elohim. There it is again, the same instinct the burning ones show around the throne, the same undoing the prophet felt in the temple. This is not the terror of a slave before a tyrant. It is the accurate response of a small creature who has just realized Who is speaking. And far from disqualifying him, it is the very thing that qualifies him. The man who hides his face at the bush is the man Yahuah trusts with the mountain. The reverence is the credential.

We have been taught, I think, to be a little embarrassed by that. We prize confidence in worship, coming boldly, singing loudly, feeling free. And there is a true and glorious boldness that the blood of Yeshua has purchased for us; we really are invited to come near, and I would never rob you of that nearness. But somewhere we let boldness curdle into casualness, and they are not the same thing. Boldness takes the sandals off and then draws close, trembling and unhindered at once. Casualness never noticed there was anything to take off. One of them is the freedom of a child running to a Father he reveres. The other is the numbness of a tourist who wandered onto holy ground and mistook it for a scenic overlook.

The wise ones have always said the fear of Yahuah is the beginning of wisdom, the beginning, the doorway, the first thing, the ground floor of the whole house. Not the fear that runs away, but the fear that takes off its sandals; the awe that finally tells the truth about the difference between Him and us and is set free by the telling. Everything real that Moses became started at that threshold. And I would say the same of everything real your worship will ever become. It does not start with the first note. It starts a moment before the first note, in the willingness to stop, to acknowledge the ground has changed, and to take something off.

So what are your sandals? Only you can name them, but you know. It is the thing you keep on when you come to sing, the hurry you never set down, the phone you never quite put away, the running commentary in your head that critiques the room instead of meeting the One in it, the familiarity so worn-in that His name has stopped meaning anything more than a lyric. Whatever it is that lets you walk right up to the fire without feeling the ground go different under your feet, that is what He is asking you to take off. Not because He is hard to approach. Because He is holy, and you were made to know the difference, and the knowing is the beginning of everything He wants to give you.

Here is the hope buried in the command, and it is the whole reason I am telling you this. The bush was on fire, and it was not consumed. That is what holiness turned out to be, not a fire that destroys the one who nears it, but a fire that burns and burns and does not use anything up, a presence you can stand barefoot in front of and live. Yahuah did not tell Moses to take off his sandals to keep him out. He told him to take them off so he could come in, close enough to be sent, close enough to be changed, close enough that the runaway walked away a deliverer.

So before you sing tonight, do the thing the fire asked first. Stop at the threshold. Feel the ground go holy under you. And take the sandals off, whatever they are, so that you can finally, barefoot and unhurried and undone, come close enough to the fire that does not consume you to be set entirely ablaze.
