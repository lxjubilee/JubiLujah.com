I want to hand you a detail from the very first Passover that most people read straight past, because on the surface it looks like a scheduling note. But it is not a scheduling note. It is a prophecy hiding in a household chore.

The instruction was this: on the tenth day of the month, take a lamb. One for a household, or share with your neighbor if your house is small. So far, so ordinary. But then comes the line that should stop you. They took the lamb on the *tenth*, and they did not slaughter it until the *fourteenth.*

Four days. Four days with a living lamb in the house before it was ever brought to the knife.

## The four days no one explains

Ask yourself the obvious question, the one the text seems to dare you to ask: why the wait? If the lamb is going to be killed on the fourteenth, why bring it home on the tenth? Why not take it the same afternoon you need it, the way you would grab anything else for a meal?

Because the lamb was not just going to be *eaten.* It was going to be *examined.*

The command was that the lamb be without blemish, *tamim* is the Hebrew word, meaning whole, sound, complete, without defect. And you do not certify something as flawless with a glance. You live with it. You watch it walk. You run your hands over it looking for the torn ear, the limp, the sickness under the wool, the thing that would make it unfit for the altar. For four days that lamb was under the most personal inspection imaginable, brought inside, kept close, scrutinized by the very family whose lives would depend on it being what it was supposed to be.

And here is the tenderness buried in the terror of that night: by the fourth day, that lamb was no longer livestock. Four days in the house with the children means the children named it. Four days means you knew its face. The cost of your deliverance had become something you loved before it was ever laid down. Passover was never designed to be cheap. It was designed to break your heart a little, so you would understand.

## Someone else was inspected for four days

Now let me walk you into Jerusalem, into the last week of Yeshua's life, and I want you to watch the calendar, because the ancient reckoning many keep says He entered the city on the very day the lambs were being selected, the tenth.

He comes riding in, and the crowds do exactly what you do with a Passover lamb: they *bring Him into the house.* Into the city. They line the road, they cry out for salvation, they usher Him toward the Temple as though the whole nation were setting Him apart. And then, for the days that follow, He is not left alone for a moment. He is *inspected.*

Read those final days and count the examiners. The chief priests come at Him. The Pharisees come, testing. The Sadducees come with their trap about the resurrection. The Herodians come with the coin and the question about taxes, hoping to catch Him in a word. The scribes, the lawyers, the elders, one delegation after another, day after day, running their hands over Him, looking for the flaw. Looking for the torn place. Looking for the one defect that would let them disqualify Him.

They were doing to the Lamb of Yahuah exactly what Israel did to the lamb in the house. They were searching for a blemish.

> They questioned Him for days and found nothing. Not because they weren't looking. They were looking with everything they had. But you cannot find a stain the Lamb does not have.

## The verdict of His own examiners

Here is what undoes me. The blemish is not declared absent by His friends. It is declared absent by the people trying hardest to find one.

His accusers drag Him to trial and cannot get their false witnesses to agree. Pilate examines Him and says out loud, more than once, that he finds no fault in Him, no basis for a charge. Herod sends Him back with nothing. Even the man hanging on the cross beside Him says it plainly: this man has done nothing wrong. The Roman soldier at the foot of the cross looks up and calls Him righteous. Every court that convened to condemn Him ended up, against its own intent, certifying Him. *Tamim.* Without blemish. The inspection that was meant to destroy Him instead became the official record that He was spotless.

That is not an accident of history. That is the pattern of the tenth day coming true in front of witnesses. The Lamb had to be examined *publicly,* by the *authorities,* over *days,* so that no one could ever say He was a defective sacrifice smuggled quietly to the altar. Heaven wanted the flawlessness on the record. So heaven let them look as long as they wanted.

## For the one being examined right now

I know why this reaches some of you today, and it is not because you love calendars. It is because you know what it is to be inspected. You know what it is to have people circle you looking for the flaw, the coworker collecting your mistakes, the family member reciting your worst season, the accuser in your own head running its hands over your life at three in the morning, certain that if it just keeps looking it will find the thing that disqualifies you.

Hear me, beloved. You do not stand before that inspection on the strength of your own record. You would not survive it, and neither would I. We are not the spotless lamb. We are the household that needed one.

But that is the whole gospel of the tenth day. A Lamb was examined *in your place,* by the harshest judges history could assemble, and He came through their four days without a single blemish found, and then He was slain, and His flawlessness was credited to the marked house. When the accuser circles you now, you are not defending your own perfection. You are pointing to His. Let him look. Let him examine. The verdict is already in, signed by the very courts that wanted you condemned: no fault found in the Lamb, and you are hidden under His blood.

So when they come to inspect you, and they will, do not scramble to prove you have no torn places. Point to the One who had none. He was watched for four days so that you could be welcomed home for good. That is a deliverance worth loving before it was ever laid down.
