Watch your own foot sometime when a good rhythm comes on.

You did not decide to tap it. You will catch it already going, already keeping time with something outside you, a beat it locked onto without consulting you at all. And if you try to stop it, you can, for a few seconds, by main force. Then your attention drifts, and there it is again, tapping, pulled back into step by a pulse it simply could not leave alone.

There is something almost comic about it, and something profound. Because this small helpless thing your foot does is one of the stranger facts about human beings, and once you see how deep it runs, it stops being small.

We fall into rhythm. Not just as a preference, as a reflex, a pull we are built to obey. Put a group of people in a room with a steady beat, and their bodies begin, without instruction, to organize themselves around it. It is why a crowd's scattered clapping, after a great performance, so often collapses on its own into a single unified pulse that no one is leading, thousands of hands that were each keeping their own time, drifting, drifting, and then suddenly *together,* one beat, as if a conductor had walked out, when in fact the only thing that happened is that human beings cannot easily stand near a rhythm and keep their own. We reach for the shared pulse. We fall into step.

I love this so much I could talk about it all day, but let me give you the fact that made me actually sit down.

## The rhythm that could shake a bridge

The pull to fall into step is so strong, and so physical, that armies have to be *ordered out of it.*

For a long time, soldiers crossing a bridge have been commanded to break step, to stop marching in unison and walk in a deliberate, ragged, out-of-time shuffle. Not for show. Because a column of people all striking the ground on the very same beat can set a whole bridge swaying in sympathy with their rhythm, and the swaying can build, and build, until the structure is in danger. The unified human pulse is powerful enough to move something enormous. So the command goes down the line: *break step.* Scatter the rhythm. And soldiers, who have trained their whole bodies to fall into the shared beat, have to consciously fight the very thing they were drilled into, because the pull toward the common rhythm does not politely switch off.

Think about what that means. We are not neutral timekeepers, each of us privately ticking along at our own rate, occasionally choosing to coordinate. We are built to *entrain*, to lock onto a rhythm outside ourselves and be carried by it, so readily that it takes discipline to resist. A pulse outside you wants to become the pulse inside you. Your body is on its side.

And the instant I understood that, a whole line of Scripture I had read a hundred times lit up from underneath.

## Keep in step

The apostle who wrote to the churches in Galatia was not, as far as I know, thinking about marching bands. But he reached for exactly this image, and I do not think it was an accident. Live by the Spirit, he wrote, and then, *keep in step with the Spirit.* Keep in step. Match your stride to a rhythm that is not your own. Let the pace of the Spirit of God become the pace of your body and your days, the way your foot lets a song become its beat.

Once you feel the weight of how *physical* that word is, it stops being a vague spiritual instruction and becomes something almost tender. He is not asking you to invent a holy rhythm out of your own effort, gritting your way into godliness by private willpower. He is telling you that there is already a pulse, steady, external, going, and that you were made, in the very grain of you, to fall into step with it. The same reflex that catches your foot before you decide, the same pull that folds a scattered crowd into one clap, that is the faculty you were given so that a life could be carried along by a rhythm larger than itself. You do not have to generate the beat. You have to *stop keeping your own* long enough to feel His, and let the built-in pull do what it already wants to do.

The prophet Amos asked it as a question that only sounds simple: *can two walk together, unless they have agreed to meet?* You cannot keep step with someone whose rhythm you have never fallen into. Walking together is not two people privately marching in the same direction. It is two rhythms become one. And the whole ache of a distracted, exhausting faith is the ache of trying to walk *with* God while insisting on keeping your own tempo, hurrying when He is unhurried, stalling when He is moving, always a half-beat off, always tired, because nothing wears a body out like fighting the pull to fall into step.

## Of one accord

And here is why it was never meant to be only *you* and the rhythm.

There is a moment in the early days of the church that the old account describes with a phrase worth slowing down over. Before the great thing happened, before the wind and the fire and the sudden speech, the whole gathered company was, it says, *of one accord.* One accord. The word underneath it carries the sense of many becoming a single motion, many rhythms fallen into one. They were not merely in the same room. They had entrained. They had become, for a while, a single pulse, and it was into *that* unified rhythm, not into a room of soloists each keeping private time, that the Spirit came like a rush of wind.

I think that is how it usually goes. We were not built to keep step with God entirely alone, any more than a single clapping pair of hands can pull a crowd into unison by itself. Something happens when a body of people fall into the same rhythm together, the beat gets *strong,* the way a scattered crowd's clap gets strong once it converges, strong enough to carry the ones who were drifting, strong enough, like an army in step, to move things that no single person could budge. This is not a threat, the way the bridge is a threat. It is the promise. A people who have truly fallen into step together generate a pulse that can hold up the weak and shake loose what needed shaking.

## Stop keeping your own time

So here is what I would leave with you, especially if you are worn out from a faith that has felt like effort.

You may be exhausted because you have been trying to *set* the rhythm, to manufacture, by your own private willpower, a holiness and a peace and a pace that were never yours to generate. Stop, for a moment, and notice: there is already a pulse going. Steady. Patient. Not frantic like your calendar, not stalled like your discouragement, a rhythm of grace that has been keeping perfect time the whole while you were fighting to keep your own. You do not have to invent it. You have to fall into it. And the very thing that catches your foot before you decide, the pull you could not resist if you tried, is the faculty God gave you precisely so that a whole life could be carried by a beat larger than itself.

Let your own tempo go for a little while. Get near enough to His that the built-in pull can take over. And if you can, do it near other people who are trying to do the same, because the beat gets strong when we fall into it together, and a body in one accord can carry you through stretches you could never have marched alone.

Your foot already knows how to do this. It has been showing you all your life. There is a rhythm you did not set, going right now, and you were made, down to the reflex, to fall into step.
