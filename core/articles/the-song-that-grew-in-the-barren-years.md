There is a woman at the front of the book of Samuel who prays so hard she cannot make a sound, and I have loved her my whole life, because I have prayed that prayer.

Her name is Hannah, and her grief has a specific, grinding shape to it. She is barren in a world that measured a woman's worth by her children, and she shares her husband with another wife, Peninnah, who has children in abundance and who uses them like knives. Year after year the family goes up to worship at Shiloh, and year after year Peninnah provokes her, needles her, reminds her of the empty place where a child should be, until Hannah weeps and cannot eat. Her husband loves her and does not understand her, *am I not more to you than ten sons?*, which is a kind, useless thing to say to a woman whose ache he will never carry in his own body.

And one year, at the temple, something in her breaks open. She gets up and goes to the house of Yahuah alone, and she pours it all out. But here is the detail I cannot get past, the detail that makes her mine: she prayed in her heart. Only her lips were moving. Her voice was not heard.

The grief was too big and too raw for volume. It came out as a silent, moving mouth and a face wet with tears, so much so that old Eli the priest, watching from his seat, assumed she was drunk and told her to put away her wine. Imagine that. She reaches the lowest, most naked moment of her whole life of prayer, and the one religious man in the room mistakes her anguish for a bender.

She answers him with a dignity that still steadies me: *No, my lord. I am a woman troubled in spirit. I have drunk nothing. I have been pouring out my soul before Yahuah.* And Eli, to his credit, hears it, and blesses her, and she goes away, and Scripture says her face was no longer sad. Nothing had changed yet. The child had not come. But something in the pouring-out had set her down.

## The prayer no one could hear

I want to stop right there, in the silence, before we rush to the happy part, because the silent prayer is the part of this story most of us are actually living inside.

We have a picture of powerful prayer as loud, articulate, confident, the kind you could put on a platform. Hannah's most powerful prayer was none of those things. It was voiceless. It was a moving mouth over an ache she could not even form into clean words. It looked, to the professional standing nearby, like a woman falling apart. And it was heard in heaven more clearly than any polished thing said in that temple all year.

If your prayer life right now is mostly a moving mouth and no sound, if the thing you carry is too heavy to say in whole sentences, and you have started to suspect that your inarticulate, tear-blurred, barely-a-prayer is beneath the notice of God. I want you to look hard at Hannah and let it land. The prayer that could not make a sound is the one Scripture chose to preserve forever. Yahuah was not put off by the mess of it. He leaned in.

Because barren years do something to worship, and it is not what you would expect. You would think the empty season would kill the song. Sometimes, honestly, it does go quiet for a while. But in a heart that keeps turning toward Yahuah in the ache, the barren years are not sterile at all. They are gestating something. Down in the silent, wordless place where Hannah stood with her lips moving, a song was forming that a satisfied woman could never have written.

## What grew where nothing else would

And it did come. Yahuah remembered her, the old text says, in that tender way it uses. He remembered her, and she conceived, and bore a son, and named him Samuel, which sounds like *asked of Yahuah*, because she had asked, and asked, and asked. And in an act of staggering faith she gave the long-awaited child back, carried him to Shiloh as a small boy and left him there to serve in the house of God, keeping only the promise she had made in the silence.

And then Hannah, who could not make a sound in her grief, opens her mouth and sings.

The second chapter of her book is her song, and it is not a lullaby. It is a thunderclap. *My heart exults in Yahuah,* she sings. There is none holy like Him. The bows of the mighty are shattered, and the feeble are armed with strength. The full have hired themselves out for bread, and the hungry are hungry no more. The barren has borne seven, and the mother of many is forlorn. He brings low and He lifts up; He raises the poor from the dust and seats them with princes. It is a revolution set to melody, sung by a woman who had lived every reversal in it from the underside.

Do you hear what happened? The song that could not find a voice in the barren years came out, in full strength, on the far side of them, and it was bigger and fiercer and more prophetic than any song she could have sung if she had never been empty at all. The silence was not the death of her music. It was the soil of it.

## The melody a young girl borrowed

Now here is the part that undoes me every time, the part that makes me sure Yahuah wastes nothing.

A thousand years later, more or less, another woman finds herself carrying an impossible promise, a girl in a nothing town called Nazareth, pregnant before her wedding with a child she was told would be the Son of the Most High. And when she opens her mouth to worship, listen to what comes out. *My soul magnifies the Lord.* He has scattered the proud. He has brought down the mighty from their thrones and lifted up the lowly. He has filled the hungry with good things and sent the rich away empty.

If that sounds familiar, it is because it is Hannah's song. Mary reaches, in the most important moment of her life, for the melody a barren woman composed in her anguish centuries before. Hannah did not know, in her silent grief at Shiloh, that she was writing a song the mother of the Messiah would one day sing. The worship that grew in her barren years crossed a thousand years and landed in the mouth of a young girl who needed exactly those words and did not have to invent them, because a heartbroken woman had already paid for them.

That is what a faithful song does. It outlives the grief that made it. It gets handed down the generations to the next person standing in the same kind of dark, so that when they get there, a way to worship is already waiting in their mouth.

## The honest word, and the invitation

Let me be truthful before I close, because Hannah's story ends with a child and not everyone's does, and I refuse to preach you a formula dressed up as faith.

Yahuah gave Hannah a son. He does not always. There are women reading this who have poured out the same silent prayer for years and are still holding the empty place, and I will not insult your faith by telling you that if you had just believed a little harder the cradle would be full. That is not the God I serve, and it is not the lesson of this story. The point of Hannah is not that worship is a coin you feed into heaven until it dispenses the thing you asked for.

The point is that the barren years are not wasted years. The song that grows in the silence is real even before, even *if*, the circumstance ever changes. Your wordless, tear-blurred, barely-a-prayer is being heard right now, exactly as it is. And the worship you are forging in the empty season may be the very melody some daughter or stranger, generations from now, will reach for in their own impossible night, and be glad someone already wrote it down.

So keep your lips moving, even when no sound will come. Pour out your soul. Yahuah is not standing at a distance calling it drunkenness. He is leaning in, remembering, and composing, in the barren ground, a song that will outlast your grief.

Open your mouth when you can. What comes out will be stronger than you think.
