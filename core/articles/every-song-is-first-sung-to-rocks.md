Every song you have ever loved was first sung to rocks.

I mean that almost literally. Long before a worship song reaches a room full of raised hands, it is sung by one person, badly, into the air of an empty kitchen or a parked car or a trail switchback where the only listeners are stone and wind. The version the crowd hears is the polished descendant of a hundred private, unwitnessed rehearsals sung to nobody. And I have come to believe that the private ones, the ones with no audience, are not the practice for the real thing. They *are* the real thing. The crowd just gets to visit it later.

We have this backwards, and it costs us.

We assume the wilderness is the waiting room. The dry stretch you endure until the door opens and the calling begins. Obscurity as the long, unfortunate hallway before the platform. So we spend our desert seasons the way you spend a layover, restless, checking the clock, treating the whole thing as time to be gotten through rather than a place to be shaped.

But look at how Heaven actually spends people, and you will notice a pattern so consistent it cannot be an accident. Almost everyone Yahuah has ever used at scale, He first sent somewhere with no scale at all.

## The pattern nobody would design

Moses did not step from the palace to the deliverance. He stepped from the palace to forty years of somebody else's sheep on the back side of the desert, until the man who once acted on impulse had been slowed down enough to notice a bush that would not stop burning. Forty years. To lead a people out of Egypt, God apparently first needed a man who had learned to pay attention to something small in a place where nothing happened.

John, the one sent to prepare the way, grew up in the wilderness and became a voice crying out from it, out where there were no synagogues to preach in and no crowds to build. His whole ministry was formed by wide silence before it was ever heard by a single pilgrim.

And Yeshua Himself, this is the one that stops me, before the miracles, before a word of public teaching, the Ruach drove Him out into the wilderness for forty days. Hungry, alone, tested, with no ministry to point to and no one watching but the accuser and the wild animals and the angels. The most important life ever lived began with more than a month in the emptiest place available, singing, we might say, to rocks. And later, once the crowds did come and would not leave Him alone, the Gospels keep noting the same quiet habit: He would slip away, before dawn, to a desolate place, to be with His Father where no one could see. The withdrawing was not a break from the work. It was the source of it.

If the Son of God formed His public life in private wilderness, what exactly made us think we could skip it?

## What the empty room does to a voice

Here is what solitude does, and why it cannot be replaced.

When you sing to a crowd, part of you is always managing the crowd. You are reading the room, feeling for the swell, adjusting. Even the most sincere among us cannot fully switch off the instinct to be received well. It is not hypocrisy; it is just what an audience does to a human. But it means that a song sung only to people is never entirely honest, because it is always, a little, a performance.

Strip the audience away, put a person alone on a windy ridge with no one to receive it, and something in the voice changes. There is no one to impress and no one to reassure, so the tune stops decorating and starts confessing. You find out what you actually believe when there is no one around to believe it *at*. The desert is the only place a song gets audited. It removes every reason to sing except the truest one, that the God you are singing to is worth singing to even when it advances nothing, wins nothing, and no one will ever know you did it.

That is the worship the wilderness is manufacturing in you. Not a better voice. A truer one. A voice that means the words when the room is empty, which is the only kind of voice that can be trusted when the room is full.

And here is the mercy hidden in it: a song proven in solitude cannot be taken from you by a crowd's absence. If your worship only exists when it is witnessed, then loneliness will silence you, and life will hand you plenty of loneliness. But a song you learned to sing to rocks. You can sing that anywhere. In the hospital corridor at 3 a.m. In the season when the calling seems to have evaporated. On the far side of a loss no one else fully understands. You already know how to worship with no one listening. You practiced it for years, out where you thought you were just waiting.

## To the one who feels forgotten in the dry place

So let me pastor whoever is reading this from an obscurity that feels like being overlooked.

You are watching others get the room, the platform, the moment, and you are still out here with the sheep and the stones, and you have started to wonder whether Yahuah has simply lost your address. I want to tell you what I have found true on my own quiet mountain: the wilderness is not evidence that He has forgotten you. It is evidence that He is not in a hurry with you, and that is a very different thing. He is doing the slow, unglamorous work of making sure that when your voice finally carries, there will be something underneath it worth carrying.

Do not waste the desert wishing it were the platform. Sing here. Sing to the rocks and the wind and the God who is the only audience that was ever ultimately real anyway. Let the empty room strip your song down to the thing that is actually true. Because the day the crowd does come, and it may, or it may not, and either way this holds. You will not be discovering your worship in front of them. You will simply be letting them overhear a song you have already, quietly, over long unwitnessed years, learned to mean.

The rocks were never the wrong audience. They were the first one, and the honest one, and in a sense they were only ever standing in for Him.
