Everyone kept telling me to invent myself, and I nearly wore myself out trying.

Build your brand. Design your identity. Become whoever you decide to be. It sounds like freedom, and for a while I chased it like freedom, a goth phase at fifteen, a preppy phase at nineteen, one costume after another, each one promising this would finally be the real me. But inventing a self from scratch is exhausting, because deep down you know you're the one holding the paintbrush, and a person you made up can be unmade just as fast. There's no floor under it.

Then one night I opened my eighth-grade yearbook to the girl in the polo shirt with the slight smile and the eyes that already knew the score. And it stopped me cold, because she was already me. Long before I tried to invent who I thought I should be, she was there, the same girl under every costume I would ever try on. I wrote the whole album out of that recognition. The title track's bridge is the thing I most needed to say out loud: **I didn't make me. I uncovered who was here all along.**

## What "Become Yourself" Actually Means

I know how the phrase sounds. "Become yourself" is stitched on pillows and posted over sunsets, and it usually means *build the person you want to be.* I mean almost the opposite.

Becoming, for me, is not invention. It is discovery. It is the archaeology of a self you did not design and could not have designed, patiently uncovered over years like something already buried in the ground and waiting. The costumes were never the becoming. They were just hair. The becoming was the moment each costume came off and the same girl was still standing there, the one who was always me. *You were always you, even when you didn't know. The costume didn't make you, the era didn't shape you.* That is a far more restful thing to believe than "you are your own invention," because it means the pressure was never on me to author myself. Somebody already did.

> I am not a self I invented. I am a self I was given, and slowly, gratefully, uncovered.

## The Album Is the Long Way Home

I built the record as a journey that keeps taking the scenic route, because that's how the uncovering actually happened.

"The Long Way Home" is about the phone saying "re-routing" for the third time, the detour through a town I never planned to see, the stranger at the gas station with a kind face and a free hour who gave me a piece of advice I still hold. It reframes every wrong turn I ever grieved: *the detour wasn't a wrong turn. It was a longer love.* "Loud Quiet Girl" is for the kid who ate lunch in the corner with a book, whose quiet everyone mistook for shyness, *the quiet was a room where I grew, the loud is the harvest of what I knew.*

There's "Mentor in the Margins," about the person who wrote pencil notes in the edges of my life, the quiet hand that shaped me from the sidelines without ever needing the credit. "The Practice Room," about the five thousand wrong notes it takes before the right one, because nobody uncovers themselves without a lot of ugly repetitions, and the uncovering is slow, patient, unglamorous work. "First Person Who Saw Me," for the one who recognized the real girl before I could. And "Outgrew the Costume," where I finally got to turn around and thank the past self I used to be ashamed of. The whole back half is one long homecoming to a girl who, it turns out, was never lost. She was *loved* the whole time. One of the songs is literally called "I Wasn't Lost (I Was Loved)."

## The Verse Under the Yearbook Photo

Here is the Scripture the whole record is quietly built on, and it's the reason I reframed the entire album away from self-invention.

Long before the culture told me to design myself, Scripture said something my whole soul had been aching to hear: that I was knit together, deliberately, in a place no camera could reach, fearfully and wonderfully made, and known before I was formed. That is the difference between *made* and *made-up.* A made-up self is fiction you're responsible for maintaining. A *made* self is a work of authorship you get to discover. The eighth-grade girl in the polo shirt was not a rough draft I improved on. She was already the handiwork of Someone who knew exactly what He was doing.

And that's why the album refuses the flattery of "you created yourself." The capstone anchors it, a song called "Made, Not Made-Up." We are, Scripture says plainly, His workmanship, crafted on purpose, for good works that were prepared ahead of time, which means the meaning of your life was written before you got here and is waiting to be uncovered, not manufactured. **Yahuah** is the Author. I am the discovery. The most freeing sentence I know about my own identity is that I am not my own design, and I did not make me. **Yeshua** does not ask you to become someone impressive enough to earn your place. He invites you to come home to the person you already were in the mind of God before you drew a breath.

> The becoming was never me building a self. It was me being handed one, and finally recognizing the handwriting.

## Who I Made This For

I made *Become Yourself* for anyone worn out from inventing themselves.

For the person exhausted by the pressure to author their own identity from scratch, terrified that if they stop performing the whole thing collapses. For the one grieving a detour they still think was a wrong turn. For the quiet kid in the corner who was told their quiet was a flaw. For everyone ashamed of an earlier self they'd rather forget, who needs to hear that she wasn't the embarrassment. She was the doorway, and she was loved.

You do not have to build yourself. You were built, with care, by Someone who has known your name since before there was a you to know. The becoming ahead of you is not a construction project; it is a homecoming. So open the yearbook. Look at the girl who was already there. Welcome her home. You were always you, and the One who made you has been waiting, this whole time, to help you finally walk out whole.

Mmm. Welcome home.
