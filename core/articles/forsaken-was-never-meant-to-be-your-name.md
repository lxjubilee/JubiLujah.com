## The God Who Changes a Name

I have never gotten over the fact that there is a passage in Scripture where **Yahuah** renames an entire land, not a person, a whole country, and does it out loud, like a Father announcing an adoption at the courthouse.

It is Isaiah 62, verse four. *You shall no more be termed Forsaken, and your land shall no more be termed Desolate.* Read it slowly, because those two words, Forsaken and Desolate, were not insults strangers threw. They were the names the land had come to believe about itself. Abandoned. Empty. Passed over. And into that settled identity the Father speaks two new names: *Hephzibah*, which means *my delight is in her*, and *Beulah*, which means *married*. He does not just comfort the forsaken land. He weds it.

I built a whole album to carry that sentence, because I have met too many people wearing Forsaken like it was stitched into their birth certificate. And it was never meant to be your name.

## Why an Old Hymn

I could have written this as a brand-new anthem and left it there. Instead I went and found a hymn from 1876, *Beulah Land*, written by a man named Edgar Page Stites, the kind of song my grandmothers' grandmothers sang through wounds and diseases and hard crossings, and I reimagined it for the modern room.

That was not nostalgia. It was on purpose, and I threaded the melody through the record like a secret. You hear a brief echo of the old tune in the opening song. You hear a second, fuller echo in a tender song halfway through called *At Home in Beulah*, where the wandering finally ends. And then. I will get to where it fully resolves in a moment, because that is the whole trick of the album.

> Beulah Land, no more Forsaken, every name retaken.

I wanted the sound of the saints who have already crossed over standing in the room while we sing. Some truths are too heavy to be new. They need the weight of everyone who has already believed them.

## The Woman with the Index Card

The tender center of this record is a testimony, and it is the reason I know the name-change is not a poetic idea. It is a lived one.

A woman walks into a Sunday-evening service alone. Family gone, a ministry that failed, a divorce that had blown through her like weather. During communion the worship leader reads Isaiah 62, and in the breaking of the bread she hears the Father say her actual name over the noise of every accusation: *you are Hephzibah. You are new.*

She wrote the verse on a three-by-five index card and pinned it to her bathroom mirror. Every single morning it was the first thing she saw. *You shall no more be termed Forsaken.*

> The Father has spoken. Every old name is broken.

That is what renaming actually costs and actually does. The accuser's voice does not get silenced by you arguing with it. It gets silenced by a louder voice, the Father's, assigning you a name the accuser has no authority to revoke. The old names shatter not because they were never spoken over you, but because Someone with more authority has spoken the new one.

### You Have to Cross the Jordan to Get There

Between the renaming and the wedding, I put a song about a crossing, because no one arrives in Beulah without passing through water. It goes to Joshua 3, the priests carrying the ark step into the flooded Jordan, and the moment their feet touch the water, it stands up in a heap and the whole people cross on dry ground.

I love that the miracle did not happen from the safe bank. It happened when the feet got wet. So much of coming into your new name works exactly that way: you have to step toward the promise before you feel the ground go solid under you. And there is a pilgrim's wisdom to it, the letter to the Hebrews says Abraham went out by faith, *looking for the city with foundations, whose builder and maker is God.* Every believer is mid-crossing, carrying a promise held but not yet fully arrived. The married land is real. The road there runs through the river. And **Yeshua** goes before you into the water like the ark went before Israel, so that every step you take is on ground He has already made dry.

## The Secret in the Last Chorus

Here is the twist the whole album is built to spring, and it is my favorite piece of writing on the record.

All the way through, I let you assume *Beulah* is a place, a married *land*, the promised country you cross the Jordan to reach. And it is. But near the end there is a song built on Revelation 21, verse two, where John sees the New Jerusalem coming down out of heaven *adorned as a bride for her husband*. And in that song, the old 1876 hymn melody finally resolves in full, the brief echoes from the beginning and middle at last finding their home, and the secret comes out:

> The married land is the bride coming down. Beulah is the bride that the Lamb came to claim.

The land and the bride are not two things. They are the same name. Isaiah's married land and Revelation's descending bride are one reality. You. The place God calls home and the people God marries are not separate items on a list. **Yeshua** is the Bridegroom, and the whole redeemed creation is the bride, and *Beulah* was always pointing at a wedding, not just a territory.

## The Dance He Never Stops

So the album ends where a wedding ends, with dancing. The Bridegroom takes the floor. The prodigals are welcomed in. And the final song stacks a four-part hymn choir on top of the arena band, the old saints and the new believers singing the same word, *Beulah forever*, because the marriage of the Lamb is an eternal endeavor that never reaches its end.

I made this for anyone who has answered to Forsaken so long they forgot it was ever assigned and not inherent. For the one who reads their own history as desolate. Go find Isaiah 62 and read it like it has your address on it, because it does. The land you thought was abandoned is being married. The name you thought was permanent is being changed. And the God doing the renaming is not merely rescuing you from the old name. He delights in you. *Hephzibah.* My delight is in her. Say it until it stops sounding like someone else's story and starts sounding like your own.
