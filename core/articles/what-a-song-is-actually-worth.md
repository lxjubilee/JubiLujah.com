Two men sat chained in the black of an inner prison. Their backs were torn open, their feet clamped in wood, no fair trial behind them and no morning promised ahead. It was midnight, the hour built to break a person. And in that hour they did the one thing their jailers had no lock for.

They sang.

The other prisoners went quiet and listened, because nobody had heard a sound like that come out of that much pain. Then the ground itself moved, the doors swung open, and every chain in the place fell loose. You can debate the earthquake if you like. What is harder to explain away is the singing, that two men stripped of everything they owned still held the one possession a prison cannot confiscate.

So here is a stranger question than it first appears: **what was that song actually worth?**

We think we know what a song is worth, because we have all seen the number. A track costs less than a cup of coffee. A month of nearly every song ever recorded costs less than one dinner out. The market has priced music down to almost nothing, and we have quietly agreed that almost nothing is what it is. It plays in elevators and waiting rooms and the background of our errands, and most days we have stopped hearing it at all.

But a price and a worth are not the same thing, and no one confuses them for long once they have buried someone.

Think of the song sung at a graveside, the one the family chose because the person who died used to hum it over the dishes. There is a price on that song. Anyone could buy it in ten seconds. And there is not enough money on earth to purchase what it does in that room: it reaches into a place grief had sealed shut and holds a whole life up to the light for three minutes. The market can sell you the file. It cannot sell you what the file carries. Those are two different economies, and most of us are only ever shown the first one.

Here is the reframe, and it changes how you hear every song for the rest of your life: **a song is not a product. It is a container.** Its worth is never the melody by itself. Its worth is whatever it has been made to carry, and what it cost to put there.

The oldest songs we have on record understood this by instinct. A woman named Miriam stood at the edge of a sea that had just closed over an empire, and she did not make a speech. She caught up a timbrel and started a song, and a people who had been someone's property the day before sang themselves into being free. Generations later, a shepherd boy's harp was the only thing on earth that could quiet a king coming apart at the seams, not a physician, not a counselor, a melody. And once, an outnumbered nation sent its singers out ahead of its soldiers, praise walking point in front of the swords, because they had learned that a song is not decoration on the battle. Sometimes it is the weapon.

None of those songs had a market. Every one of them had worth beyond calculation, because of what it carried, memory, covenant, courage, the very presence of the Maker moving into a room on the back of a melody.

And every one of them cost the singer something. This is the part the market cannot see and a steward must not miss. Miriam sang on the far side of a terror she had walked through on foot. The shepherd sang out of years alone in a field. The two men in the prison sang from raw backs. A song lifted off the surface of an easy life carries about as far as you would expect. The ones that move nations are quarried out of somebody's obedience, and the cost is precisely why they are worth anything at all.

Which is why the worth of a song and the money for a song must never be allowed to change places. The moment a song is engineered to move a wallet instead of a heart, it has been counterfeited, same melody, forged contents. The container is still there; it is just carrying nothing but the ask. People feel the difference even when they cannot name it, the way you can tell foil from gold by its weight in the hand. Worship was never meant to be a lever on anyone's fear or their bank account. A song bent to pull money out of frightened people is not a song. **It is a theft wearing a chorus.**

Now stand back and look at what music has actually outlived. The empire that jailed those two singers is a ruin you visit on holiday. The songs are still being sung tonight, in languages that empire never heard of, by people who will never see the ruins. A single melody has crossed centuries no army could hold, slipped every border no treaty could open, and buried every regime that ever tried to ban it. You cannot own a song like that. Kings have tried. You can copyright the recording; you cannot copyright what it carries, because that belonged to Yahuah before it was ever pressed onto anything, and it will be sung long after the last recording is dust.

The oldest picture we are given says the morning stars sang together before the world was finished, the music was in the architecture before the ground was laid. The last picture we are given is a new song, sung by a crowd no one could number, that nobody could learn until they had been bought out of every nation on earth. Music opens the book of creation and it closes it. That is not a soundtrack. That is a thread the whole thing is strung on.

So, what is a song actually worth?

Not the number on the screen. A song is worth exactly what it carries and exactly what it cost, and the greatest songs carry the one thing the market has no line item for: the presence of the One who invented sound, handed to a person who could never afford it, on the strength of Yeshua, who already paid. That is the real economy of worship. Nothing about it is free, and none of it is for sale.

So here is the one thing to try. The next time a melody stops you in the middle of an ordinary day and your throat tightens and you cannot say why, do not reach for the price. Ask the steward's question instead: what is this carrying, and Who is it trying to hand me? Sit in that for a moment before you scroll on. There is a Voice underneath the good ones, and it has been singing over people since before the stars had their parts. It knows your name, and it is still, right now, trying to reach you on the back of a melody you could have bought for the price of nothing.

**You were never as cheap as the market told you. Neither was the song.** Go and listen like someone who has finally understood what he is holding.
