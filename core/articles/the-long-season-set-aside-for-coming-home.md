There is a Hebrew word for repentance, and it does not mean what you are afraid it means.

It does not mean grovel. It does not mean hate yourself until God is satisfied. It does not mean finally, after enough guilt, managing to be good enough to be let back in. The word is *teshuvah*, and it comes from a root that means, plainly and beautifully, *to turn*, to turn around, to turn back, to return. It is the word you would use for a traveler who has been walking the wrong way and simply stops, turns his body, and starts back toward home.

That is repentance in the language God gave it. Not a punishment you inflict on yourself. A homecoming. A change of direction. The tired heart hears the word *repent* and braces for a lecture, for shame, for a long ledger of everything it has done wrong. But the word itself is gentler than all of that. It just says: you are facing the wrong way, and home is behind you, and you are allowed to turn around.

## A whole season for the turning

Here is what most Christians have never been told: the calendar sets aside an entire stretch of time for this one thing.

Leading up to the great fall feasts of Israel, the trumpet blast, the day of atonement, the week of booths. There comes a long, slow season, a kind of on-ramp into the holiest weeks of the year. In the Hebrew reckoning it falls across the final month of the year, the month called *Elul*, and by long tradition it became the season of return. Not a single frantic day of getting right with God, but weeks of it. An unhurried approach. A whole appointed stretch whose entire purpose is to give a wandering people time to turn back before the holy days arrive.

I want you to feel the mercy in that design. God did not spring the day of atonement on His people cold. He did not say *be perfect by tomorrow morning*. He built a long, gentle runway in front of it, weeks of slowly turning your face back toward home, so that by the time the solemn day came, you had not been ambushed. You had been walking home for a month.

Let me be careful, as I always try to be, about what is Scripture and what is the tradition around it. That the fall feasts stand where they stand on the calendar is written in the text. The rich customs that grew up around the weeks before them, the season of return, the daily turning of the heart, the old practice of sounding the ram's horn each morning through that final month to wake the drowsy soul, those are the beautiful tradition of God's people, and I name them as tradition. But the instinct underneath the tradition is deeply right, and it is this: coming home takes time, and God, in His kindness, gave us the time.

## The Father was already running

If you want to know what teshuvah actually looks like, Yeshua told the story better than anyone ever has.

You know it. A son takes his inheritance, walks as far from home as his feet will carry him, and burns through everything until he is feeding pigs and envying their food. And there, in the pigpen, the Scripture says something quietly enormous: *he came to himself.* He turned. He did not, notice, spend three days perfecting a speech until he was worthy. He was not worthy and he knew it. He simply decided to get up and go back, rehearsing a small confession on the road, expecting at best to be taken on as a servant.

And here is the part that has undone more hard hearts than any sermon: while he was still a long way off, his father saw him. Which means the father had been watching the road. And the father *ran.* An old man, gathering up his robes and running down the road to reach a boy who still smelled of the far country, running not to scold him but to fall on his neck and kiss him and call for a robe and a ring and a feast.

> Repentance is not the long grovel back. It is one honest turn toward home, and a Father who covers the rest of the distance at a run.

That is teshuvah. The son's whole part was to turn around. Everything else, the welcome, the covering, the restoration, the feast, was the Father's part, and the Father had been ready the entire time. He was not waiting to be persuaded. He was waiting to be *given the chance.*

## Turning is not the same as grinding

Let me speak to the ones who have made repentance into one more exhausting labor.

I have shepherded many sincere souls who are worn out not by their sin but by their *repenting*, endlessly rehearsing their failures, apologizing to God for the same thing for the fortieth time, never quite able to believe they have grovelled sufficiently to be received. If that is you, hear me gently: that is not what the word means, and it never was. Teshuvah is a turn, not a treadmill. You do not repent by feeling bad enough for long enough. You repent by facing the right direction and taking a step home. The turn can happen in a single honest moment. The Father does not need you to suffer your way back into His good graces. He needs you to *come.*

And the endless self-punishment, well-meant as it is, is actually a subtle way of staying in the far country, because as long as you are working off your debt, you are still trusting your own effort instead of His welcome. The prodigal did not pay his father back. He could not. He simply came home and let himself be loved, and the feast began. Your groveling adds nothing to a grace that was already complete. Turn, and let yourself be received.

## There is time, and there is a road home

So here is what I want to leave in your hands.

You may have been facing the wrong way for a long while. Maybe you drifted so gradually you cannot even name the day you turned from home, most drifting is like that, quiet and slow, no dramatic rebellion, just a thousand small steps in the wrong direction until one day you look up and the house is far behind you. If that is you, then this is your season. Not a season of shame. A season of turning.

You do not have to arrive all at once. That is the mercy built into the long approach. You are allowed to walk home slowly, to turn a little more each day, to let the return be gentle. And you do not have to walk the whole distance under your own power, because the moment you turn, the Father is already moving toward you. He has been watching the road. He is not standing at the door with His arms crossed. He is coming down it, fast, with your name in His mouth.

So turn. That is the whole of your part. Just face home, and take the step, and discover that the season set aside for coming back was never about how far you had to crawl. It was about how far He was willing to run. Come home. He has been waiting for exactly this, and there is a feast already being prepared.
