The last thing Moses ever did was climb a mountain, and he did it alone, and he was a hundred and twenty years old.

We do not usually end his story there, because it is not the ending we would have written. Forty years leading a stubborn people through a desert, and the reward, when it finally comes, is not to walk into the land he had bled for. It is to climb one more mountain and look at it. The account is spare and almost unbearably tender. Moses went up from the plains of Moab to the top of the mountain, Nebo, the peak of Pisgah, across from Jericho. And Yahuah showed him the land. All of it.

I want to slow down on that, because it is easy to rush to the sadness and miss the glory. God did not just point vaguely at the horizon. The text takes you around the whole panorama with him. It says Yahuah showed him the whole country, the northern hills, the land of the tribes stretching out, the western sea, the southern desert, the valley of Jericho with its palm trees, all the way to the far edges. An old man on a summit at the end of everything, and laid out below him in the clear high-altitude light was the entire thing he had given his life to, every mile of it real, every promise kept, spread out under him like a map made of actual ground.

And then Yahuah said the hard thing, gently: this is the land I swore to give your fathers. I have let you see it with your own eyes. But you will not cross over into it.

## The reward that is a view

We recoil from that, and I understand why. It offends our sense of what faithfulness ought to earn. Surely a life like that should be paid in possession, surely the man who endured everything deserved to set his own feet on the ground he had marched a whole nation toward. And instead he got a view. He climbed to see it, not to keep it. And then he lay down on that mountain and died, and the account says Yahuah Himself buried him in a valley nearby, and no one to this day knows the place.

But sit with the mountain a while and something turns over, the way it always does at altitude. Because a view is not nothing. A view is the thing you climb for. Ask any mountaineer why they suffer up a peak they will only stand on for ten minutes before turning around, and they will struggle to explain it, but it comes down to this: to see. To get high enough that the whole country finally arranges itself beneath you and you understand where you have been and where it all leads. You do not summit a mountain in order to move in. You summit it to see. The seeing is the summit's own gift, and it is enough of one that people give their lives to get it.

And notice what Moses was actually shown. Not his failures. Not the forty years of complaint and rebellion and sand. Yahuah walked him around the fulfillment. Every promise, kept. The land, real. The word God had spoken to a childless old man named Abraham generations before, now spread out in living color under the feet of the prophet who had carried it the last long leg of the way. God gave Moses, at the very end, the one thing that makes an entire lifetime of endurance make sense: He let him see that it had all been true. That the walking had gotten somewhere. That the promise was not a story they told themselves in the desert but an actual country with actual hills, and here it is, look at it, all of it, in the morning light.

## The one who plants and the one who reaps

Here is the part I most want you to hold, because it reframes the ache.

Moses did not enter the land. But the people he had carried did. The next generation walked in on the ground he only ever saw. His obedience did not run out at the river; it ran *through* it, into lives that would cross over on the strength of a faithfulness that had spent itself getting them to the bank. He was, in the deepest sense, the man who planted what someone else would harvest, the one who climbed so the ones behind him could enter.

We have a shrunken idea of a finished life. We think a life only counts if the person who lived it gets to walk into the fulfillment and enjoy it. But the mountain of God has always dealt in a longer economy than that. Some of the most important people in the whole story of Yahuah's people are the ones who got the view and not the possession, who climbed high enough to see that it was all true and real and coming, and then handed the entering to those who came after. Their reward was not the land. Their reward was to know, from the summit, that the land was there, and that the ones they loved would walk into it. That is not a lesser thing. In some ways it is the highest thing a person can be given: to see the whole point of your life from above, confirmed, before you close your eyes.

And Moses did not die diminished. The account pauses to tell you something astonishing about a man of a hundred and twenty: his eyes were not weak, and his strength had not gone. He climbed that last mountain with clear sight and a strong body, not a man wrung out and used up, but a man who had lived every drop of his years and had them still, standing at the top, seeing clearly, strong to the end. The altitude did not defeat him. He met God on the summit and lay down in peace, buried by the hand of the very One he had followed up every mountain of his life.

## For the one who may not get to enter

So let me speak plainly to whoever is reading this from near the end of a long climb, wondering whether they will ever get to walk into the thing they gave their life to.

You have carried something a long way. A family, a work, a calling, a prayer, a people. And you have started to make peace with a hard possibility, that you may not live to see it walk on its own two feet into the land. You may get the view and not the possession. The dream you planted may be harvested by hands that are not yours, by people who will not even know your name, on ground you only ever saw from a distance in the failing light of your own years.

Hear me as a pastor who trusts the high places with his whole heart: the view is not a consolation prize. The view is the summit's true gift, and Yahuah gives it on purpose, to the ones He loves, at the end of a faithful climb. He will not let a life like yours close without walking you up to somewhere high and clear and showing you that it was all true, that the walking got somewhere, that the promise was real ground, that the ones you carried will cross over on the strength of what you spent. You climbed to see. And you will see. And the seeing, from that altitude, in that light, with the whole country spread out below you kept and finished and real, will be more than enough.

You do not have to enter every land you were sent to reach. You only have to climb faithfully to the top of the one mountain God appointed you, and let Him show you the horizon, and lie down in peace knowing the crossing is in surer hands than yours. The summit was always more about the view than the staying. Climb it. Look. It is all true.
