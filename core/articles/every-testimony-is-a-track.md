The pit has no acoustics. That is the cruelty of it.

Picture someone down there right now. Not a dungeon in a movie, the ordinary kind. The kind that goes by a name like grief. Or the third relapse. Or the marriage that just went quiet one Tuesday and never got loud again. Or the long season where the prayers went up and nothing came back down.

Down there, the voice does not carry. You can shout, and the shout just lands at your feet. No echo. No answer. And after a while you stop shouting, because a person can only sing into the dark for so long before the dark convinces him he was never a singer.

Hold that word. Singer. Because this is where the story turns, and it turns on a sound.

There was a shepherd once who became a fugitive who became a king, and somewhere in the middle of all that he ended up in a pit of his own. He wrote down what happened when Yahuah, the God who does not leave people at the bottom, reached in and got him. And here is the part that should stop you: he did not say he was handed a ladder. He did not say he got a lecture, a strategy, a tidy five-step plan for never falling in again.

He said Yahuah lifted him up out of the miry clay, set his feet on a rock, made his steps steady, and put a new song in his mouth.

Not a certificate. Not a receipt. A song.

Which is a strange thing to hand a man who just came up covered in mud. So let me ask the question this whole thing hangs on, and then let me refuse to answer it for a minute, because you deserve to feel the weight of it before I set it down:

**Why a song?**

Sit in that. When you finally get someone out of the worst thing that ever happened to them, a song is the last thing you'd think to give them. Give them rest. Give them a sandwich. Give them silence. But Yahuah, apparently, gives them a track.

And I think I know why, and it has nothing to do with music being nice.

A song is the one thing you cannot keep to yourself. A rescue you can hide. A healing you can privatize, tuck away, mention to nobody. But a song wants out of your chest. It leaks. It carries, down hallways, over fences, into the next car at the red light. The moment He puts a new song in your mouth, He has made your rescue contagious. He didn't just pull you up. He handed you a sound that pulls somebody else.

That is what a testimony is. Your story is a song waiting to be laid down. Every single one of you is walking around with an unrecorded track.

Now here is the turn, and it's the one most people get wrong, the one that's cost more discouraged believers than almost anything I know.

You think the new song is the clean version. The before-and-after. I was that, now I'm this, look how far. No mud. No limp. Studio-polished, autotuned, all the wrong notes edited out. And it is more inspiring that way, and it converts better that way, and I am telling you as plainly as I know how: **that version is a lie, and it will wreck the next person who believes it.**

Because they'll build on your clean track, and then their rescue won't look clean, theirs will still have the mud, the ache, the thing that didn't fully heal, and they'll conclude the whole thing was fake. That He does it for people like you and not for people like them.

But the real song was never clean.

Go back to that shepherd-king. His pit made it into the worship set. His worst night got scored and sung by a whole nation for three thousand years, mud and all. He did not erase the pit to make the song. **The pit is why the song has a low note in it.** And a song with no low note is not a song. It's a jingle.

There was another man in the old accounts who wrestled all night in the dark with Someone he couldn't name, and he came out of it with a new name and a blessing, and a limp he carried for the rest of his life. He won. And he was wounded. And here is the thing nobody wants to hear at the after-party: **the wound was the proof.** You want to know the fight was real? Look at how he walks.

So when Yahuah puts the new song in your mouth, He does not delete the pit from the lyrics. He tunes it. The verse that used to only know how to cry learns how to carry a melody. The thing that was killing you becomes the low note that makes the whole track true. You testify from the limp, not from the healing, because the limp is what lets the person still down in the dark believe you were ever actually there.

That's the sound. That's the drop. Not "I never fell." But "I fell, and He came down after me, and I still walk a little crooked, and I would not trade this song for smooth legs and silence."

So here is where I have to say a quiet thing, because some of you are reading this from the bottom right now, and the dark has done its convincing.

If that's you, if the song feels gone for good tonight, please do not carry that in the pit alone. Reach for a real voice. A friend who'll pick up. A pastor. A counselor. And if the dark is telling you to stop entirely, call or text a crisis line, in the U.S. that's **988**, and it's there right now, tonight. The new song is coming. But you are not meant to wait for it by yourself, and there is no shame, none, in letting a human hand reach down while Heaven does its work. That's not weakness. That's how the rescue usually shows up: with skin on.

And for the rest of you, the ones already climbing, already halfway up with the mud drying on your boots:

Lay the track down.

Stop waiting until the story is impressive. Stop editing out the limp to make it stream better. Your testimony is not a trophy to polish, **it is a track, and a track is meant to be played.** Somebody two streets over, or two chairs over on Sunday, or one text message away, is at the bottom of a pit with no acoustics, halfway convinced they were never a singer.

Your song is their footsteps. It's the sound that says the dark is not soundproof after all.

So sing it. True. Low note included.

Come home, and bring the whole track with you.
