I have pulled a few living things out of places they could not get out of themselves, and I will tell you what I learned each time, before I learned anything else: from the bottom, you cannot see the way up.

You can see the walls. You can see the little disk of sky far overhead. You can see that other people are up there where the ground is solid and the light is warm. But the actual route out, the handhold, the ledge, the angle of escape, is exactly the thing the pit hides from the one who is in it. That is not weakness. That is the geometry of being sunk. Down is a country with its own logic, and one of its laws is that you will always misjudge how to leave.

So when Scripture wants to tell you what your rescue was actually like, it does not reach for a ladder. It reaches for a pit.

## The song from the bottom

There is a song of David that opens down in the low place, and it does not flinch from it. He calls it a pit of destruction, a bog of miry clay, the kind of ground that is worse than no ground, because it holds your feet while it takes you down. Anyone who has stepped into a bog knows the particular terror of it: the harder you fight, the deeper you are pulled, until struggling itself becomes the thing that drowns you. He was stuck like that. Sinking like that. And the whole first movement of his song is not a plan. It is a waiting.

*He waited.* That is the word he chooses. Not *he devised.* Not *he climbed.* He waited, down in the clay, for Someone with a longer reach than his own.

And then the turn comes, and I want you to notice the exact shape of it, because everything is in the shape. Yahuah, he says, *bent down.* He heard the cry, and He reached into the pit, and He drew the man up, up out of the destruction, up out of the miry bog, and set his feet on a rock, and made his steps steady, and put a new song in his mouth.

Read that again slowly and you will find there is no rope in it. There is no coaching from the rim. There is no *you can do it if you really want it.* There is a God who comes down to where the man is stuck, gets under him, and lifts. The clay did not give the man back. He was taken back. And the rock under his feet at the end of the song is not the rock he climbed to. It is the rock he was *set on*, placed there, by hands that had to descend to place him.

> The pit gives you nothing. But the pit is exactly where He reaches, because reaching into the pit is the whole of what He does.

## Why He does not throw the rope

I have thought a long time about why the rescue works this way, and here is the tenderness I have come to.

A rope thrown down still leaves the burden on you. You have to find it in the dark. You have to have the strength left in your arms to grip it. You have to climb your own body-weight up a wet wall while the bog fights to keep you. A rope is a rescue that still requires a rescuer's worth of strength *from the one being rescued*, and a person at the bottom of a real pit has already spent that strength. That is how they got to the bottom.

So He does not throw the rope. That is the gospel the world keeps trying to edit, because the world loves the rope. The rope lets you keep a little of the credit. The rope lets you tell the story afterward as *the day I pulled myself together.* But the true story, the one that actually happened to you, has no such line in it. You did not climb out. You could not have. **You were carried.**

And notice what that costs the One who carries. To lift a sunk thing, you cannot stay clean and dry on the edge and supervise. You have to come down into the same mud. You have to let the bog take hold of your own feet for as long as it takes to get your arms around the one who is drowning. Every rescue that is a carry and not a rope is a rescue where the rescuer goes *into* the thing he is pulling you out of. Hold that thought about Yeshua, and hold it gently, because it is the reason your salvation smells like descent. He did not shout salvation down from a safe height. He came down the ravine.

## The one He carries home

There is a story Yeshua told that lives in the same country as David's song, and it is the plainest picture of a carry that we have.

A shepherd has a hundred sheep, and one of them is not lost in any dramatic way. It simply wanders, the way sheep do, one mouthful of grass at a time, until it lifts its head at dusk and does not know a single thing around it. Now a lost sheep is not a lost dog. It will not find its way back on the scent of home. It will not, in fact, do the one thing that would save it. It stands in the wilderness and cries, and the crying only draws the things that hunt at night. That is the condition. Stranded, unable to self-recover, and running out of light.

And the shepherd, and here is where I have to slow down, because we read past it too fast, the shepherd *goes.* He leaves the settled ninety-nine on the good ground and he walks out into the exact terrain that swallowed the sheep. Down the slope, into the ravine, into the cold that is coming, until he finds the one shivering thing that could not save itself.

Watch what he does not do. He does not stand at the top of the gully and call the sheep's name and wait to see if it has the sense to climb. He knows it does not. It never did. That is what *lost* means. So he goes down to it, and he does not scold it up the hill, and he does not tie a lead around its neck and drag it stumbling behind him. He *lifts it onto his own shoulders* and carries it the whole way back. The entire distance the sheep could not walk, the shepherd walks, with the weight of the sheep across his neck, uphill, in the dark, glad.

And Yeshua says the shepherd comes home rejoicing. Not weary. Not resentful of the trouble. *Rejoicing.* The carry is not the shepherd's burden grudgingly borne. The carry is the shepherd's joy.

## To the one who cannot see the way up

So let me speak to whoever is reading this from the bottom of something.

You have been waiting for the strength to come back so you can climb out and finally deserve the ground up there. You have been listening for instructions, the right prayer, the right discipline, the right amount of wanting it, some rope you could grab to prove you were not as far gone as you feared. And every scheme has left you a little deeper in the clay, because the pit does that to plans.

Hear me as a pastor who has knelt at the edge of more than one drop and reached down into the dark: *the rescue you are waiting for is not a rope.* It never was. Stop measuring whether your arms are strong enough for a climb you were never meant to make. The strong arms in this story are not yours, and they were never supposed to be. Yahuah bends down. Yeshua walks into the ravine. The reach is His, the lift is His, the long uphill carry with your whole weight across His shoulders is His, and He does it *rejoicing,* which is the part your shame will fight hardest to disbelieve.

You do not have to find the handhold. You have to let yourself be found, and be lifted, and stop kicking against the arms that are already under you. The new song David sang did not begin the day he escaped. It began the day he let himself be carried up onto the rock and stood there on feet that Someone else had steadied.

Let yourself be carried. The clay does not get to keep you. The wilderness is not your address. There is a Shepherd already down in the gully with you, already glad, already turned toward home, and the whole distance you cannot walk, He means to walk for you, with you on His shoulders, all the way back.

Rest now, the valley is prayed over, and so are you.
