I want to talk to you about a clock. Not a metaphor, an actual span of hours on an actual afternoon, because sometimes the most staggering thing about what Yahuah did is not the meaning He hid in it but the *timing* He built into it.

The Passover lamb was to be slaughtered on the fourteenth day of the first month, and the Hebrew phrase for when is *bein ha'arbayim*, "between the evenings." Now, I am going to be honest with you about that phrase, because I will not build you a cathedral on a foundation I have to fake. There has been genuine discussion for a very long time about exactly what "between the evenings" means, some understood it as the stretch of the late afternoon as the sun declines toward setting, others reckoned it differently. I am not going to pretend the debate does not exist. But in the era of the Temple, the widely-kept practice was that the lambs were slain in the afternoon, in the hours as the day leaned down toward evening. Picture it: a whole city, thousands of families, thousands of lambs, the priests working through the afternoon, blood and prayer and the smell of a nation being ransomed all over again.

Now hold that afternoon in your mind, and let me take you to a hill.

## The same window of hours

On that fourteenth day, outside the walls of Jerusalem, Yeshua hung on a Roman cross. The accounts tell us darkness came over the land from about the sixth hour, and that near the ninth hour He cried out and gave up His spirit. In the reckoning of that day, the ninth hour lands in the mid-afternoon, the very window when, all across the city below Him, the Passover lambs were falling under the priests' knives.

Let that settle. As the lambs were dying in Jerusalem, the Lamb was dying above it. Not near the same day. The same *afternoon.* The same leaning-down of the sun. While one family after another was watching their lamb give its life so that judgment would pass over their house, the true Lamb was giving His so that judgment would pass over the world.

> The festival had been rehearsing this afternoon for fifteen hundred years. Every lamb that ever died at Passover was a clock ticking toward the one afternoon when the Lamb of Yahuah would die at the very hour the rehearsal had always pointed to.

I do not need to exaggerate a single detail to make this land, and I am not going to, because the naked timing is more powerful than anything I could inflate. He did not die at a random hour on a random day. He died on Passover, in the afternoon of the lambs. The One whom the prophet had called a lamb led to the slaughter went to the slaughter in the very hours the lambs were being slaughtered.

## When a memory becomes a fulfillment

Here is why the clock matters so much, and why I refuse to let it be a footnote.

For fifteen centuries, Passover was a *memorial.* Israel killed the lamb every year to *remember* the night in Egypt when the blood on the door turned death away. It looked backward. It was a nation saying, once a year, "this is what Yahuah did for our fathers." A beautiful, aching memory.

But a memorial that repeats every year is also a memorial that is *waiting.* Every lamb slain was a promise that had not yet fully come due, a picture, a rehearsal, an appointed shadow of something not yet arrived. And then, on one specific fourteenth day, in the afternoon of the lambs, the substance the shadow had been tracing all along stepped into the exact hour and *fulfilled* it. The memory of a deliverance in Egypt became the accomplishment of the deliverance of the world. That is the difference the timing announces. This was not one more lamb in the long line of remembering. This was the Lamb the whole line had been remembering *toward.*

This is why the renewed covenant can say, plainly, that Messiah our Passover has been sacrificed. Not *like* a Passover. *Our* Passover. The feast did not merely illustrate Him from a distance; He walked into its calendar and became the thing it had always been for. The moedim, the appointed times, are exactly that, *appointments.* And He kept this one to the hour.

## For the one who thinks the timing of their life is random

Now let me come sit beside the person who needs this, because I do not teach a clock for the sake of a clock.

Some of you are convinced that the timing of your life is an accident, that your suffering landed in your season by chance, that the hour you are in has no design in it, that you are just weather, not appointment. And I understand why. When you are the one on the hill, the afternoon does not feel like fulfillment. It feels like abandonment. Even Yeshua, in that ninth hour, cried out the ache of feeling forsaken.

But look at what was actually happening in the hour that felt most like being forgotten. It was the most *appointed* hour in the history of the world. Heaven had been pointing to it for fifteen centuries. The very afternoon that looked, from the inside, like the collapse of everything was the afternoon every prophet and every priest and every slain lamb had been reaching toward. The timing that seemed random to the ones watching Him die was, in truth, the most precisely kept appointment ever kept.

So hear me, beloved. The God who timed the death of His Son to the afternoon of the lambs is not careless with the timing of your life. Your worst hour is not outside His calendar. The very season that feels forsaken may be the one that is most appointed, the one that a thousand smaller providences have been reaching toward without your knowing. You are not weather. You are an appointment. And the One who kept His own appointment to the hour, in the dark, forsaken, faithful, is keeping yours too.

He died on the afternoon of the lambs, right on time, so that you would never again have to wonder whether the hours of your life are being watched by Someone who knows exactly what afternoon it is. They are. Rest in that, and lift your head. The festival became a fulfillment on schedule, and so will yours.
