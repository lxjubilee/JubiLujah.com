Picture the far shore in the first light, because we almost never do. We are so trained to stop the story at the water.

A whole nation of slaves has just walked across the floor of a sea. Behind them, the empire that owned them for four hundred years is gone under the returning water, the chariots, the whips, the officials who counted their bricks, the entire machinery that had told them for generations exactly who they were and exactly what they were worth. It is all under the sea now. And the people are standing on the other side, soaked, blinking, free for the first time in living memory, in a body so long enslaved it barely knows the shape of its own liberty.

What do you do with the first hour of freedom? You have earned rest. You have every reason to sit down in the wet sand and simply shake. There is a wilderness ahead that needs planning; there are hungry children and no obvious food; there is a thousand years of trauma to unlearn. Any counselor would forgive them for collapsing.

They sang instead. And a woman named Miriam reached for a tambourine.

## Freedom's first act was a song

Read the account slowly and let the order of events sink in, because the order is the message. The sea closes. The danger ends. And immediately, before the census, before the strategy, before the complaining that we know is coming, before any of the ordinary business of a nation getting organized, the whole people lift a song. It is one of the oldest songs written down anywhere in the Scriptures, this song on the far shore, and its subject is simple: Yahuah has triumphed; the horse and its rider He has thrown into the sea. The empire that could not be argued with, could not be out-run, could not be survived, is at the bottom of the water, and the slaves it could not keep are singing on the beach.

Then the text does something tender. It pulls in for a close-up. Miriam the prophetess, a woman, named, honored, takes a tambourine in her hand, and all the women go out after her with tambourines and with dancing, and she leads them in the refrain. The first named worship leader in the story of the nation is a woman on a beach with a hand drum, teaching a chorus of former slaves how to dance.

I want you to feel how outrageous that instrument is in that moment. A tambourine. Where did it even come from? These were slaves. They left in the middle of the night in a hurry. And yet somewhere in the bundle a woman had thrown over her shoulder as she fled the only home she had ever known, with room for so little. She had made room for that. She packed a tambourine to escape a genocide. Which means that somewhere in the long dark of the slavery, before there was any evidence, before the sea had parted, before there was a single reason to believe the morning would ever come, she had already decided there was going to be something to celebrate. She carried the instrument of the celebration through the years that gave her no cause for one. She was ready to worship on the far shore while she was still on the near one.

## Praise is what freedom sounds like

I carry songs across hard borders for my living, into the guarded tongues of guarded places, and I have learned to watch for a particular sound. It is the sound a person makes the first time they worship out loud after a long season of only being allowed to worship in a whisper, or not at all. It does not sound like performance. It sounds like the far shore.

Because here is what the beach teaches you: praise is not the reward you receive after you are free. Praise is the first thing freedom does. It is how a liberated people announces to itself that the old owner is really gone. Every note Miriam struck on that drum was a declaration the empire could no longer contradict: *you do not tell us who we are anymore.* For four hundred years, someone else had defined them, as property, as labor, as a problem to be managed. The song on the far shore was the first sentence they got to write about themselves, and they wrote it to Yahuah, and they wrote it dancing.

This is why the powers that hold people in hard places are so nervous about singing. It is not the volume. A whispered song frightens them as much as a shouted one, sometimes more. It is what the song *means*. A person who is singing to Yahuah has already crossed the sea in their heart. They have already located the true owner of their life, and it is not the regime, not the border, not the fear, not the thing that has been telling them for years exactly what they are worth. The tambourine on the far shore is a border-crossing you cannot arrest, because it happens inside a person, and the empire is already at the bottom of the sea by the time you hear the drum.

## The rider is already in the water

Let me bring this all the way home, to whatever near shore you are standing on tonight.

You may still be able to hear the empire behind you. That is the honest truth of most crossings, the water has barely closed and the roar of the old thing is still in your ears, and it does not yet feel gone. The addiction, the fear, the accusation that has narrated your worth for years, the border of shame you were sure you would die on the wrong side of. It feels very present. It feels like it could still reach you.

But watch the story. Miriam did not wait until the empire had been gone long enough to feel safe. She sang while the beach was still wet, while the memory was still fresh, while the trauma of four centuries was still in every body on that shore. She sang *because* the rider was in the water, not because she had forgotten he was ever chasing her. The song was not denial of the danger. It was the announcement that the danger was over, sung into a crowd that did not fully believe it yet, until the singing made them believe it.

So reach for the tambourine now. Not when you feel free. Now, while you are still soaked and shaking on the far shore, while the wilderness ahead is still unplanned and frightening. The first act of a freed people is not to understand their freedom. It is to sing it, to strike the drum on the beach and let the sound tell your own trembling heart what your eyes cannot quite trust yet: the horse and its rider are in the sea, the water has closed over the old owner of your life, and you, of all people, are standing on the far shore in the light, free, and it is time to dance.
