A man was once handed a sum of money to look after, and he was so afraid of losing it that he dug a hole in the ground and buried it.

He did not steal it. He did not gamble it or spend it on himself. He simply froze. The fear of doing the wrong thing left him doing nothing at all, and in the story Yeshua (Jesus) told, that was the one choice that cost him everything.

Read that slowly. It may be the most honest picture of your relationship with money that Scripture offers.

You know the feeling. The offering plate comes down the row and something tightens in your chest. Not generosity. Not joy. A low hum of guilt.

Did I give enough? Does wanting more make me greedy? Does having more make me suspect? You have carried that knot into church for years, and no one has ever untangled it for you. They preached around it. They never preached through it.

There is a way this was meant to be taught. It is clean, it is old, and it lifts the weight off your chest instead of piling more on. Most of us were simply never handed it.

## Here is why the conversation gets dodged

Money is the one subject that can empty a room two different ways. Preach that wealth is a sign of favor, and you flatter the comfortable while crushing the struggling. Preach that poverty is holiness, and you shame the provider and make a virtue out of lack.

Both are ditches. So most teachers steer for the middle and say almost nothing, one vague stewardship sermon a year, careful enough to offend no one and free no one.

And the guilt stays. Guilt kept in the dark always grows.

## Now the thing almost no one says plainly

You do not own your money.

Not as a scolding, as a relief. Scripture opens with a claim the modern world has quietly dropped: the earth is Yahuah's, God's, and everything in it. The ground, the gold, the grain, the account with your name on it. Nothing passing through your hands begins with you, and you will not carry a cent of it out.

Which means the whole frame you were handed is wrong. You were taught to see yourself as an owner. And an owner is responsible for outcomes he cannot control, which is exactly why you are anxious.

But you are not an owner. You are a steward. A steward holds what belongs to Another and answers for one thing only: was he faithful with it.

Feel what that removes.

An owner is measured by how much he ends up with. A steward is measured by whether he was trustworthy with what he held. Yeshua said it in a single line that should have reordered how the church talks about money: if you have not been faithful with worldly wealth, who will trust you with the true riches?

Not how much did you accumulate. Faithful. The measure is faithfulness, and it never reverses into gain.

This is why the poor widow who dropped two small coins into the treasury was honored above the wealthy giving from their surplus. The amount was nothing. The faithfulness was everything.

And it is why the man who buried his coin was not judged for losing money. He lost none. He was judged for letting fear turn a trust into a hole in the ground.

## So bring it back to the plate coming down your row

The tightness in your chest was the anxiety of an owner. You were straining to answer a question that was never yours: Am I keeping enough? Getting enough? Is my pile big enough to make me safe?

Set the ownership down and the question changes entirely. Not how much of this is mine. But: am I being faithful with what was never mine to begin with?

That question has an answer you can actually reach. You cannot control the markets, your health, the economy, or tomorrow. You can be faithful today with what is in your hand today. Faithful in little qualifies you for much, and the order never runs backward, which means you begin exactly where you are, with exactly what you have.

Wealth guilt and scarcity fear turn out to be the same mistake wearing opposite coats: both seat you on the throne of a kingdom that was never yours. Come down off it. That throne was always heavy, because it was never built to hold you.

There is a warning folded inside the relief, and honesty requires it.

Yeshua said you cannot serve two masters, Yahuah and money both. He did not call money evil. He said it competes for the seat only One should hold. A steward can handle great sums and stay free. A servant of money can handle small sums and be owned by them. The dividing line is never the size of the account. It is who sits on the throne.

Here is the plain truth. Money is not your master, and it is not your shame. It is a trust, a piece of a kingdom inheritance moving through your hands on its way somewhere else, and your only assignment is to be found faithful with the part you were handed.

That is the conversation the church keeps dodging. And it is the one that sets a guilty conscience free.

So this week, take one thing. A bill. A gift. A paycheck. A decision you have been avoiding. And ask the steward's question instead of the owner's. Not how much of this is mine, but what would faithful look like, right here, with this?

The moment you ask it, you will feel the weight lift. It was never yours to carry. It belongs to the One who owns the earth and everything in it, and He has never once asked you to be its owner.

Only its steward. And that, it turns out, is a far lighter thing to be.
