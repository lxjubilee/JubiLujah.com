I want to teach you to hear something, and I promise that once you hear it you will never listen to a worship song the same way again.

Underneath the melody, underneath the words, underneath the clever architecture of verse and chorus and bridge, every true worship song is doing one thing: it is traveling toward a destination. It is going somewhere. And if you follow enough of them all the way to the end, you discover, with a start, that they are all going to the same place. They are all straining toward one small word. Different songs take wildly different roads to reach it, some sprint, some wander, some climb for four minutes before they arrive, but the destination does not change.

The word is glory.

Not the sound of the word, necessarily. Many songs never sing the syllable at all. I mean the thing the word points to, the moment where the song stops being about the worshiper and becomes entirely about the worth of God, where the self falls quiet and only He is left, filling the frame, and the only fitting response is the lifting up of His name for no reason other than that He deserves it. That destination has an old name. Theologians call it doxology. Musicians would call it the resolution. It is the note the whole song was tuned to land on. And I am convinced that a worship song is only finished, truly finished, when it gets there.

## The song that never arrives

You can feel the difference between a song that reaches it and a song that does not, even if you have never had words for why.

Some worship songs are lovely the whole way through and yet leave you strangely unsatisfied, and for the longest time I could not explain the feeling. Now I can. Those songs never arrive. They circle the worshiper. They are about my longing, my surrender, my need, my journey, real things, honest things, and worth singing, but they keep the camera pointed at the self, and they end still pointed at the self. The song fades out with me still at the center of the frame. It was a beautiful road that never reached the city. And some part of you, made for the destination, feels the lack even when you cannot name it. You wanted to arrive somewhere, and the song set you down where it picked you up.

Then there are the songs that arrive. They may start with the self, most do, most should, because that is where we honestly are, down in the need, the ache, the mess. But they do not stay there. Somewhere in their travel they turn, and the turning is the whole art of it: the focus lifts off the worshiper and swings onto God, and by the final passage the self has gone quiet and only His worth is filling the room. You are no longer singing about your journey. You are simply beholding Him and saying so. That is arrival. That is the song reaching glory. And the deep satisfaction you feel when a song lands there is not merely musical. It is the satisfaction of a thing that finally became what it was for.

## Why this is the deep structure of all praise

Here is the part the builder in me finds almost unbearably beautiful, because it means the shape of a good worship song is not arbitrary. It is a rehearsal of the shape of reality.

We were made to move from ourselves toward God. That is the whole trajectory of a redeemed life, off the throne of the self and onto our faces before the worth of Another, not by being crushed but by being finally set free from the exhausting work of being the center. Every human being is either at the center of their own frame, straining, or they have made the turn and let God fill it, and are at rest. The entire journey of faith is that one turn, made and remade, deeper each time.

And a well-built worship song rehearses exactly that turn, in four minutes, in your own mouth. It takes you where you actually are, self at the center, needy and honest, and then it walks you through the turn, and it sets you down at the destination you were made for, beholding Him. Every time you sing a song that arrives, you are practicing the master motion of your own existence. You are training your soul to make the turn. That is why the songs that reach glory do something to you that the songs that circle the self never can. They are not just describing worship. They are drilling you in the actual shape of it.

This is what I mean when I say the whole architecture of praise has a deep structure. Verse, bridge, key change, these are just the road. The road exists to get you to the one destination. And a songwriter who understands this stops arranging pretty rooms and starts building roads that actually arrive.

## Where the destination comes from

None of this is my invention. I am only describing the pattern Scripture sets everywhere it lets us watch worship happen.

When Solomon's temple was finished and the singers and players became one sound, lifting up the goodness and enduring love of God as a single voice, the account tells us the glory of the Lord filled the house so completely the priests could not stand to serve. The song reached for glory, and Glory came and filled the room. That is the destination made visible. The praise arrived, and God met the arrival with His weighty, present, overwhelming worth.

Look wherever worship is unveiled and you find the same shape. Around the throne, the living creatures never stop, and what do they finally arrive at, over and over? Holy, holy, holy, and worthy. Not a word about themselves. Pure destination. And when the veil lifts on the end of everything, heaven does not sing a song about its own long journey home. It sings a song whose whole content is the worth of the Lamb who was slain. Every road in Scripture, followed to its end, arrives at the same place: God, filling the frame, worshiped for His worth. Glory. The songs of heaven are not still circling the self. They arrived long ago, and they are still standing in the destination, singing.

That is the word your songs are reaching for, too. When you feel a worship song straining upward toward something, that strain is the pull of home. The song wants to arrive where the whole redeemed creation is already gathered. It is trying to get you there early.

## Learn to hear it, and sing toward it

So here is what I want you to do, and it costs you nothing but attention.

The next time you sing, listen for the destination. Ask, as the song moves: where is this going? Is it circling me, or is it turning toward Him? Is it going to arrive, or is it going to fade out with me still at the center? You will start to feel it immediately, which songs travel and which songs merely idle, which ones set you down at the destination and which ones leave you where they found you.

And when a song does arrive, when it makes the turn and lifts your eyes off yourself and fills the frame with God and leaves you beholding His worth with nothing left to add, do not rush past that moment. Stay in it. That is the whole point of the song. That is the word it was built to reach. That is the place you were built to reach.

Every worship song is trying to get to one word. Sing the ones that actually arrive. And when they set you down in glory, let them leave you there, because that is not the end of the song.

That is home.
