I work in two languages, and I have learned that translating a worship song is one of the most spiritually revealing jobs in the church. Not because it is glamorous. Because it forces you to decide, line by line, what you are actually willing to lose.

People imagine translation is a matter of finding the equivalent words. It is not. If it were, you could hand it to a machine. The trouble begins the instant you try to carry a hymn from English into Romanian, or the other way, and discover that the song is not made of words the way you thought. It is made of words that rhyme, in a specific number of syllables, landing on specific beats, arriving at specific emotional weight, all while saying something true about God. And no two languages agree on how to do all of that at once. Romanian stresses its syllables in its own way, stacks its sounds in its own way, reaches for its rhymes in its own way. The English original was carved to fit English. Pour it into Romanian and it does not fit. Something has to give.

So you sit there with the beam in your hands and you have to choose what breaks.

## You cannot keep everything, so you rank

The first honest thing a translator admits is that a perfect carry is impossible. You will not keep the rhyme and the meter and the exact images and the literal words and the emotional temperature all at once. The languages will not permit it. You are going to drop something. The only real question, and it turns out to be a theological question, not a linguistic one, is what you are permitted to drop and what you must protect with your life.

Over years of this, I have built a ranking, and I did not build it on my own taste. I built it on what the song is for.

The rhyme can go. It hurts to lose it, and I fight for it, but if keeping the rhyme forces me to bend the meaning, the rhyme goes. It is trim. Beautiful trim, but trim.

The exact images can flex. A metaphor that lands perfectly in English sometimes lands strangely in Romanian, or means something slightly off, and I will trade it for a different image that carries the same truth to a Romanian heart. The picture is a vehicle. If a different vehicle delivers the same cargo, I will change the vehicle.

The meter I fight harder for, because the meter is married to the melody, and the melody is not mine to rewrite, the whole congregation already carries it in their bodies. So the new words have to fit the old tune. That constraint is brutal and I honor it.

But underneath all of it, un-droppable, non-negotiable, is one thing. The truth about God that the song exists to confess. That does not move. That is the load-bearing wall, and you do not relocate a load-bearing wall to make the rhyme prettier. If I have to choose between a gorgeous Romanian line that slightly distorts who God is, and a plainer line that says it true, I take the plainer line every single time, and it is not close.

Because here is what I finally understood: I am not translating a song. I am translating a confession that happens to be sung.

## The verse that taught me what I was really doing

There was a hymn I loved, a stout old English one about the sufficiency of Christ, and I spent the better part of a week on a single verse. The rhyme scheme in English was tight and lovely, and I wanted it in Romanian so badly. And I found a way, a genuinely pretty Romanian couplet, it sang beautifully, but to get the rhyme I had to soften one word. In the English, the line said God's grace does the saving, fully, finished, nothing added. My pretty Romanian version nudged it toward God's grace helps. Helps. Just helps.

It rhymed. It sang. And it was a different gospel.

I sat with that couplet for a long time, because it was the best line I had written all week, and I had to throw it away. A grace that helps is a grace you finish yourself, and that is not the grace the hymn was written to confess, and it is not the grace that holds a person up at the end of their strength. The whole reason the song was worth singing lived in the word I had softened to make it pretty.

So I broke the rhyme. I wrote a rougher Romanian line, unrhymed, slightly graceless, that said the true thing plainly: grace does the saving, all of it. And that rougher line is the one that goes out and gets sung, because a true rough line will hold a grieving Romanian believer at 3 a.m. and a pretty false one will drop her.

That was the week I stopped being a wordsmith who translated songs and became a builder who guards a foundation while moving it between houses.

## Why this is the church's oldest work

If this sounds like a niche craft problem, look wider, because moving the truth across languages without dropping it is not a modern hobby. It is very close to the central labor of the whole church.

The good news was never meant to stay in one tongue. From the beginning it was being carried, the words of a Hebrew and Aramaic world poured into Greek, spoken suddenly in every language at once when the Spirit fell, sent out under a commission that reached every nation, every people, every tongue. And the vision at the end of the story is not one language triumphing over the others. It is a countless multitude out of every nation and tribe and people and language, and they are not reading in silence. They are singing, together, one song, in a thousand mother tongues, and it does not fracture into noise. It resolves into praise.

That is the promise the translator is serving, one hymn at a time. Every language is invited to sing the same truth in its own voice. The voices differ; the truth does not. My job, the builder-teacher's job, is to make sure that when the song crosses from English into Romanian, the thing that arrives is genuinely the same confession, wearing new clothes, and not a lovely counterfeit that rhymes.

## What this asks of you, even if you never translate a word

Most people reading this will never carry a hymn between languages. But every one of you carries the faith between people, to a child, a friend, a stranger, a doubting version of yourself at 3 a.m. And you are doing exactly what I do at my desk: deciding what you are willing to lose to make the truth fit the moment.

The temptation is always the same. Soften the hard word to make the line prettier. Trade the true thing for the thing that lands easier. Let grace saves quietly become grace helps because it goes down smoother in the room you are in.

Do not do it. Drop the rhyme. Drop the polish. Drop the version of the truth that flatters. Guard the one thing that is load-bearing, who God actually is and what He has actually done, and let everything else flex around it. A rough true word will hold a person's weight. A polished false one will drop them exactly when they lean.

Carry the song into every language and every heart it can reach. Change the words as much as you must. Just never, ever move the wall that holds up the house.
