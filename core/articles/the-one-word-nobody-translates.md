When one of my songs crosses a border, almost everything about it gets remade.

I have watched it happen. A line I wrote in English is carried into Japanese, into Dutch, into Romanian, into Swedish, into Thai, and the translators, who are artists in their own right, take it apart and rebuild it. The rhymes vanish and new ones are found. The images bend to fit a different sky. A metaphor that sings in one tongue lands flat in another and has to be replaced with something a grandmother in that language would actually feel. By the time the song arrives in a new mouth, nearly every word has been through customs and come out changed. That is not a loss. That is the miracle of translation, love, taking the trouble to become local.

But there are one or two words that refuse.

They reach the border, and the border waves them through unchanged. The translator sets down the pen. Because some words cannot be improved by remaking, and everyone who works with language for long enough eventually meets them.

*Hallelujah* is the great one.

Follow it around the world and it will astonish you. In English we sing it. But so do worshippers in Japanese, and in Dutch, and in Romanian, and in Swedish, and in Thai, and in nearly every other language that has ever lifted a song to God. Not a translated version. The *same word*, Hebrew at the root, *hallelu-Yah,* praise Yah, carried whole and un-remade across thousands of years and thousands of miles into mouths that share almost nothing else. A word so full that no language felt it had the right to replace it, only to receive it. *Amen* does the same thing. So does the older *alleluia.* A tiny handful of words that the whole singing world holds in common, untranslated, unchanged, passed hand to hand across every border on earth like a coin everyone already recognizes.

I cannot tell you how much this moves me, and I want to try to tell you why, because I think it is pointing at something enormous.

We are a scattered species. That is the plain fact of us. Thousands of languages, and most of them cannot understand each other at all. The old Scriptures tell a piercing little story about how it happened, a tower, a proud people determined to make one name for themselves and climb to heaven on their own engineering, and a God who, rather than let that pride harden into something monstrous, scattered their speech until they could no longer understand one another and had to spread out across the earth. Ever since, we have lived behind the walls of our own tongues. It is why translation is holy work and heartbreaking work at once, every act of it is a small, tender attempt to reach across a divide that runs right down the middle of the human family.

And yet, here is the wonder, through all that scattering, one word got through the walls intact.

Not a word of commerce. Not a word of conquest. Not the name of an empire. A word of *praise.* The single thing the scattered languages of the earth still hold in common, unchanged, is the word we use to worship. When we go to lift something up to God, we reach past all our different vocabularies and find, waiting in every one of them, the same untranslated sound. It is as if the scattering that runs through everything else stops at the door of worship, as if, at the exact point where humanity turns together toward its Maker, the confusion of tongues quietly lifts, and for one word we are one people again.

I don't think that is an accident of history. I think it is a rumor of the ending.

Because the Scriptures do not leave us scattered. They open, at the very end, onto a scene that has kept singers like me awake at night with longing, a vast crowd that no one could number, from every nation and tribe and people and language, standing together before the throne, and out of that impossible diversity comes not chaos but a *song.* And what does the great multitude of every tongue on earth cry out together in that final scene? The old writers tell us plainly. *Hallelujah.* The one word that never needed translating turns out to be the first word of the world made whole. The sound the scattered languages kept in common all along was the sound of where they were always heading.

So every time you sing that word, in whatever language you were born into. You are doing something far larger than you know. You are rehearsing the reunion. You are speaking the one syllable that the Japanese worshipper and the Romanian worshipper and the Thai worshipper and the Swedish worshipper and the Dutch worshipper are speaking too, in the very same form, so that in that single word, and, for now, almost only in that word, the scattering is already undone. Worship is the place where the walls of Babel go transparent. It is the one room where we already understand each other.

I find that unspeakably hopeful, and I want to hand the hope to you directly, because I think some of us have quietly given up on ever belonging to the whole.

You may feel like the odd voice. Wrong language, wrong background, wrong story to ever fully fit the great human choir, always translating yourself, always at the border, never quite waved through. But there is a word that was made for exactly you, and it asks nothing of your accent, your fluency, your pedigree, or your past. It has crossed every border ever built between people, and it will cross the ones inside you too. You do not have to learn a new tongue to belong to the song at the center of everything. You already know the word. You have probably already said it. It is the one nobody translates, because everybody is welcome to it, because it was always meant to be the sound of the whole family finding its way home.

That is the whole promise, folded into four syllables the world could not stop passing along.

The scattering is not the last word. *Hallelujah* is.

Go ahead and say it, in your language, which is also, in this one word, everyone's. You are not only praising. You are rehearsing the day the walls come down for good, and the numberless crowd of every tongue on earth turns toward the throne and discovers, with joy, that they all know how the song begins.
