The person I most want in our gatherings is usually the one standing farthest from the door, convinced the whole thing is happening for somebody else.

I meet them everywhere. At the funeral of a relative, hanging back by the wall, moved by the singing and certain they have no business being moved. Outside the building on a Sunday, close enough to hear the worship through an open window, close enough to want it, not close enough to believe they would be welcome inside. They have decided, somewhere along the way, that they are on the wrong side of a line, too far gone, too unchurched, too broken, too late, and that the best they can do is listen from the margin and not make a scene. And so the very people the whole song is trying hardest to reach are the ones most sure it is not for them.

There is a man in the Gospels who was exactly that far out on the edge, and the way his story turns is the truest picture I know of what actually happens when God comes near.

His name was Bartimaeus, and he was a blind beggar who sat by the roadside outside Jericho. That is the whole of his life as the world saw it, the edge of the road, the far margin of the town, a man on a mat with a bowl, one of the people you learn to walk past. And one day the road fills with a crowd, and word ripples down that Yeshua of Nazareth is passing by. Bartimaeus cannot see Him. He can only hear that He is near. And out of the dark, from the edge of the road, he starts to shout. Son of David, have mercy on me. He shouts it into a crowd that is not looking for him, a crowd with no reason to notice one more beggar's voice.

And the crowd does exactly what crowds do to the ones on the margin. They tell him to be quiet. Many of them, the Scripture says, rebuked him, told him to hold his peace, to stop making a scene, to keep his broken voice down. The respectable procession did not want the interruption. The people close to the action tried to shut out the one furthest from it. And I have to name this plainly, because the church has done it too: the crowd around Yeshua is often the very thing that stands between the desperate one and Him. The ones who think they belong can make the one who fears he does not feel it more sharply than ever.

But watch what Bartimaeus does when they try to silence him. He does not shrink back. He does not decide they must be right about him. He cries out all the more, louder, more insistent, more shameless in his need. Son of David, have mercy on me. He would rather be an embarrassment than stay unheard. And that, I have come to believe, is the truest form of faith available to a person on the edge: not certainty, not eloquence, just the refusal to stop calling when everyone tells you to stop.

And then comes the moment the whole story exists for. Yeshua stops. In the middle of the moving crowd, with everywhere to be and everyone pressing in, He stands still, because one voice from the margin reached Him even though a hundred voices tried to bury it. And He says three words that I think are the most pastoral words in the Gospels: call him here.

Now feel what happens next, because it is beautiful and we rush past it. The message has to travel. It goes back down the line, from the center of the crowd to the very edge of it, from the ones who belong to the one who did not. And the same crowd that a moment ago was telling Bartimaeus to shut up is now the crowd carrying him the invitation. Take heart, they tell him. Get up. He is calling you. The word passes hand to hand, voice to voice, all the way out to the man on the mat: He's calling you. He's calling you. The message crossed the whole distance from the center to the margin to reach the one person who was sure it was not for him.

I have spent a good part of my life trying to be one link in that line. When we take a single song and carry it, painstakingly, across ten different languages, into tongues so small the strategists say do not bother, this is what I believe we are really doing. We are passing the message down the line. We are carrying the same three words all the way out to the far edge of the road, to the one who has been shouting into the dark in a language she was sure no one on the inside spoke, to say to her in the words her own mother used: take heart, get up, He is calling you. The song is not the point. The song is the runner, sprinting the invitation from the center out to the one who thought the whole procession would pass without ever stopping for someone like her.

And then Bartimaeus does the thing that I cannot stop preaching. When he hears that Yeshua is calling him, he throws off his cloak, springs up, and comes.

You have to understand what that cloak was. For a beggar, the outer cloak was everything. It was his blanket in the cold and his shelter from the sun, and spread on the ground it was the very thing he laid out to catch the coins that kept him alive. It was his security, his livelihood, his whole safety net, the one possession a man in his position could not afford to lose. And when the call came, he did not gather it up carefully and bring it with him just in case. He flung it aside. He left his security lying in the dust of the road and ran toward a voice he could not even see, because the moment he heard that he was wanted, the thing he had been clutching for survival suddenly weighed nothing at all.

That is what coming to God actually looks like. It is not arriving with your life in order. It is not being sure you belong. It is hearing, against every argument in your head, that you are the one being called, and then leaving in the road the thing you were positive you could not live without, and running toward Him before you have time to talk yourself out of it.

So let me say it to the one on the edge, the one who has read this far from the far side of some line you drew yourself. You have the story backwards. The center of that crowd is not where the action is; the edge of the road is. Yeshua stopped the whole procession for the one voice everyone else was trying to quiet. He is not annoyed by your shouting. He is not waiting for you to make yourself presentable before He will notice you. The interruption you are afraid to be is the exact thing that makes Him stand still. And the message is already traveling down the line toward you, in whatever language your heart actually speaks, carrying the only three words that matter: He is calling you.

You do not have to fix your life first. You do not have to bring the cloak. Leave it in the road. Get up. He stopped the whole crowd for you, and He is standing still right now, waiting for the sound of someone running toward Him from the edge, out of the dark, at last.
