There is a woman in the third row and she cannot sing today. I have watched her a hundred times, in a hundred rooms, though it is never quite the same woman. This week she buried someone. Or the marriage ended, or the test came back wrong, or the child is still not speaking to her, or she simply woke up under a weight she cannot name and cannot lift. And she came anyway, because some stubborn thread of faith pulled her through the doors, and now the band is bright and the room is glad and every song is a celebration, and she stands there with her mouth closed, because there is not one honest word in the whole set that her heart can form.

She is not faithless. She is grieving. And the tragedy is not that she has no faith to sing with. The tragedy is that the church handed her a songbook with half the pages torn out, and the pages that are missing are the only ones she could have sung today.

Because the songbook God actually gave His people was never all in the major key. A great portion of it, scholars who count these things say something close to a third, is grief. Complaint. Protest. Sorrow set to melody and kept, on purpose, forever. The woman in the third row has a song. We just stopped singing it.

## God authorized the sad songs and never took them back

Open the oldest hymnbook of God's people and you will be startled by how much of it is in pain. These are not stray verses of sadness buried in otherwise happy songs. These are whole songs, entire compositions whose reason for existing is to bring grief, anger, confusion, and complaint directly to God and lay it at His feet.

There is a song that opens by crying *how long?* four times over, how long will You forget me, how long will You hide Your face, how long must I wrestle with my thoughts and carry sorrow in my heart all day. That is not a warm-up to praise. That is the song. It is a man shaking his fist at the silence of heaven, and God kept it, and gave it to His people to sing, which means God authorized His children to say *how long* to His face and call it worship.

There is a song that asks, plainly, *why are you cast down, O my soul, and why so disturbed within me*, a believer talking to his own drowning heart, admitting he is going under, refusing to pretend the water is not rising.

And there is one song, the darkest in the whole book, that goes down into the pit and does not come back up. It names God as its only hope and then spends every line describing abandonment, and in its final words it says that darkness has become the singer's closest friend. That is how it *ends.* No sunrise. No resolution. No triumphant last verse where everything turns out fine. And that song is in the canon. God, who edits His own book with perfect care, chose to keep a song that ends in the dark, because He knew there would be nights that end in the dark, and He would not leave His children with nothing to sing on those nights.

Read that and feel the weight of it. The One who could have filled His songbook with nothing but victory chose to fill a third of it with sorrow. That was a decision. It is doctrine, sung. It tells us something the bright-only worship of our day has forgotten: that faith is not required to pretend, that God is not offended by your grief, that bringing Him your complaint is not the *opposite* of worship. It *is* worship, of the costliest and most trusting kind.

## Lament is not the absence of faith. It is faith in the dark.

Here is the thing I most want the woman in the third row to understand, and the thing the church most needs to relearn.

We have quietly come to believe that a sad song is a small failure of faith, that if she really trusted God she would be able to sing the happy ones, that lament is what you do until you get your faith back. That is exactly backward. Turning your sorrow *toward* God instead of away from Him is one of the deepest acts of faith a human being can perform. The person who has stopped believing does not lament. They go silent, or they leave. Lament is what faith does when it is in pain but has not let go. It is the refusal to walk away *and* the refusal to pretend, both at once, held together in a song. To cry *how long, O Lord* is to still be talking to Him. It is to still call Him *Lord* while you are angry at His silence. That is not the failure of faith. That is faith doing the hardest thing it will ever do.

And notice what nearly every one of those grief-songs does, sooner or later, though not always by the end. Somewhere in the middle of the complaint, the ground shifts. The man who cried *how long* four times turns, before he is finished, and says he will yet trust in God's steadfast love and sing to Him. Not because the circumstances changed, nothing in the song got better, but because the very act of dragging his sorrow into God's presence and leaving it there did something the sorrow alone could never do. The lament was the road back. He did not get to the trust by skipping the grief. He got there by singing straight through it. And the songbook keeps both the grief and the turn, because a people needs to know that the road runs *through* the valley, not around it.

## Yeshua sang in the minor key too

If you ever wonder whether the sorrow-songs are beneath the dignity of real worship, look at the One we worship.

On the night He was betrayed, after the last supper, Yeshua and His friends sang a hymn before they went out to the garden. The songs traditionally sung on that night were not light. They carried lines of anguish and rescue, of being surrounded by death and calling on the name of the Lord. He sang them, walking knowingly toward His own arrest. And then in the garden itself He was, in His own words, sorrowful to the point of death, and He fell on His face and asked if the cup could pass. He wept at the tomb of a friend He was about to raise, standing in the full knowledge of resurrection and grieving anyway, because grief is not unbelief and He knew it in His body. The Man of Sorrows did not sing only in the major key. He knew the whole songbook. He sang the hard songs the night before the hardest thing, and He did not consider it a failure of faith. He considered it the truth.

So the woman in the third row is in the best possible company. When she cannot sing the bright songs, she is not falling short of Yeshua. She is standing exactly where He stood, in the sorrow, facing God, refusing to pretend and refusing to leave.

## So put the minor-key pages back

Here is what I want us to do with this, whether you lead worship or only ever stand in the rows and grieve.

If you have any hand in what a room sings, do not give them only the major key. Do not build a songbook that has no room for the woman in the third row, because she is in every room, every week, and the gospel you are singing claims to be good news for exactly her, the brokenhearted, the crushed, the ones at the floor. Make sure some of what you sing is honest enough that a grieving person can sing it without lying. Give the room permission, in melody, to say *how long* and *why* and *I am going under and I still call You Lord.* That permission is not a concession to weakness. It is one of the truest things you can hand a congregation, and one day it will be the only page they can sing from, and they will need to already know it is there.

And if you are the woman in the third row today, hear this. Your silence in the bright songs is not a verdict on your faith. There is a song for you, and it is old, and it is holy, and God Himself wrote it into the book and never took it back. You are allowed to sing in the dark. You are allowed to bring Him the complaint, the anger, the *how long,* and call it worship, because it is. And if you will sing your sorrow *toward* Him instead of swallowing it or carrying it out the door, you may find, as the old singers found before you, that the lament itself is the road, and that it runs straight through the valley to the place where, one day, you will trust again.

A third of the songbook is in a minor key. God put it there on purpose, for a night He knew was coming. Do not let anyone tell you those pages are torn out. Sing them. They will hold you, all the way through the dark, to the morning on the other side.
