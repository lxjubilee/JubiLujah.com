There is a command in the law of the altar so quiet you can read the whole book and never notice it, and yet once you have seen it you will never worship the same way again. Among all the instructions about the offerings, Yahuah gives one about the *fire itself*, not what to burn on it, but the flame beneath everything. The fire on the altar, He says, shall be kept burning. It shall not go out. And then, as if once were not emphatic enough, the command is repeated and pressed down: a perpetual fire, burning continually, that must never be allowed to die.

Not a fire for the festivals. Not a fire lit fresh each morning for the day's work and let go cold at night. A fire that had to be *always burning*, through the darkness when no one was offering anything, through the long ordinary stretches between feasts, through every season of the year without exception. The Hebrew word behind it is **tamid**, continually, perpetually, without interruption. The one thing the priests could never let happen was for that altar to go cold.

And so someone had to tend it. Every morning, before the sun was up, a priest came in the dark and laid fresh wood on the coals and made sure the flame had lived through the night. It was the first act of the day, done before the congregation gathered, before anyone was watching, in the grey hour when no one would have known if he had skipped it. The whole visible worship of the nation rested on an invisible, unglamorous, daily act of *feeding a fire nobody was looking at.*

## Worship Is a Fire, Not an Event

We think of worship as an event, a gathering, a moment, a service that begins and ends. And there is real glory in the gathering; I love the assembled fire, the roar of many voices together. But the altar teaches something the gathering alone can never teach: that beneath every event of worship there must be a *continuous* worship, a flame that was already burning before the crowd arrived and will still be burning after they leave.

The offerings came and went. Morning and evening, feast and fast, the sacrifices changed with the calendar. But the fire underneath them did not change. It was the constant beneath all the variety, the thing that made every particular offering possible, because you cannot present anything on a cold altar. The event needs the fire. The fire does not need the event.

I want you to hear what that means for your own life with Yahuah. Your gathered worship, the Sunday, the set, the corporate roar, is the offering laid on the altar. But there is meant to be a fire underneath it that burns on Tuesday afternoon and Thursday at midnight and through the whole grey week when nothing is happening and no one would know if you let it go cold. *That* fire, the perpetual one, is the real state of a worshipping life. The gatherings are what you lay on it. The flame is what you keep.

> The question was never whether the fire could blaze on the feast day. The question was whether it survived the ordinary night.

## The Danger Was Never a Storm

Notice what the command guards against. It does not say, *guard the fire from floods, from enemies, from great catastrophes.* The threat to a perpetual flame is never dramatic. Fires do not usually die in storms. They die in *neglect*, in the slow, quiet, unremarkable failure to add fuel, one skipped feeding at a time, until the coals grey over and the warmth is gone and no single moment can be blamed.

That is exactly how worship dies in a life, and it is why I write this with tenderness rather than alarm. Almost no one loses their fire in a crisis. The crisis, if anything, blazes it up. The fire goes out in the *undramatic* seasons, the tired weeks, the busy months, the stretch where prayer got skipped once and then twice and then stopped being missed. No decision was ever made to grow cold. The wood simply stopped being laid on, quietly, in the dark, where no one was watching, and one morning the altar was cold and no one could say when it had happened.

Which means the whole battle for a burning life is fought in the grey hour before dawn, in the unglamorous, unwitnessed act of *feeding the fire again*, one more log of prayer, one more song sung to an empty room, one more return to the Word when nothing in you feels it. This is the priestly work no congregation applauds and no worship set contains. It is done by hand, in private, before anyone gathers.

## Fire You Feed, Fire He Sends

Now hear the mercy in it, because there are two fires in the story and you must not confuse them. The fire on that altar did not begin with a human match. Its origin was *heaven's* fire, sent down and received. Yahuah is the one who kindles. No priest could manufacture that flame out of his own effort. But once it had fallen, the keeping of it was entrusted to human hands. God sends the fire. We feed it.

That is the shape of a burning life, and it saves you from two lies at once. The first lie says worship is all on you, that you must generate the flame by sheer willpower, and if you feel nothing then there is nothing there. Not so. The fire is His gift; He kindled it and He sustains its source. The second lie says worship is all on Him, that you may sit passive and wait to be moved, and if you feel cold it is heaven's job to fix it. Also not so. He handed you the wood. The perpetual flame is a partnership: His fire, your faithfulness; His kindling, your keeping. You do not create the fire, but you were made to *never let it die.*

## Feed It Before Dawn

So let me leave you where the priest began each day, in the dark, at the altar, with wood in his hands and no audience but the One who lit the flame. Your fire is burning right now because Yahuah once sent it down. Do not let the ordinary nights be the thing that puts it out. When you feel nothing, feed it anyway. When no one is watching, lay the wood on anyway. When the season is grey and the feast is far off, come in the pre-dawn quiet and stir the coals and keep the flame alive one more day.

Because worship was never meant to be a fire you light for the gathering and let go cold on the way home. It was meant to be perpetual, tamid, continual, unquenched, a flame that outlives every feeling and every season because it is fed by faithfulness and kindled by heaven. Keep it burning, beloved. He sent the fire. He is trusting you, in the long ordinary dark, to never let it die.
