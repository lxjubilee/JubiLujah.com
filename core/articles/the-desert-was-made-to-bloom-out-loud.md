We have a picture in our heads of where worship comes from, and it is green.

Worship, we assume, rises from the watered places. From the full life, the answered prayer, the season when the fields are heavy and the family is whole and there is obvious reason to sing. And when we find ourselves in the other kind of country, the dry stretch, the cracked ground, the years that take more than they give. We assume the singing is supposed to stop. Wait for the rain, we tell ourselves. Sing again when things grow again.

Then you read the thirty-fifth chapter of Isaiah, and the prophet has it exactly backwards, and it stops your breath.

Because in his vision, the first thing to rejoice is not the garden. It is the **desert**.

## The ground with the least reason sings first

Isaiah looks out at the *midbar*, the wilderness, the wide empty land where flocks are driven because nothing settles there. He looks at the *arabah*, the desert plain, the sun-hammered flatness where the heat shimmers and nothing green has any business surviving. And he does not say *someday water will reach it.* He says the wilderness and the dry land will be **glad**. The desert will rejoice and blossom. He gives the barren ground a voice and the first thing it does with that voice is shout for joy.

Sit in that reversal. The prophet did not pick the lush hills of the north to carry the praise. He handed the song to the one place that had no natural reason to sing. He made the wasteland the worshipper.

And the blossom he names is a small, tough, low thing, the Hebrew word points to a flower that comes up out of a bulb, and honest translators have argued for centuries over exactly which one, crocus or rose or meadow-saffron, because the plant is old and the word is rare. But you do not need the botany settled to catch the point the prophet is making. It is not a hothouse orchid coddled under glass. It is a hardy little bloom that shoves its way up through ground that had been written off as dead, and it does it *abundantly*, and it does it *with singing*.

## Praise from the places nothing should grow

I know something about a piece of ground everyone had given up on. I have stood on a slope that fire had stripped down to black, the route that used to be gold, the old trees that used to sing in the wind, all of it gone to ash, and I have watched, over slow seasons, the green come back different and still come back. So I will tell you what that kind of country teaches, because Isaiah is teaching the same thing.

**The most powerful praise on earth does not rise from the watered places. It rises from the ones that had no business producing it.**

Anyone can sing when the fields are full. That song is real, but it is easy, and everyone expects it. The song that shakes the heavens is the one that comes up out of the cracked ground, the thanksgiving offered by the woman whose year took everything, the worship lifted on the burned slope over seeds that have not sprouted yet, the shout of joy from the desert plain that has not felt rain in living memory and lifts its voice anyway. That is the blossom the prophet saw. Not praise *because* the water came. Praise that came up first, in the dry, and *called* the water toward it.

> The garden's song says *look what I was given.* The desert's song says *look Who I trust*, and only one of those can be sung with nothing in your hands.

## The wilderness was always where He spoke

There is something the wilderness has always been for in the story of Yahuah's people, and it is worth naming, because it turns the desert from a punishment into a meeting place.

It was in the *midbar* that Israel first learned the Voice, camped in a wasteland with no crops and no cities, fed by bread they could not have grown, led by a fire and a cloud. It was into the wilderness that the prophets went to hear clearly, out where nothing else was speaking. It was in the desert that Yeshua Himself was led before He began, and He came out of it in power. The dry country, again and again, is not where Yahuah abandons His people. It is where He gets them alone and teaches them to listen.

So when Isaiah promises that the wilderness itself will one day rejoice and blossom and sing, he is not just predicting rain. He is saying that the very place you thought was your exile is the place appointed to bloom loudest, because it is the place where nothing competes with His voice, and a heart that learns to worship *there* has learned to worship with nothing propping it up.

The chapter goes on to promise that water will break out in that wilderness, streams in the desert, the burning sand becoming a pool. But notice the order. The gladness comes *before* the water in the prophet's telling. The desert rejoices, and *then* the springs break open. The blossoming heart came first, and the rain followed the singing.

## Sing from where you are, not where you wish you were

So here is the word for whoever is reading this from the dry country, and I mean it with all the rugged tenderness I have.

Do not wait for the green to come back before you lift your voice. That is exactly backwards, and the prophet says so. You are not disqualified from worship by your barrenness. You are *positioned* by it. The cracked ground is not the reason to stay silent; it is the very ground appointed to bloom out loud. The desert does not sing because the water arrived. It sings, and the water comes toward the sound.

If your life is a wilderness this season, if you are looking at flat, hard, unpromising country and cannot see a single reason to sing, then you are standing precisely where the loudest, purest, most heaven-moving praise has always come from. Not the watered valley. The desert plain. The barren land with a voice, throwing its head back, blossoming abundantly, shouting for a joy it has every earthly reason not to have and lifts anyway because it belongs to Yahuah.

The wilderness was made to bloom out loud. So bloom. Sing from the dry ground. And watch what your singing calls up out of the sand.
