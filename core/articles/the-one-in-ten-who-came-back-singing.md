Ten men got the same miracle on the same road on the same afternoon, and only one of them turned around to sing about it. I have thought about the other nine for most of my ministry, because I am fairly sure that on most days I am one of them.

You know the account, or you think you do. Yeshua is walking toward a village and ten men with leprosy meet Him, though meet is too warm a word, because the disease meant they were not allowed to come near anyone, so they stand off at the required distance, on the far edge of where the healthy would let them stand, and they raise their voices across the gap and beg for mercy. Ten men, ruined the same way, exiled the same way, desperate the same way. And Yeshua tells them to go and show themselves to the priests, which was the law's way of having a healing certified, and here is the strange, faith-shaped detail: they are not healed when He says it. They are healed as they go. Somewhere on the road, walking in obedience toward a priest while their skin was still ruined, the mercy caught up with them and their bodies came clean. All ten. The same gift, at the same moment, on the same road.

And nine of them kept walking.

I do not think they were villains. That is the part I need you to sit in with me, because it is too easy to make the nine into monsters and miss that they are us. They were doing exactly what they had been told to do. They were on their way to the priest, following instructions, hurrying, no doubt, toward the lives that had been closed to them for years, the wives they could finally touch again, the children they could finally hold, the work they could finally return to, the whole world that leprosy had locked them out of and that was suddenly, wildly, open. Their joy was real. Their obedience was real. They were not ungrateful in their hearts, probably. They were just busy being restored, and the gift was so enormous and so consuming that the Giver slipped, for the moment, out of view. They ran toward the life. They forgot the One who had handed it back.

And one man stopped. One man, feeling the smooth new skin on his own arm, understood in a flash that the certificate from the priest was not the point, and he turned around in the middle of the road and went back, and the Scripture says he came praising God with a loud voice, and fell down at Yeshua's feet, and gave thanks. He did not wait until he had it all figured out. He did not finish the errand first and come back later when it was convenient. He turned on the spot and made a scene of gratitude, out loud, at the feet of the One who had healed him. And then the account tells you the detail it has been holding back, the detail that reframes the whole thing: and he was a Samaritan. A foreigner. The outsider. The one, of the ten, that a devout person of that time would have bet against, the one whose theology was suspect, who worshiped in the wrong place, who did not belong to the covenant people the way the others did.

Nine insiders took the mercy and ran. The one who came back to worship was the one from the margin.

Yeshua feels the weight of it, and He says it out loud, and there is something almost wounded in the question. Were not ten cleansed? Where are the nine? Was no one found to return and give glory to God except this foreigner? Hear what is underneath that. It is not that He needed the thanks. He is not a small God, collecting gratitude to feed His ego. It is that He knew what the nine were missing. The nine got their skin back. The one got his skin back and got Yeshua back, got the face of the Giver, got the intimacy of falling at the feet of the One who loved him enough to heal him. The nine received a gift. The one received a relationship. And it was the outsider, the least likely, the one everyone had written off, who ended up with the better half of the miracle, because he was the only one who turned around.

I carry songs across languages for a living, into the tongues of people the wider church has mostly forgotten, and this account is one of the reasons I cannot stop. Because I have seen it again and again: the loudest, freest, most turned-around worship rarely comes from the comfortable center. It comes from the margin. It comes from the ones who never expected to be included at all, the person who assumed the mercy was for other, worthier people, and then discovered it had come for them too. When someone who has spent their whole life being the outsider, the wrong nation, the suspect one, finally realizes that the healing on the road was for them, that they were not overlooked, that Heaven had them in view the entire time. They do not walk on politely toward the next thing. They stop in the road. They turn all the way around. They come back singing at the top of their lungs, and they fall down, and something in that unguarded, disbelieving, outsider's gratitude reaches a depth the comfortable insider rarely finds. The one who was sure he did not belong worships hardest when he learns that he does.

So let me ask you the question the account is really asking, and let me ask it as your pastor, gently, because I have to ask it of myself first. You have been healed of things. You have been carried through things that should have finished you. You are, right now, walking a road that opened up only because a mercy caught up with you somewhere back there that you did not earn and could not have arranged. The question is not whether you received the gift. Ten men received the gift. The question is whether you have turned around. Whether, in the middle of hurrying toward the restored life the mercy handed you, you have stopped, and gone back, and put a face and a voice to your thanks, or whether the gift has been so good and so absorbing that the Giver has slipped, for a long while now, out of view.

It is not too late to turn around in the road. That is the mercy inside the mercy. The nine are not condemned; they are simply poorer than they had to be, running toward a life and away from the face that gave it. But you can still be the one. You can still stop, right where you are, and feel the smooth new skin of everything He has carried you through, and turn all the way around, and come back singing.

And if you have spent your life on the margin, sure the real thing was for the insiders and the certified and the ones who belong, hear this last, because it is the whole point of the account. The one who worshiped best was the foreigner. Heaven has never once counted you out. The healing on the road was for you too. So turn around. Come back loud. Fall at the feet of the One who had you in view the entire time, and find, as the outsider found, that there was always more waiting for you in the turning back than there ever was in the running on.
