When we decided to carry our songs into ten languages, someone reasonable asked me the reasonable question. Why ten? Two would reach most of the earth. Add a third and a fourth and you have covered almost everyone who will ever hear you. But the ninth language, the tenth, those belong to a few million people scattered thin across hard country, and some of them will never own a device to play the song on. The math does not close. Why spend the months?

I have a stubborn answer, and it is not a strategy. It is a face I have not met yet.

Somewhere there is a woman who has sat in the back of rooms her whole life while worship happened in a language that was not the one her mother used to sing her to sleep. She can follow along. She has learned the borrowed words the way you learn directions in a city you are only visiting. But she has never once heard the deep thing said in the tongue her heart actually thinks in, the language of her scoldings and her lullabies and the prayers she says when she thinks no one is listening. She has come to assume that God speaks the important languages, the ones with big churches and printed books, and that her small tongue is something He tolerates on the way to the real conversation.

She is wrong about that. And the tenth translation exists to tell her so.

I keep thinking about the story Yeshua told about the shepherd, the one everybody knows and almost nobody lets land. A man has a hundred sheep. One wanders off. And the arithmetic of that story is deliberately outrageous. He leaves the ninety-nine, the whole sensible majority, the ones who are behaving and accounted for, and he goes out into the dark after the single one that is lost. No efficient shepherd does this. You do not risk ninety-nine for one. Unless the one is not a number to you. Unless the one is a name, a face, a particular animal you would know by its cry in a crowded field.

That is the sentence the whole Gospel turns on. To Heaven, the one is never a rounding error.

So the tenth language is not bad math. It is the math of the shepherd. It is leaving the crowded, well-served ninety-nine of the major tongues for a little while, and going out into the thin country after the one who has spent her life assuming she was not worth the trip.

I felt the weight of this most on the day we recorded the pilots, the first songs we ran through the whole translation, tongue by tongue, to see if the thing could even hold. Somewhere around the seventh or eighth language, I stopped understanding the words entirely. I could not tell you what the singer was saying. I only knew, because I had built the song, what she was saying it about. And I sat there with my eyes stinging, undone by a lyric I could not parse, because I understood for the first time what we were actually doing. We were not translating words. We were carrying a welcome across a border, to someone standing on the far side who had stopped expecting anyone to cross.

There is a day in the Scriptures that has haunted the church ever since it happened. The followers of Yeshua were gathered together when the Spirit fell, and they began to speak, and the wonder was not only in the ones speaking. It was in the ones listening. People had come from every country under heaven, and each one heard the great deeds of God spoken in his own language, the one he was born into, the household tongue. They said it out loud, amazed: how is it that we each hear them in our own native speech? Not a translation they had to strain toward. Their own. The first thing the Spirit did when He came in power was to make sure that no one in the crowd got the message secondhand.

That is not an accident of the story. That is the point of it. God did not choose one holy language and make the whole earth climb toward it. He came down into all of them. When Heaven finally spoke to the nations, it spoke to each one in the accent of its own mother.

And that is what I want the woman in the back of the room to know. The tenth translation is Pentecost's echo, made small and stubborn and specific, arriving in her town centuries later. It is God bending down once more to say the same thing He said that day, that He has no intention of making her love Him in a borrowed tongue. He will come the whole way to her. He will say it in the language of her lullabies.

I will be honest about the cost, because I do not want to make it sound easy. The tenth language does not fill a room. It does not trend. When we release it, the numbers are small enough that a strategist would quietly recommend we stop. And I understand the recommendation. But I pastor a small church too, and I know what it is to be the one everyone's math would write off. I know what it does to a person to discover that they were counted, not as a demographic, but as a name someone went out into the dark to find.

Some of the most faithful worship I have ever witnessed happened in rooms of eleven people in languages I will never learn, sung by people the wider church has never heard of and never will. Heaven was not doing arithmetic in those rooms. Heaven was leaning in.

So here is the honest word, whether you are the one who feels unreached or the one wondering whether reaching costs too much. If you have ever sat at the edge of the room and assumed the real thing was happening in someone else's language, some holier vocabulary you were never taught, some fluency other people have and you lack, hear this plainly. The shepherd is not standing with the ninety-nine, glancing at his watch, hoping you find your own way in. He has already left them. He is out in the thin country. He is coming for the one, and He knows your name, and He is not going to say the most important thing of your life in a language you have to translate.

He is coming to say it in the one your mother used.

Sit still long enough to hear the footsteps. That song you thought was for other people, in other tongues, in other, larger rooms. We made the tenth one for you. And the One whose welcome it carries has been on His way to your particular door since long before you learned to doubt you were worth the walk.
