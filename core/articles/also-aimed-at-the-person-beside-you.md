Twice in his letters, Paul writes almost the same sentence about singing, and both times, if you read it slowly, it says something that should stop you cold.

He tells one church to speak *to one another* in psalms and hymns and spiritual songs. He tells another to *teach and admonish one another* with those same three kinds of song. And we breeze past it, because we already know what singing is for. Singing is what you do *toward the front,* toward the throne, toward Yahuah. Up.

But that is not the preposition Paul reaches for. He does not say sing *up.* He says sing *to one another.* Sideways. To the person beside you.

There is a whole dimension of worship hiding in that little phrase, and most of the church has been standing with its back to it.

## Worship has two directions

Let me be careful, because this is easy to get lopsided. Paul does not delete the vertical. In the very same breath he says we sing *making melody in our hearts to the Lord.* The song does go up. Worship is, first and gloriously, addressed to Yahuah, and nothing here touches that.

But Paul refuses to let it be *only* vertical. He describes a song that goes up *and* across at the very same time, a single act of worship that is, simultaneously, offered to God and *ministered to the people around you.* When the congregation sings, something is being given upward to Yahuah and, in the identical motion, something is being handed to the believer at your shoulder.

That is why he can call singing a form of *teaching.* Think about how strange that is if worship were purely a private transaction between you and God. How would your singing *teach* anyone? It only makes sense if the song is also traveling outward, if, when you sing the truth about Yahuah with conviction, the person next to you is being *discipled* by the sound of your faith. We do not usually think of the worship set as the teaching portion of the service. Paul did.

> When you sing, you are not only telling God the truth about himself. You are telling the trembling person beside you the truth they came in doubting. The song is aimed both ways at once.

## The three kinds, and why there are three

Paul names three: *psalms, hymns, and spiritual songs.* I want to be honest with you, scholars have argued for centuries about the precise line between them, and I will not pretend to a certainty the text does not hand us. But the broad shape is discernible, and it is beautiful.

*Psalms* points back to the ancient songbook itself, the words Yahuah's people have been singing for three thousand years, the tested and inspired language of Scripture set to music. When you sing a psalm, you are being handed words older and truer than your own mood.

*Hymns* are songs of praise composed to declare who Yahuah is and what he has done, the great confessions, the doctrine set to melody, the truth a whole congregation can plant its feet on together.

*Spiritual songs* seem to be the freer thing, the Spirit-prompted, in-the-moment overflow, the song that rises fresh in a room and could not have been printed in advance.

And here is why it matters that there are *three* and not one. Because the body is not made of one kind of person. Some of your people are drowning and need the tested, ancient words handed to them because they have none of their own left. Some need the solid confession, the doctrine they can stand on when their feelings have quit. And some need permission for the fresh, overflowing, unrepeatable song of a heart that has just been met by God. A congregation that only ever sings one of the three is feeding only one kind of hunger. The instruction to sing *all three* is a mercy for the whole variety of the gathered body.

## Facing the body, not only the front

Here is the picture I cannot shake since I began to see it.

We have built our rooms so that everyone faces forward. Rows of backs. Each worshipper alone with the screen, alone with the band, alone with their own private feeling, a hundred solos happening to occur in the same room at the same time. And it can be genuinely sweet. But it is only *half* of what Paul described, because in that arrangement no one is singing *to one another.* We have kept the vertical and quietly amputated the horizontal.

Imagine the older way. A room where you can *see* each other. Where when you sing *he is faithful,* the widow three seats over, who is not at all sure this morning that he is, can *hear the conviction in your voice* and borrow it. Where the young believer watches a grey-haired saint sing a hard line through tears and learns, without a single word of instruction, that faith survives the thing they are afraid of. Where your song is not only expression but *encouragement,* literally putting courage into the person who came in empty.

That is what Paul means by *teaching and admonishing one another.* Not from a lectern. From the next chair. The most powerful sermon many of your people will receive this week is not the one preached at them. It is the sound of the whole church around them refusing to stop singing the truth, and them realizing, in the middle of it, that they are being carried by a room that believes.

## Turn a little

You do not have to rearrange the furniture to obey this. You have to change what you *know you are doing* when you open your mouth.

The next time you sing in a gathered room, remember that the song has two addresses. Yes, lift it to Yahuah, first, highest, always. But know, at the very same time, that it is landing on the person beside you. So sing it like you mean for them to hear it. Sing the faithful lines with your whole conviction, because someone within earshot cannot find theirs and is about to be handed yours. Let your face be seen. Let the tears show. You are not performing; you are *ministering,* in the exact way Paul said congregational song was built to minister, sideways, to one another, teaching and strengthening a body that cannot always tell itself the truth alone.

And if you are the empty one this morning, if you came in unable to sing a word of it, then do the humble, worshipful thing: stand in the middle of the singing body and *let it be aimed at you.* Let the room disciple you back to faith. That, too, is the design. Some days you are the voice; some days you are the one being sung over. The song was always meant to travel between us.

So turn a little toward the person next to you. Heaven is not only above the room. It is also standing at your shoulder, singing.
