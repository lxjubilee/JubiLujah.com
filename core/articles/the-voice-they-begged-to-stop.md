They had walked out of slavery asking to hear from God. And on the morning He finally spoke to them with His own voice, they could not get away from the mountain fast enough.

Picture the third day at the foot of Sinai, because Scripture spends real ink making sure we can. In the morning there was thunder and lightning and a thick cloud on the mountain, and a trumpet blast so loud that everyone in the camp trembled where they stood. Moses brought the people out to meet God, and they took their places at the base of the mountain, and the whole of it was wrapped in smoke because Yahuah had descended on it in fire. The smoke went up like the smoke of a furnace, and the entire mountain shook violently. And the trumpet, this is the detail I cannot get past, the trumpet did not fade. It grew louder and louder and louder. Whatever was sounding on that mountain was not winding down toward something manageable. It was building toward something they were not sure they could survive.

And then God spoke. Not through a prophet, not in a whisper to one man on the back side of the wilderness, but out loud, to all of them at once, the ten words, the covenant, the voice of the living Elohim rolling down the slope in thunder over a nation of freed slaves. This was the thing they had wanted. This was intimacy on a scale no people before or since has been offered: God addressing an entire congregation directly, holding nothing behind a veil, speaking so they could all hear.

And they could not bear it. The account says the people saw the thunder and the flashes and the trumpet and the mountain smoking, and they drew back and stood far off, and they said to Moses. You speak to us, and we will listen; but do not let God speak to us anymore, or we will die. Do you feel the weight of that? The most direct word God had ever given a people, and the people's response was, please make it stop. Put someone between us and that Voice. We asked to hear from Him, and now that we have, we find we cannot stand in front of it, and we would rather have a mediator than the mountain.

I want to be careful here, because there are two things happening in that plea and we have to hold them both.

The first is that their trembling was right. It was accurate. They were not being faithless when they shook; they were telling the truth about the difference between the God on the mountain and the men at its foot. Moses said as much to them: do not be afraid, for God has come to test you, so that the fear of Him would be before you and keep you from sin. He did not scold them for trembling. The trembling was the beginning of wisdom, exactly as the wise ones would later say. A people who could stand at the smoking foot of Sinai with their arms crossed, unmoved, casually chatting through the voice of God, would not have been more mature than the ones who shook. They would have been more dead. The reverence was not the problem. The reverence was the only honest thing in the valley.

But there is a second thing in the plea, and it is the one I want to sit us down in front of, because it is the temptation of every worshipper in every age. Underneath the honest trembling there was also a quiet retreat. They did not only fear the Voice. They wanted distance from it. They wanted to keep loving God at a volume they could control, to have Him near enough to rescue them but far enough that His speaking would never again shake them like that. And so they backed away from the mountain and asked for a middleman, not only so they could bear the holiness, which was fair, but so they would never have to be that undone again.

We do the same thing, and we have gotten very sophisticated about it. We say we want a God who speaks to us, who is real and present and personal, and then we arrange our worship so that He never actually says anything we did not expect. We keep Him at a manageable volume. We build the room so the Voice can only ever come to us softened, filtered, run through enough production that it will comfort us but never make the doorposts shake. We want the warmth of the mountain without the fire of it. And the tragedy is not that we are too reverent. It is that, like the people in the valley, we have used a real reverence as cover for a real retreat, backing away from the very Voice we claimed we came to hear.

Now let me tell you where all of this was always going, because Sinai is not the end of the story and it was never meant to be.

Centuries later, a letter to the scattered believers picks up this exact mountain and does something breathtaking with it. It says: you have not come to a mountain that can be touched, that burns with fire, to darkness and gloom and a trumpet blast and a voice whose words made the hearers beg that no more be spoken to them. You have not come to Sinai. You have come to Mount Zion, to the city of the living God, to countless angels in joyful assembly, and to Yeshua the mediator of a new covenant, whose blood speaks a better word. Read that slowly, because it does not do what we expect. It does not tell us the fire has been put out. It does not say the mountain has cooled and God has grown safe and small. The very same letter warns, a breath later, that our God is still a consuming fire. What has changed is not the holiness. What has changed is that there is now a Mediator who lets us come all the way up.

That is the answer to the people's plea, and it is far better than the thing they asked for. They wanted distance so they would not die. Yeshua gives us nearness that will not kill us. He stands in the place they were begging for a middleman to fill, and He does it so well that we are invited not to stand far off in the valley but to come boldly, right up onto the mountain, into the presence of the same God whose voice once made a nation shake. The trembling was never meant to end in retreat. It was meant to end in a Mediator, and through Him, in the drawing near the people in the valley could not yet imagine.

So do not ask the Voice to stop. That is the old mistake, and we make it in newer, quieter ways than they did. When your worship starts to shake you, when a line lands with a weight you did not schedule, when the presence gets heavier than the production, when you feel for a moment that the ground has gone different under you, do not reach for the volume knob. Do not back away from the mountain. Ask instead for the One who lets you stand on it. Because the fire is still burning up there, and the trumpet is still growing louder, and the God who spoke at Sinai has not lost His voice. He has only, in Yeshua, made a way for you to hear it and live, trembling and unafraid at once, close enough to be spoken to, and never again begging Him to stop.
