The best voice in that little country church belongs to nobody you'd put on a stage.

He's an old farmer in the third pew, and he is loud, and he is wrong about half the notes. He comes in early on the choruses and late on the verses. His hands are the size of dinner plates and they don't lift so much as hang there, open, like he's catching rain. And every soul in that room, if you asked them honestly, would tell you the same thing: when he sings, something in the building gets more true.

Nobody taught him to sing. Somebody clearly should have. And I would not change one flat, cracked, half-a-beat-behind note of it for the finest voice in the state.

Here is what I want to say plain, because plainness is the only thing that ever gets believed in a room like that: **God is not grading your pitch.**

We have gotten something backwards, and it has quietly kept a lot of good people standing in the back with their mouths shut. Somewhere along the way worship got handed to the professionals. The lights, the mix, the ones who can actually sing, and everybody else became an audience. So now a whole lot of folks stand in a service and *watch* worship happen the way you'd watch a concert, mouthing along, careful not to be heard, certain that their voice would only mess up a nice sound.

And the tragedy of it is that the careful, silent, in-tune non-singing they're offering is worth less to Heaven than the old farmer's glorious wreck of a melody. Because his is *true*, and theirs is a performance of not-performing.

Think about what worship actually is for a minute. It is not a concert you attend. It is not a product the talented make and the rest consume. It is a family singing at the supper table. And no family that ever loved each other sang in tune. You've heard it, Happy Birthday around a cake, twelve people in six different keys, the uncle a full measure behind, and not one soul in that kitchen thinking *this needs better production.* It is the most out-of-tune song in the world and it is one of the truest, because nobody is trying to sound good. They're just trying to love the person in front of the cake.

That is the sound Heaven leans toward.

Read the old songs and you'll find they were never precious about polish. The instruction is *make a joyful noise*, a **noise**, mind you, not a flawless performance. The people are told to clap, to shout, to bang the tambourine, to dance until the dignified are embarrassed. A king once danced through the streets with everything in him, undignified and glad, and when his wife sneered that he'd made a fool of himself in front of the servant girls, he told her plainly he'd be more undignified than *that.* He was not on key. He was on fire. There is a difference, and Heaven has never once confused the two.

Now here is the tender part, the part I most want the one hanging back to hear.

The reason your voice shakes is probably not that it's bad. It's that it's *honest*. A cracked note is what comes out when the feeling is bigger than the skill, when there's more in your chest than your throat knows how to carry. That is not a flaw in your worship. That is the sound of a real heart exceeding its own technique. A perfect voice can sail clean across a room and never once leave the throat it came from. A cracked one leaves the throat because something behind it *pushed.* Guess which one God is listening for.

He is not standing at the front of the room wincing at the flat notes like a judge with a scorecard. If He's anywhere, He's down in the third pew next to the loud old farmer, glad, because that man stopped worrying years ago about how he sounds and started just *meaning it*, and meaning it is the whole thing. Meaning it is what worship is made of. Pitch is what performance is made of. Do not bring Him a performance. He never asked for one and He can hear straight through it.

So let me say the freeing word out loud, the one that ought to get somebody's mouth open this Sunday: **you are allowed to sound bad.** You are allowed to come in on the wrong beat. You are allowed to lose the tune halfway through and hum the part you forgot. None of that lowers the worth of your worship by one ounce, because the worth was never in the sound. It was in the direction your heart was facing when you opened your mouth.

The tidy, careful, in-tune silence you've been offering to protect the nice sound, set it down. It has kept you an audience your whole life, and you were never meant to be the audience. You were meant to be in the family, at the table, singing badly and gladly with everybody else.

So this week, when the music starts and that old fear puts its hand over your mouth, do the brave, out-of-tune thing. Let one true note out. Then another. Sing it wrong. Sing it loud. Let your voice crack right on the word you mean the most, and don't you dare apologize for it.

Because the best worship in the room is almost never the most beautiful. It's the most true. And true has never once needed to be on key.

Take the long way home and sing the whole way, badly, if you have to. He's been waiting to hear it, and He is not grading.
