There is a moment in the wilderness wanderings that most people read straight past, and it is one of the only places in all of Scripture where the text stops the story to hand you the actual song.

The people of Israel had been in the desert a long time by then. They had complained, they had wandered, they had buried a whole generation in the sand. And they came to a place the account simply calls Beer, which is the Hebrew word for *well*. It was there that Yahuah said to Moses: gather the people together, and I will give them water. And then the text says something that ought to make anyone who leads worship sit up straight. It says: *then Israel sang this song.* And it gives you the words. Spring up, O well, sing to it, the well that the leaders dug, that the nobles of the people hollowed out with the scepter and with their staffs.

I want you to picture the scene precisely, because everything is in the details. A worn-out desert people, gathered around a hole in the ground. The leaders down in the dirt, digging, not with shovels and machines, but with the very staffs of their office, the walking sticks that marked them as princes, driven into the sand like tools. And the whole crowd around them, not silent, not grumbling for once, but *singing to the well.* Singing at it. Singing water up out of the dry ground.

## Worship over the empty place

Now think about the order of things, because the order is the whole lesson.

They did not wait until the water came and then throw a celebration. That is how we usually do it. We keep our praise in reserve until the provision arrives, and then, once the well is full and we can see it and touch it and drink from it, we sing our thank-you. That is real, and it is good, and there is a place for it. But it is not what happened at Beer. At Beer, the singing came *while* the ground was still dry and the leaders were still digging. The song was not a response to the water. The song was sung over the absence of water, into a hole that had not yet given anything, as an act of expectation. They were singing to summon what they could not yet see.

I have spent enough of my life in high, dry, wilderness places to tell you this is one of the hardest kinds of worship there is, and one of the most important. It is easy to sing over a full well. Anyone can rejoice at a spring that is already flowing. But to gather around the dry place, the marriage that has gone quiet, the body that will not heal, the calling that has borne nothing yet, the child who has not come home, to gather around the dry hole and *sing to it*, sing over it, sing as though water were on its way when there is no evidence in the sand that it is, that takes a faith the comfortable never have to develop. That is desert worship. And the desert is the only place it can be learned.

Notice, too, that Yahuah had already spoken. He said, *I will give them water.* The song was not a technique to twist God's arm; the song was the people agreeing with a promise already made. That is the difference between anxious wishing and worship. Worship over the empty well is not you trying to force something out of God by the force of your singing. It is you taking a word He has already given and setting it to music before it comes true, so that your heart lives in the promise while your eyes still see only sand.

## They dug it together

And do not miss the other thing, because it is easy to miss. *The well that the leaders dug, that the nobles hollowed out with their staffs.* This was not a miracle that dropped from the sky onto passive spectators. The people sang, and the leaders dug, and both were happening at once.

There is a false spirituality that says if God is going to provide, then I should sit still and wait and touch nothing, and any effort of mine only gets in the way of grace. And there is an opposite lie that says it is all up to my effort, my strategy, my sweat, and God helps those who help themselves. The well at Beer refuses both. The song rose *and* the staffs went into the ground. The people worshiped over the dry place *and* the leaders got down in the dirt and dug. The provision was God's. He said plainly, *I will give them water*, and yet He gave it through singing people and digging hands, and He did not seem to think the digging insulted the gift.

This is how it almost always works in the high country of faith. You sing over the dry place, and you also pick up the staff and dig. You worship expectantly, and you also do the next faithful thing in front of you. You go to the appointment, you make the call, you keep showing up, you drive the plain ordinary tool of your obedience into the ground where you are believing for water. And somewhere down in that dark, out of your sight, the source God had already placed there begins, at last, to rise toward the light.

And it was *together.* The leaders did not dig a private well for themselves. The nobles hollowed it out for the whole thirsty nation, and the whole nation sang over it as one. Nobody in that crowd was drawing water alone. That is the church at its best in a dry season, not every person frantically digging their own hole in isolation, but a people gathered around the same empty place, some singing when others have no voice left, some digging when others have no strength left, all of them believing for the same water, all of them about to drink from the same well.

## For the one standing over a dry well

So let me speak to whoever is standing over an empty place right now, exhausted, with no water in sight.

You have prayed over it for a long time and nothing has risen. The ground is dry and it stays dry, and you have started to go quiet, because it feels foolish to keep singing over a hole that gives you nothing back. The silence feels more honest than the song. And a voice keeps telling you that when there is finally something to celebrate, then you can worship, but not before, not over sand, not over nothing.

Hear me: sing over it now. Not because pretending will make it so, and not to perform a happiness you do not feel, but because Yahuah has spoken over your desert the same kind of word He spoke over Beer, and worship is simply the sound of a heart that has decided to trust the word before it sees the water. Sing to the dry well. And then pick up your staff, the plain, ordinary tool of your next act of obedience, and put it in the ground, and dig where you are, and do not despise how unmagical it looks. The well the leaders dug with their walking sticks still gave a whole nation water.

The source was already there. Yahuah had placed it under that dry ground before the people ever arrived, and He placed it under yours too. Your singing does not manufacture the water. Your singing calls it up, agrees with the God who promised it, and lives inside the promise while the ground is still bare. So gather at the dry place with whoever will stand there with you, and lift the song the desert people lifted, and dig, and wait, and watch.

Spring up, O well. It is already on its way to the surface.
