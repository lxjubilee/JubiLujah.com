There is a moment at the Passover table that would have shocked me if I had grown up outside it.

The instruction, at the meal where a whole people remembers being slaves, is not "sit up straight." It is not "be respectful." It is, of all things: **lean.** Recline. Sprawl back against the cushions. Get comfortable. And not just the head of the house, not just the honored guest, *everyone.* The tradition is emphatic and almost defiant about this: even the poorest man in Israel, even a servant, even the smallest child at the table, must recline. Nobody eats this meal sitting bolt upright. Everybody leans.

Let me name the thing honestly, the way I am bound to. The custom of reclining at the Seder is **tradition**. It is written into the old rabbinic teaching about how to keep the meal, not spelled out as a command in the Torah itself. But it is not a random tradition. It is one of those customs that reaches down and grabs the deepest truth of the whole night and puts it in your *body.* Because there is a reason free people lean. And there is a reason it undoes me that they make the servant lean too.

## Why leaning meant everything

You have to understand what reclining meant in the ancient world to feel the weight of it.

In the world where this custom took shape, the way you ate told everyone exactly who you were. Slaves and servants **stood.** They stood at the edge of the room, ready to run, ready to fetch, ready to serve. You do not sit down when your body belongs to someone else. And the free, the noble, the master of the house, *they* reclined. They stretched out on cushions, propped on one elbow, at ease, in no hurry, because a free man does not have to be ready to jump. His time is his own. His body is his own. Reclining was the physical signature of freedom. It was the posture of a person who is not afraid of being told to get up.

So when the tradition says *everyone leans tonight*, even the servant, even the poorest, even the child. It is staging a small, glorious revolution at the dinner table. For this one meal, the man who stands and serves all year is told: not tonight. Tonight you lean. Tonight you take up the posture that says *I am free, I belong here, my time is my own.* The people at that table spent generations being told to stand at the edge and be ready to run. And the meal that celebrates their rescue orders them, with tender insistence, to *stop standing.* To lie back. To take the posture of the redeemed.

## The question the child asks

There is a beautiful thing built into the Seder about this very posture.

Part of the tradition of the night is that the youngest child asks a set of questions, the "why is this night different" questions, and one of them is exactly this: *why is it that on all other nights we eat sitting or reclining however we please, but on this night we all recline?* The child names the strangeness of it out loud. Why is everybody leaning tonight?

And the answer the night gives is the whole point of Passover: **because we were slaves, and now we are free.** We lean because free people lean. We recline because the God who brought us out did not bring us out to make us slightly-more-comfortable slaves. He brought us out to make us sons and daughters, people with a place at the table who do not have to stand ready to bolt. The posture *is* the theology. You cannot lean back in ease and still believe, in your body, that you are on the edge of the room waiting to be dismissed. The leaning tells your own muscles the truth your fear keeps trying to forget.

> Fear stands at the edge of the room, always ready to be sent away. The redeemed lean back into the middle of the table, because they finally believe they are allowed to stay.

## The night He leaned with them

Now let me take you to the table I cannot stop thinking about.

When Yeshua kept the Passover with His friends on the last night, the meal we call the Last Supper, the accounts tell us something we skim right past in our paintings and our imaginations. They did not sit at that table on chairs, upright and formal, the way the famous pictures show them. They **reclined.** The word used for how they were at the table is the word for leaning back, stretched out on the low couches around it. This is why, on that very night, the disciple Yeshua loved could lean his head back against Yeshua's chest to ask Him a question. You cannot do that sitting in a chair. You can only do that when you are already reclining beside Him, close, at ease, leaning.

Think about what that means. On the night He was betrayed, on the night the whole weight of the world's rescue was about to fall on His shoulders, Yeshua and His friends took the posture of *free people.* They leaned. And one of them was so unafraid, so at home, so certain of his welcome, that he let his head fall back onto the Lord's own chest. That is the posture Yeshua invites His people into. Not standing at the edge, anxious, ready to be dismissed. *Leaning*, close enough to feel Him breathe.

And here is the wonder of it: He was about to become the servant. On that same reclining night, He got up from the leaning and took a towel and washed feet like the lowest slave in the house, and then He went out and poured His whole life out so that *we* could stop standing at the edge forever. He took the servant's place so that the servant could take the free man's cushion. He stood and served so that you could lean.

## Come and lean

So here is what the reclining puts into my very back, and what I want it to put into yours.

A great many of God's own children are still eating at His table standing up. I do not mean literally. I mean in their spirit. They are at the meal, they are technically invited, but they are perched on the edge of the chair, ready to be told they do not really belong here, braced for the moment somebody notices they snuck in. They serve and serve because they are secretly sure their welcome depends on it. They have been rescued out of Egypt and they are still standing like slaves.

Mi gente, **lean back.** The whole scandalous instruction of the night is aimed straight at you: even the poorest servant reclines tonight. Not because you earned the cushion. You did not, none of us did, but because the Master got up and took the servant's towel so that you could take His place at ease. Your time is your own now. Your body is your own now. You are not on the edge of the room. You are in the middle of the table, and there is a chest to lean your tired head against, close enough to feel Him breathe.

You are not a slave who got a nice meal. You are a free child of the house. So do the bravest, most believing thing there is to do at the table of a God who set you free.

Stop standing. Lean.
