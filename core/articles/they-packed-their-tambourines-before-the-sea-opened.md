There is a detail in the crossing of the sea that I cannot stop turning over in my hands, and it is not the wall of water and it is not the drowned army. It is the tambourines.

You know the story, or you think you do. A nation of slaves, four hundred years deep in someone else's economy, is brought out in a single night. They flee toward the wilderness and hit the edge of the sea, and behind them the dust rises because the empire has changed its mind and sent its chariots to bring them back. They are trapped between the water and the wheels, and they do what trapped people do. They panic, they accuse, they wish they had never left. And then the sea opens. They walk through on dry ground with the water standing up on either side like it is holding its breath, and when the last of them reaches the far bank the water comes down and the pursuit is over. Forever.

And on that far shore, wet and stunned and free, they sing. It is the first full song recorded in the Scriptures, not a psalm David wrote in a cave centuries later, but this, the song at the sea, sung by people who had never in their lives owned a free morning. Moses leads the men. And then Miriam, his sister, takes a tambourine in her hand, and all the women go out after her with tambourines and with dancing, and the whole far shore turns into the loudest celebration the world had ever heard.

Here is the detail. Where did the tambourines come from?

## What you carry out of the house of bondage

Scripture does not stop to explain it. It simply tells us Miriam had a tambourine, and the women had tambourines, enough of them to arm a dancing multitude on a beach in the middle of a wilderness. But think about where these women had been the day before. They had been slaves in Egypt. They had left in the middle of the night, in a hurry so great that their bread had no time to rise and they baked it flat on their backs in the desert. They packed what mattered and they ran.

And in among the things that mattered, the flat bread, the borrowed silver, the children, the little that a slave is allowed to own, these women packed their tambourines.

I want you to feel the weight of that. A tambourine is not survival gear. It does not feed a child or carry water or defend against a chariot. It is an instrument of celebration, and celebration was the one thing these women had the least evidence they were about to receive. They were leaving the only life they had known, walking into a desert with an army at their heels, and somewhere in the packing a woman looked at her tambourine and decided it was coming. She was still a slave when she made that decision. The sea had not opened. There was no far shore yet, no drowned army, no proof. There was only a rumor of a God who had heard, and a woman who chose to pack the instrument of a party she had not yet been invited to.

That is faith. Not the feeling that everything will work out. Faith is packing the tambourine while you are still in the house of bondage, because you have decided that the God who is bringing you out is worth being ready to celebrate.

## The song only the freed can sing

There is a particular sound a person makes when they cross from slavery into freedom, and it cannot be manufactured by anyone standing on this side of the water who has never made the crossing.

The song at the sea is not a polite song. It is not restrained. It is the roar of people who have just watched the thing that owned them go down into the deep and not come back up, and there is a wildness in it that makes some of us, raised on quiet religion, a little uncomfortable. But that wildness is the whole point. You do not thank a God for a deliverance you did not need. The volume of the song is a measure of the depth of the chains. These women were not being excessive. They were being accurate. When you have been a slave for four hundred years and you are suddenly, impossibly, free, a whisper would be a lie. Only the dancing tells the truth.

This is why, all through the story that follows, the memory of that morning becomes the anchor for everything. When the people forget who they are, the word that calls them back is always the same: remember that you were slaves in Egypt, and Yahuah brought you out. Not remember the rules. Remember the crossing. Remember the water standing up. Remember the tambourines on the far shore. The whole identity of a free people is built on a song they sang the day they got out.

## The far shore is a real place

I preach this because I know what side of the water most people feel they are on.

Maybe you are still in the middle of it, between the water and the wheels, the old thing you were trying to escape roaring up behind you and no visible way forward, and everything in you is doing what the people did that day, which is accusing and despairing and wishing you had never tried to leave. If that is you, hear the oldest instruction in the story: stand still, and watch. The sea has opened for people more trapped than you. It is the specialty of the God I serve. He does not part the water when you have figured out how to swim. He parts it when there is no other way through, because a deliverance you could have arranged yourself would never have produced a song like the one at the sea.

And maybe you are already across. Maybe the thing that owned you is behind you now, gone down into the deep, and you have been standing on the far shore for a while, free, technically, on paper, but you have not sung. The tambourine is still packed. You have not let yourself believe that the celebration is really yours, that a freed person is allowed to make that much noise, that the God who brought you out actually wants the dancing and not just the gratitude.

Unpack the tambourine. That is the whole message. The women did not wait until the wilderness ahead of them looked safe. They did not wait until they understood how the water had done what it did. They looked back at the settling sea, and forward at the unknown desert, and they chose the shore in between to throw the loudest party a hungry, homeless, newly-free people ever threw, because the crossing itself was reason enough, and it always is.

Yeshua opened a deeper sea than the one at Egypt, and led a longer captivity out through it, and there is a far shore with your name on it where the water is already settling behind you. You do not have to earn the song. You only have to unpack what you have been carrying since the house of bondage, take it in your hand the way Miriam took hers, and let the free people do the one thing that slaves were never allowed to do.

Dance.
