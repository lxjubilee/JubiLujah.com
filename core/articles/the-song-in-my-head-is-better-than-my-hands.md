I have never once written down the song I actually heard.

This is the private grief of every person who makes things, and I might as well confess it plainly. Before I pick up an instrument, before a single note is committed to anything, there is a moment where I hear the song whole, not the words yet, but the thing itself, its shape and its glory, complete and perfect and almost unbearably beautiful in the space behind my eyes. And then I reach for my hands. And my hands are not that good. What comes out is a translation, a rough copy, a nearest-available approximation of a thing that was flawless a second ago and is now smudged by the limits of my fingers and my craft and my ear.

Every finished song I own is, in this exact sense, a disappointment. Not because it is bad, some of them I am even proud of, but because I heard the true version first, and I know what got lost in the crossing from the hearing to the making. There is always a gap. There is always a distance between the song in my head and the song in my hands, and I have never once closed it.

For years I read that gap as failure. If I were a better writer, I told myself, the copy would match the original. The gap was the measure of my inadequacy, and someday, with enough skill, I would eliminate it. I no longer believe that. I have come to think the gap is not a flaw in me at all. I think it is the most honest and important thing about the whole business of making, and I understood why the day I really looked at a man standing on a mountain being handed a blueprint.

## Make it according to the pattern

When Yahuah called Moses up the mountain to receive the plan for the tabernacle, the tent where the presence of God would meet the people. He did not simply hand over a list of instructions. He showed Moses something. And then, more than once, He gave a peculiar and specific command: see that you make everything according to the pattern that was shown you on the mountain.

I cannot stop thinking about the position that put Moses in. He was shown a pattern, a true, heavenly, complete original, and then sent back down to build a copy of it out of goat hair and acacia wood and hammered gold, using the hands of craftsmen who had never seen what he had seen. Moses carried the glimpse. The workers carried the tools. And every single thing they built was, by definition, a shadow of a pattern that existed in full somewhere they could not reach.

The letter to the Hebrews says exactly this about that tabernacle later, that the priests were serving a copy and shadow of the heavenly things, built according to the pattern shown on the mountain. A copy. A shadow. Not the real thing. The realest thing was the pattern Moses glimpsed, and everything human hands assembled down below was a faithful, gorgeous, deliberately imperfect attempt to point back up at it.

And I realized, reading that, that this is not a description of failure. This is a description of how making is supposed to work when the maker has been shown something greater than the maker's hands.

## The gap is proof, not shame

Here is what changed for me. I had assumed the gap between the song I heard and the song I built was evidence that the beautiful version did not really exist, that I had imagined a glory I could not deliver, and the shortfall exposed the fantasy.

But Moses's story says the opposite. The gap between the pattern and the tabernacle was not proof that the pattern was imaginary. It was proof that the pattern was real. The very fact that the copy fell short is what tells you there was an original to fall short of. If the tent had perfectly captured heaven, the tent would be heaven, and it plainly was not. The shortfall was the honest confession, built right into the architecture, that the true thing was higher than the hands.

So the disappointment I feel looking at my finished songs is not the disappointment of a liar caught inventing a beauty that was never there. It is the ache of a copyist who has actually seen the pattern. I heard something true. My hands could not fully carry it. The distance between the two is not the measure of my fraudulence. It is the measure of the glory of the thing I glimpsed, a glory real enough to be missed.

That reframes the whole enterprise. My job was never to close the gap. My job was to be faithful to the pattern, to build the truest copy my hands can manage and let the shortfall stand as a kind of pointing finger, an honest arrow aimed back up at the original I was shown. A tabernacle that pretended to be heaven would be an idol. A tabernacle that humbly confessed it was only a copy became the very place where the presence came down.

## What the ache is telling you

I think you know this ache even if you have never written a note. It is the gap between the person you long to be and the person you can actually manage to be on a Tuesday. It is the distance between the love you feel and the clumsy way it comes out of your hands. It is the marriage, the parenting, the ministry, the faithful life that always falls short of the shining thing you glimpsed when you first said yes. You heard a pattern. Your hands keep building a shadow of it. And the enemy of your soul would love for you to read that gap as proof that the pattern was a lie.

It is not a lie. The ache is the evidence that you have seen something true and high. The dissatisfaction is not the voice of your failure. It is the homesickness of a copyist who remembers the pattern on the mountain.

> The distance between what you glimpsed and what you built is not the measure of your fraud. It is the measure of the glory you were shown.

And there is a mercy folded into all of this that I hold onto hardest. The pattern still exists. It exists in full, untouched by my limited hands, kept whole in the place it was shown from. Everything I build down here is a shadow, but a shadow requires a light and a solid thing casting it. One day, the letter to the Hebrews insists, the copies give way to the reality; the shadow is swallowed by the substance it was always pointing at. The song I heard and could not quite play is not lost because I played it badly. It is safe in the pattern. And I have staked my life on the belief that on the far side of everything, I will finally hear the original, whole, exactly as beautiful as it was in the moment before I reached for my inadequate, faithful, glory-haunted hands.

So build the truest copy you can. Let the gap stand and stop calling it shame. And let the ache do the one thing it was made to do, turn your face back up the mountain, toward the pattern that was always more real than your hands.
