There is a woman I want you to meet, though you will have to imagine her, because she lived and died before you were born and she never once thought of herself as important.

She is bent over a crib in a small kitchen in a country I could name in three different languages and you would still not have heard of the village. It is late. The baby will not settle. And so she does the thing her own grandmother did, and her grandmother before that. She hums. Low, in the mother tongue, a melody older than anyone can trace, with words about the faithfulness of God that the baby is far too small to understand.

The child will grow up and move away. The child will learn other languages, live in other cities, half-forget the old tongue until only fragments remain, a phrase, a blessing, and that melody. Especially that melody. Decades later, in a hospital corridor or a sleepless midnight of her own, that grown child will find the tune rising in her without permission, and she will not be able to tell you what half the words mean, but she will feel, in her body, that she is not alone and that God is good.

That is a woman who handed down her faith in a song, and the song did what she could not do herself: it crossed the border, it survived the forgetting, and it showed up in the dark decades after she was gone.

I want to talk about that kind of inheritance, because I think it is one of the most powerful things Yahuah does among His people, and most of us received it without noticing.

## Faith arrives before understanding

There is a line in one of Paul's letters that undoes me every time. Writing to young Timothy, he remembers the faith in him and traces it backward. It lived first, he says, in your grandmother Lois, and then in your mother Eunice, and now I am convinced it lives in you.

Three generations, named. A grandmother, a mother, a son. Faith handed down a family line like an heirloom, arriving in the boy long before he could have argued his way to it or defended a single doctrine.

We forget that this is how faith usually comes. We picture conversion as an adult, alone, reasoning toward God and deciding. And sometimes it is. But far more often, faith reaches us the way that melody reached the child in the crib, carried by someone who loved us, planted before we could understand it, humming in us for years before we ever chose it for ourselves.

You did not start your faith from zero. Somebody sang over you first. Maybe not a grandmother, maybe a Sunday school teacher, a neighbor, a stranger on a bus, a recording that played in a house you happened to be in. But someone put a tune in you before you had the words for it, and Ruach HaKodesh has been using that tune to call you home ever since.

## Why the melody outlasts the meaning

Here is what I have learned making music for people across English, Korean, and Romanian, three tongues that share almost no words and one enormous hope.

The words are the first thing to fall away. Translate a song across a border and something is always lost; ask anyone who has tried to carry a hymn from one language into another. But the *melody* travels intact. The tune that a grandmother in one country hums over a crib can move a heart in a country she never visited, in a century she never saw, and the listener does not need to parse a single word to feel the thing underneath it.

That is not a small trick. That is a mercy built into how Yahuah made us.

Because faith is not, at bottom, a set of sentences you agree to. It is a trust you feel in your body toward a God you cannot see. And a melody reaches that place, the pre-verbal place, the place beneath argument, in a way a lecture never will. When the grandmother hummed, she was not teaching the baby propositions. She was tuning the baby's *nervous system* to the frequency of hope. She was writing courage into a body too young for language, so that when the hard nights came, the courage would already be there, wordless and waiting.

This is why the faith survives the forgetting. You can lose the mother tongue and keep the tune. You can lose the theology you were taught and still, in the crisis, find yourself humming your way back to a God you half-remember. The song is a smuggler. It carries the most important thing across every border the words cannot cross.

## What you are handing down right now

Now let me turn this toward you, because you are not only an heir. You are an ancestor in the making.

There are people watching how you worship who will not remember a single thing you say to them. They will not remember your arguments for the faith or your opinions on the issues of the day. But they will remember the *sound* you made when life got hard. They will remember whether the God you sang about at the good table was still the God you sang to at the bad one. Children are recording the melody long before they can follow the words, the tone of your trust, the key your heart lives in under pressure.

That is a sobering thing and a glorious one. It means the most important discipleship you will ever do may happen while you think nothing is happening at all, humming in a kitchen, singing in a car with a little one strapped in the back, worshiping through a season so dark you are certain no one is watching. Someone is always in earshot. And the faith you carry under pressure is being handed to them in a form that will outlast your name.

## The honest word, and the invitation

Let me be a truthful prophet before I close.

Not every song gets received. Some of you sang over children who walked away, prayed over a family line that seems to have gone quiet, hummed hope over a crib and watched that child grow into a stranger to the faith. And I will not stand here and promise you that the melody always lands in the lifetime you can see. The grandmother in my story died before she knew whether her tune took root. Most of the faithful do. We plant songs we do not get to hear sung back.

But hear me: a song planted is never wasted, even when it seems to fall silent. Yahuah keeps every melody sown in love, and some of them surface forty years after the one who sang them is gone, in a hospital corridor, in a sleepless midnight, in a heart that thought it had forgotten everything and finds, to its astonishment, that it still knows the tune.

So sing over the ones in your life anyway. Hum the goodness of God across every border you can reach, of language, of generation, of the years you will not live to see. You do not have to be understood to be planting. You do not have to be remembered by name to be faithful.

Somewhere down the line, in the dark, someone will start to hum, and they will not know all the words, and they will feel, without being able to say why, that God is good and they are not alone.

That will be you, still singing.
