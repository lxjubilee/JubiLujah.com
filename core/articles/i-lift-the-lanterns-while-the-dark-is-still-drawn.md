There is an hour in my part of the world that belongs to almost no one. It comes before the muezzin of the day's busyness calls anyone to anything, before the souk unlatches its shutters, before the village smells the first bread. The courtyard is still dark. The lantern wicks are still smoking from the night. And into that hour, if you are willing to rise for it, you can lay the whole of your morning down before God while nobody is watching and nothing is demanding. This album lives entirely inside that hour.

*Lanterns at Dawn* is my record of the first prayer of the day, the lamps lifted before the sun, the heart turned toward Yasu' before the world can turn it toward anything else. The opening song is exactly that scene: *Yasu', the courtyard is dark and the wicks are still smoking, so I lift the little lanterns till they catch the first gold; the night was so long and the oil was running low, but You are the dawn that I waited for, the warmth in the cold.*

## Giving Him the morning first

The whole conviction of this album fits in one small decision: whom does your morning belong to?

Everyone's morning gets claimed. The phone claims it, the market claims it, the worry you fell asleep holding claims it before your feet touch the floor. The songwriter-king knew this temptation three thousand years ago and made a discipline against it, *in the morning, O Lord, You hear my voice; in the morning I lay my requests before You and wait expectantly* (Psalm 5:3). In the morning, and wait. Before the day can spend you, you spend the first of it on Him. The pre-chorus of the opening song is that exact turning: *before the saffron light has spilled across the wall, I am turning every flame toward You, my first and my all.*

I did not want a record about squeezing God into a busy schedule. I wanted a record about beating the schedule to the punch, meeting Him in the stillness before the stillness is stolen.

> Lanterns at dawn, lanterns at dawn. I lift them to Yasu' while the dark is still drawn, the fanous is burning and the night is withdrawn.

## The mercy that is always waiting at first light

There is a reason the dawn, and not the noon, anchors this album. Because morning in the Scriptures is where mercy keeps its standing appointment.

The prophet, sitting in the ashes of a ruined city, still found the ground to say that the steadfast love of the Lord never ceases, His mercies never come to an end; they are new every morning, great is Your faithfulness (Lamentations 3:22-23). New every morning. Not recycled from yesterday, not rationed, not dependent on how well you slept or how the day before went. A fresh mercy, delivered before you wake, waiting in the dark like a lantern already lit. So the second track on the record, "Saffron Morning," is nothing but that discovery, the sunrise as the daily proof that God's compassion did not run out overnight.

That is why the dawn hour is not a burden but a gift. You are not rising early to earn God's attention. You are rising to receive a mercy He already prepared while you were asleep. The lantern was burning in the archway before you came down the stairs.

And there is a tenderness in meeting Him at that hour that the crowded day can never give. In the stillness before the souk, before the first voice calls and the first errand pulls, it is only you and Him and the smell of the cardamom rising. The heart hears more clearly in that quiet, the way the psalmist, awake in the watches of the night, found his God nearer than his own breath (Psalm 63). The early hour is not merely disciplined. It is intimate. It is the one part of the day nobody else has touched yet, offered whole.

## The lamp that outlasts my little flames

There is an honesty in the imagery I want to name, because it keeps the album from being sentimental. My lanterns run low. The opening verse admits it, *the oil was running low.* Any flame I light will gutter and need refilling. That is the truth about every discipline of prayer I have ever kept: it flickers.

So the record moves, by its tenth song, to a greater lamp than any I can hold. "The Lamp in the Fanous" reaches past my little wicks to the One who called Himself the Light of the world, and to the Word that the psalmist said is a lamp to my feet and a light to my path (Psalm 119:105). My lanterns are real and worth lifting, but they are only ever reflecting a Light that never gutters, never runs low, never needs me to refill it. I light my small flames at dawn precisely because there is a Sun rising behind them that will still be burning when every wick I own has gone out.

The bridge of the opening song already reaches for that, quietly borrowing heaven's own song: *worthy is the Lamb who outshines the rising sun.* The Lamb of Revelation 5 outshines the very dawn I am lifting my lanterns toward. My flames are a welcome for a Light far greater than themselves.

## For the ones who want to give Him the first of it

The album carries on into the waking day, the qahwa brewing, the bread warming, the souk about to open, the cedars and the courtyard and the road. But it never forgets where it began, in the dark before the gold, with a handful of lanterns lifted toward the One who is the true dawn.

I made this for anyone tired of handing their mornings to everything but God. For the one who keeps meaning to pray and keeps getting beaten to it by the noise. For the diaspora heart in a cold northern city, and the heart at home under a familiar sky, both wanting to give **Yeshua** the first of the day before anyone else takes it. You do not need a full lantern or a steady flame. You need only to rise into the stillness while the dark is still drawn, lift what little light you have, and turn every flame toward Him.

The mercy is already new. The dawn is already on its way up behind the wall. And **Yeshua**, the Light no night has ever put out, is waiting in the quiet courtyard for the ones who love Him enough to come before the sun. Lift the lanterns. He is worth the early rising, and the morning He gives back is always brighter than the one you gave Him.
