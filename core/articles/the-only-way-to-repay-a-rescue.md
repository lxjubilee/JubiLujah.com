There is a moment in the hundred-and-sixteenth psalm where a rescued man stops and asks himself the most natural question a grateful person ever asks.

He has just come through something terrible. He talks about the cords of death wrapping around him, about being brought low, about crying out and being heard and delivered. He is a man who nearly went under and did not, because God reached down. And now, standing on the other side of it, he asks the question every rescued heart eventually asks: *What shall I give back to Yahuah for everything He has done for me?*

It is the honest instinct of a decent soul. Someone did something enormous for you. Now you want to even the ledger. You want to repay. You want to give something back that comes close to matching what you were given.

And then he answers his own question, and the answer is so strange, so upside down, that most of us read right past it without noticing what he actually said.

## He answers a debt by taking, not giving

Watch it. He asks what he can give back. And then he says: *I will lift up the cup of salvation and call on the name of Yahuah.*

Stop there. That is not a payment. That is the opposite of a payment. He asked what he could *give,* and his answer is to pick up a cup and receive. In Hebrew it is *kos yeshuot*, the cup of *salvations,* the cup of rescues, plural, brimming. The image is of a man at a table who has been asked "how will you repay this?" and who responds by holding out his cup to be filled *again.*

Think about how backward that is by every rule we know. When someone rescues you, you are supposed to pay them back, not ask for seconds. You are supposed to reduce the debt, not deepen it. But the rescued man in this psalm looks at the impossible size of what God has done and understands something that changes everything: **there is no repaying it. There is only receiving more of it.** And astonishingly, receiving more is exactly what pleases the One who gave it.

## Gratitude that takes

We have this deep and stubborn belief that real gratitude *repays.* That the grateful thing to do, the mature thing, the honorable thing, is to give back, to work off the debt, to prove we were worth the rescue, to hand God something that shows we are not just takers.

I want to say this as gently and as firmly as I can, because I think it is quietly exhausting a lot of good, tired believers: **you cannot repay God, and He never asked you to.**

The whole idea collapses the moment you look at the sizes involved. What could you possibly hand the One who made you, saved you, keeps your heart beating, and holds your whole life in His hands? Everything you own already came from Him. Every breath you would offer Him He gave you first. To approach God as a debtor scrambling to pay Him back is not humility. It is a misunderstanding of what kind of relationship this is. He is not a lender collecting. He is a Father rescuing. And the rescued man in the psalm understood it perfectly: the only fitting response to a gift that large is not to repay it but to lift your cup and let Him fill it again.

> You were never asked to pay God back for saving you. You were asked to keep letting Him.

That is a word for the striver. Because the striver hears "what shall I give back?" and immediately starts calculating what they owe, and the calculation never ends, and the ledger never balances, and they spend their whole life tired and behind and secretly afraid they have not given enough. And the psalm walks up to that exhausted person and takes the pen out of their hand and closes the ledger and says: *put down the accounting. Pick up the cup. The right response to His salvation is to receive more of it.*

## The name in the cup

There is one more thing hidden in that cup that I cannot leave out, because it is too good.

The cup he lifts is the cup of *yeshuot*, salvations. And that word shares its root with a name you know. The name that would one day be given to a child in Bethlehem, the name that means *Yahuah saves,* the name **Yeshua.** I want to be careful here and not claim more than the text does, the psalmist was lifting a cup of God's rescues, and he could not have known everything that word would come to hold. But the servant who ministers to the tired in me cannot help but see it: the cup of salvation, the cup of *yeshuot,* pointing down the centuries toward the One who *is* our salvation, the One who Himself would lift a cup on His last night and say *this is for you.*

Because that is where this leads. The whole pattern of the gospel is a cup held out to be received, not a bill handed over to be paid. On the night before He died, Yeshua took a cup and gave thanks and gave it to His friends, and He did not say "now pay Me back." He said "take, drink, this is poured out for you." The response He asks for is the same response the psalm asks for. Not repayment. Reception. Hold out your cup. Let Him fill it.

## Come thirsty, not even

Here is the rest I want to leave you holding this morning.

If you have been coming to God like a debtor, tired from a lifetime of trying to give back enough, to prove you were worth saving, to work off a rescue that was never a loan. I want you to hear the freedom the rescued man found. You do not have to even the ledger. You could not if you tried, and He is not asking you to. The most grateful thing you can do, the most *worshipful* thing, is the very thing that feels backward: to come back thirsty. To lift the cup again. To let a God who loves to give have the joy of giving to you one more time.

That is not laziness and it is not ingratitude. It is the highest honor you can pay a generous Father, to trust Him enough to keep receiving from His hand without shame, and to call on His name not as a debtor but as a beloved child who has learned that the cup is never empty and the well never runs dry.

So put down what you have been trying to repay. Hold out your cup. He has been waiting to fill it again, and the filling of it is His delight, not your debt. Come thirsty this morning, and let Him.
