Every other song in the Bible has words. The psalms have words, the priestly blessing has words, the songs at the sea and in the upper room have words. But there is one appointed day in the Hebrew year whose central act of worship carries no words at all. It is a sound. A single, raw, curling blast pushed through a hollowed ram's horn until the note breaks and the air shakes and everyone within earshot feels it in the middle of their chest before they have thought a single thought.

In the Torah it is called *Yom Teruah*, the Day of the Blast, or the Day of the Shout. Leviticus lists it among the appointed feasts: on the first day of the seventh month, a day of rest, a holy gathering, "a memorial of *teruah*." Numbers calls it, plainly, "a day of blowing." (The names by which the world now knows this day, and the elaborate order of blasts developed for it, came later, in the tradition that grew up around the command. The command itself is beautifully simple: on this day, make the sound.) No lyrics were given. No melody was written down. The instruction was to take the *shofar*, the horn of a ram, and blow.

## An instrument that cannot lie

I have spent my life around instruments that can be made to say almost anything. A harp can be sweet or sorrowful, a flute can flirt or mourn, a voice can be trained to sound humble while the heart behind it is proud. But the shofar is nearly impossible to dress up. It has no keys, no frets, no reed, no finesse. It is a bent piece of animal bone-hard horn with a hole worn through it, and what comes out is not pretty by any ordinary standard. It cracks. It wavers. It sounds like a cry more than a tune.

And that, I have come to believe, is the point. There are moments in a life when polished worship is exactly the wrong thing, when you do not need a well-turned phrase, you need a sound that tells the truth about how it actually is down there. The shofar is the sound of worship stripped of its vocabulary. It cannot flatter. It cannot perform. It can only sound the alarm your heart is already sounding and cannot find the syllables for. When the words run dry and you are still standing before God with something enormous pressing on you, the horn says what the tongue cannot. It is the groaning too deep for words made audible.

## One sound, two meanings

Here is the thing about the Hebrew word *teruah* that has never stopped moving me. It is used for two things that seem like opposites.

*Teruah* is the sound of war. It is the alarm blown from the walls when an enemy is coming, the blast that sends a city running for its weapons, the note that makes the hair on your arms stand up. The prophets use it that way, a trumpet of judgment, a warning you ignore at your peril.

But *teruah* is also the sound of joy. It is the shout that went up when the ark came home, the roar of a people so glad that the noise carried for miles, the blast that crowned a king and opened a festival. The same word covers the blare that means *danger, run* and the blare that means *he is here, rejoice*.

One sound. Two meanings. And I do not think that is an accident, because they turn out to be the same event seen from two sides. The blast that terrifies the enemy is the blast that gladdens the child of the house. When the king comes, it is dreadful news for the rebellion and homecoming news for the family, and the horn does not change its note between them. The shofar simply announces that Someone is arriving, and whether that lands on you as alarm or as joy depends entirely on whose side of the wall you are standing on. The day of the blast asks a quiet question underneath all its noise: when you hear that He is coming, does your stomach drop, or does your heart leap? The sound is the same. Only the listener is different.

## The horn that stopped a hand at Moriah

There is a reason the horn is specifically a *ram's* horn and not just any animal's, and the reason reaches all the way back to a mountain and an old man and a boy and a knife. When Abraham had bound his son on the altar at Moriah, certain that he was about to lose everything, it was a ram, caught by its horns in a thicket, that God provided in the son's place. The knife came down on the ram, and the boy walked home alive.

So the ram's horn is not a random instrument. It is the horn of the substitute. Every time it sounds, it is, in a sense, the voice of that ram still speaking, the memory of a life given so another could go free, the sound of a rescue that came at the last possible second on a hill outside the city. When Israel blows the shofar, an old strand of tradition says, it is calling on the mercy of that moment: *remember the ram; remember that You provide the substitute; remember the mountain where the son walked home.* The wordless blast is, underneath, a plea grounded in a rescue already given, which is why a sound that begins like an alarm can end like relief.

## Learning to hear it as an invitation

Most of us live with a low, constant background hum of dread about God's arrival. Somewhere in us is the suspicion that if He really came near, it would go badly, that the trumpet, if it ever sounded for us, would sound like an alarm. And I understand that fear. There is a version of the blast that is genuinely a warning, and Scripture never pretends otherwise.

But listen again to what the horn is made of. It is the horn of the ram, the substitute, the one who took the place of the one who should have died. For anyone who has come home under that rescue, the sound changes meaning entirely. It is no longer the alarm on the enemy's wall. It is the welcome-home blast blown over the returning child, the note that goes up when the King everyone else fears turns out to be your own Father, coming up the road with His face already shining.

You do not need words to answer it. That is the mercy of the day of the blast. When you have run out of language for what you carry, you are not disqualified from worship. You are, if anything, closer to its center than the fluent are. Push the breath out. Let the sound break. Let the horn say what you cannot. The One it announces has already provided the substitute, and the blast that others hear as terror, you get to hear as the oldest, warmest word there is: *come home.*
