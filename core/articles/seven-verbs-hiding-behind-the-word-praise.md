The Hebrew name for the book of Psalms is *Tehillim*, "Praises." That title alone is worth a long pause, because the book is full of complaint, terror, weeping, and rage, and yet Israel filed the whole collection under the heading *Praises.* But leave that for another day. I want to look at the word itself, because *tehillah* is only one of the words the Hebrews had for the thing we lump together as "praise", and the fact that we have collapsed a whole vocabulary into a single pale word has quietly cost us something in how we worship.

English is generous in many places, but here it is stingy. It gives you *praise*, maybe *worship*, maybe *thanks*, and asks those few coins to buy everything. So when you open your Bible and read "praise the Lord" in one verse and "praise the Lord" in the next, you assume they are the same act. They are frequently not. Beneath that flat English word lie at least seven distinct Hebrew verbs, and each one has a body attached to it, a posture, a volume, a temperature. To learn them is a little like being colorblind your whole life and then, one morning, being handed the colors. The scene was always this rich. You were reading it in gray.

Let me walk you through seven of them, gently, the way you would introduce old friends.

## Halal, to shine, to be gloriously undignified

*Halal* is the root that gives us *hallelujah*, literally, *praise Yah*, praise the Name. But the root does not mean quiet approval. It means to shine, to make a show, to be clamorous, even to look a little foolish with gladness. It is the praise of someone who has stopped worrying about how they appear. When David danced before the ark with everything he had, and his wife despised him for making a spectacle, David's answer was essentially: I will be even more *halal* than this. This is the praise that does not check the room first. Most of us have a lifetime of training in *not* being *halal*, and Scripture keeps inviting us to unlearn it.

## Yadah, to throw out the hands

*Yadah* comes from the word for *hand*, and it means to praise with extended, thrown-out hands, the gesture of a heart so full it pushes the arms up and open. It is also the word for *thanks* and, tellingly, for *confession*, because the same open-handed posture that says "thank you" also says "here I am, nothing hidden." *Yadah* is the raised hands you see across a room in worship, not a performance and not a rule, but the body doing on the outside what the heart is doing on the inside: reaching, releasing, holding nothing back.

## Barak, to kneel, to bless

*Barak* means to bless, and its root picture is *to kneel*. Here is praise from below, not above, the bending of the knee before the One who is worthy. It is the same word used when God blesses us and when we bless God, which is a beautiful strangeness: the direction changes but the posture of honor does not. Where *halal* lifts you to your feet, *barak* brings you to your knees, and worship needs both. There are things you can only say standing up, and things you can only say kneeling down.

## Zamar, to make the strings speak

*Zamar* is the musician's word. It means to make music with an instrument, to pluck the strings, to touch the notes. It is the root inside *mizmor*, the heading over dozens of psalms that essentially means "a piece to be played." *Zamar* is praise that refuses to leave the instruments in the closet, the conviction that God is worth not only our words but our craft, our tuned strings and worked-out arrangements and the skill of hands that have practiced. It is worship that takes trouble. The God who gave us music is honored when we bother to play it well.

## Todah, the thanks offered before the answer comes

*Todah* is the word a modern Israeli uses to say *thank you*, and in the Scriptures it names a specific offering of thanksgiving, brought with a song. What makes *todah* extraordinary is that it is often offered as a sacrifice, that is, it costs something, and it is frequently voiced *before* the deliverance has actually arrived. It is thanks given on the strength of God's character rather than on the receipt of the goods. *Todah* is the praise you sing into the dark because you trust the One who promised the dawn, and it may be the bravest of all the seven.

## Tehillah, the song that comes up on its own

*Tehillah* is the word that titles the whole book, and it means a song of praise, specifically a spontaneous one, praise that is sung, that bubbles up as melody rather than being merely spoken. There is a haunting line in the Psalms that says God is *enthroned on the praises*, the *tehillot*, of His people, the sung praise of the gathered making a kind of throne. *Tehillah* is what happens when the other verbs have done their work long enough that praise stops being a duty you perform and becomes a song that rises out of you before you decide to sing it.

## Shabach, to shout it out loud

*Shabach* means to laud with a loud voice, to shout, to address in a raised tone, and, curiously, also to still or to soothe. It is the praise of full volume, the roar of a people who are done being quiet about what God has done. And its second meaning is the secret: this loud, shouting praise is also the thing that *stills* the soul. We think we get quiet first and then we praise; *shabach* says sometimes you have to shout before you can be still, that the loud declaration of who God is settles the storm inside.

## Why the colors matter

Now hold those seven together, *halal*'s shining exuberance, *yadah*'s open hands, *barak*'s bent knee, *zamar*'s skilled strings, *todah*'s costly forward-looking thanks, *tehillah*'s spontaneous song, *shabach*'s stilling shout, and you can see how much poorer our single word *praise* really is. When your Bible says "praise Him," it is not always asking for the same thing. Sometimes it is calling you to your feet and sometimes to your knees; sometimes to open your hands and sometimes to open your throat; sometimes to pick up an instrument and sometimes to bring a costly thanks before you have anything to show for it.

I do not tell you this to make worship complicated. I tell you it to make it free. Most of us have a single default setting, one narrow band of the spectrum we are comfortable in, and we quietly assume that is what worship *is*. The Hebrew vocabulary breaks that assumption open. It tells the reserved that there is a *halal* in them waiting to shine. It tells the exuberant that there is a *barak* that will bring them low. It tells the person in the pit that *todah* is available before the rescue, and it tells the one who cannot stop weeping that *shabach*, the loud declaration, is also, mysteriously, the thing that stills.

You were never meant to worship in a single color. The God who made the whole spectrum of light and the whole range of the human body and voice gave His people seven words and more for one glad response, precisely so that no mood, no season, no temperament would be left without a way in. When words like *praise* flatten in translation, the richness does not disappear. It just waits under the surface for someone to come and turn the lights back on.

So this week, try a color you do not usually reach for. If you are a kneeler, stand and *halal*. If you are a shouter, go quiet and *zamar* something on an instrument with real care. If you are stuck in the dark with nothing to show, bring the costly, forward-looking *todah* anyway. The scene was always this bright. Praise was always this many things. It has just been waiting, all this time, for you to open your hands and let more of it through.
