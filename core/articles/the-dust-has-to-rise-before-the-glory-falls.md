I built this record around a picture I could not shake: a rough altar of stacked timber on a high ridge, first light coming up cold and clean, and a thin line of smoke climbing off the offering into a sky that has not decided yet whether it will answer.

That smoke is the whole album. Not the fire that falls, the smoke that rises before it. I named the thing *Altar Dust & Glory* because I had come to believe those two words belong on the same breath, and most of us have been taught to keep them apart.

## Why I had to make it

I grew up around people who thought worship was what you did after the glory showed up. You wait for the goosebumps, the swell, the moment, and then you lift your hands. But out on the frontier, in the log-house meetings and the clearing revivals, I watched something older. I watched people bring the offering first. Before they felt a thing. In the cold, in the dark, with the lanterns held low, they laid it down and they let the dust rise, and they did not demand that heaven hurry.

That order matters. The dust goes up before the fire comes down. Every single time. I have never once found it reversed in the whole record of Scripture, and I have looked, because there were seasons I badly wanted it to be reversed, seasons I wanted the glory first, as proof, before I risked the offering. It does not come that way. The altar gets built in the dark, and the smoke goes up while you are still cold, and only then does the sky decide to answer.

Exodus chapter twenty-nine calls the burnt offering a sweet savor to **Yahuah**, the smoke itself is the thing that pleases Him. Not the ash left over. The rising. The part where you can still smell the cedar burning and you do not yet know what He will do with it. I wanted a record that lived right there, in the ache between the offering and the answer, because that is where most of my life actually happens, and I suspect it is where most of yours does too.

## What the title means

"Altar dust" is the residue of a real sacrifice. You cannot get it without giving something up, the bullock, the timber, the morning you would rather have spent asleep. It is dirty. It gets on your boots. The opener, *Altar Dust Rising*, puts a plain altar "on the high ridge" and lets the "dust of the offering" climb "into the dawn," and I sing it the way I mean it: every offering my frontier heart could carry, smoking on the timber of the cross, lifted in the glory at no cost.

"Glory" is what **Yahuah** does with the dust that we could never do ourselves. In Second Chronicles chapter seven, when Solomon finishes praying, the fire falls and the glory fills the house so thick the priests cannot even walk back in to do their jobs. That is track eight of this record, *Glory Filled the House*, the whole assembly thrown to the floor, not because they worked it up, but because He came down.

> The dust is yours to raise. The glory is His to send. Do not confuse the two, and do not skip the first one waiting on the second.

## The heart under the songs

The center of this album is a fire on a mountain. Track nine, *Cedar Fire Cinematic*, is Elijah on Carmel, and it opens with the plain words of First Kings chapter eighteen: the fire of **Yahuah** fell and consumed the sacrifice, and the wood, and the stones, and even the dust. I put that verse right up front on purpose. The dust that Elijah had raised, the offering he built with his own hands and then drenched in water until it looked impossible, that same dust is what the fire came for. Heaven did not answer his cleverness. It answered his altar.

But I did not want a record that only shouted. So I hung the quiet tracks around the fire like lanterns. *I Will Lift My Eyes* is Psalm one hundred twenty-one sung almost in a whisper, the pilgrim song, "I will lift up mine eyes unto the hills, from whence cometh my help." I wrote it for the bedside and the night watch, for the one who cannot raise a shout right now and needs to be told that the Keeper of Israel neither slumbers nor sleeps. And *Holy Holy Holy on the Mountain* takes Isaiah chapter six, the seraphim covering their faces, and drops that trembling awe down into an Appalachian valley where the cedars bow in the wind of the Spirit.

Then there is the trail. *Wagon Trail Hallelujah* and *Cloud of Witnesses* both stand on Hebrews, the strangers and pilgrims of chapter eleven, the great cloud of chapter twelve cheering us on. Because an altar is not a destination. It is a place you stop on a long road to lay something down, and then you get back on the wagon and keep moving toward home. I wanted the record to feel like that: fire, then dust, then wheels turning again at dawn.

## Who I made this for

I made *Altar Dust & Glory* for the ones who are tired of manufacturing a moment.

For the mother holding a lantern who brought her offering before she felt a thing. For the man whose sacrifice this year cost him more than anybody in the room will ever know, and who is standing at the timber in the cold wondering if the fire is ever going to fall. For the pilgrim who has been on the trail so long the dust has settled into his skin, and who needs to be reminded that the dust on him is not proof he is far from the altar. It is proof he has been near it.

Hear me on this, because it is the thing I most want you to carry off this ridge: you do not have to feel the glory to raise the dust. You raise it in the dark. You lay the offering on the timber before the sky answers. And the record of **Yahuah**, from Elijah's soaked altar to Solomon's finished prayer to the cross where **Yeshua** became the offering Himself so we would never have to earn the fire, that whole record says the same thing every time. The One who told you to build the altar has never once let the dust rise for nothing.

So build it. Raise your dust into the cold dawn. Sing it before you feel it.

And then watch the ridge. Because the fire that fell on Carmel is the same fire that fills the house, and it is still, this very morning, looking for an altar to answer.
