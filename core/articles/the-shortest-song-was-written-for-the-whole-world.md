There is a chapter in your Bible so small you have probably turned past it without stopping. Two verses. It is the shortest chapter in the whole book, shorter than a paragraph, shorter than most of the notes in the margins around it. And here is what undoes me: of all the things a writer could have packed into that tiny space, the smallest chapter in Scripture was spent handing the song of worship to every nation on the face of the earth.

Read Psalm 117 slowly, because you can. It will not take you long. *Praise Yahuah, all you nations. Laud Him, all you peoples. For His covenant love has prevailed over us, and the faithfulness of Yahuah endures forever.* That is the entire psalm. And it is not a suggestion whispered to insiders. It is a **command**, and it is shouted across a border to peoples who, at the time it was written, had no covenant, no temple, no promise, and no reason on earth to obey it.

## Two words that both mean "everyone who is not us"

Look at who is being addressed, because the Hebrew makes it wider than our English can.

The first line calls out to the *goyim*, the nations, the Gentiles, the peoples outside Israel. The second line calls out to the *le'ummim*, another word for the peoples, the tribes and clans and language-groups of the earth. Two different words in two short lines, and both of them mean, roughly, *everyone who is not us.* The psalm does not say *praise Him, house of Israel.* It leans its whole weight over the wall of the covenant and shouts to the outside.

And the verbs are worth your attention. The first is *halal*, the root hiding inside the word *hallelujah*, a word that means to shine, to make a show, to boast out loud about someone until you look almost foolish with it. The second is *shabach*, to laud, to commend at the top of your voice, to address someone with a loud and public honor. So the smallest chapter in the Bible is telling the peoples who were never taught the songs to sing them at full volume anyway. To boast about a God whose Name they had barely learned. To make a show of a covenant love they had not yet tasted.

I have carried worship into rooms exactly like that, rooms full of people who had every reason to believe the song belonged to somebody else, some older, holier nation with a longer history in the faith. And I have watched their faces change when they understood that this psalm was not written *about* them from a safe distance. It was written *to* them. Their name is in it. *All you nations* includes the one you were told did not count.

## The reason is aimed at people it does not yet fit

Now sit with the reason the psalm gives, because it is stranger than it looks.

*For His covenant love has prevailed over us.* The word there is *chesed*, the loyal, unbreakable, covenant-keeping love of Yahuah, and the verb, *gavar*, means to be mighty, to overpower, to win. His faithful love did not merely reach Israel; it *conquered* them, it prevailed over them like a strong man overcoming a weaker one. And the psalm holds that up as the reason the *nations* should praise.

Do you see the beautiful audacity of it? Israel is essentially turning to the whole watching world and saying: *look what His love has done to us. You should praise Him for it too.* The mercy shown to one people is offered as evidence to every people. The kindness that overpowered a small nation on the edge of the great empires becomes the argument for the whole earth to sing. His faithfulness is not a private inheritance to be guarded. It is a rumor meant to travel.

> The smallest chapter carries the largest invitation. Nothing in Scripture reaches wider than the verses that take up the least room.

There is another short psalm just up the road from this one, Psalm 67, that beats with the very same heart. It takes the ancient blessing the priests spoke over Israel, *may God be gracious to us, and bless us, and make His face shine upon us*, and it turns that blessing outward like a lamp. *So that Your way may be known on the earth, Your saving power among all nations.* And then it breaks into that ringing refrain: *let the peoples praise You, O God; let all the peoples praise You.* The blessing Israel received was never meant to stop at Israel. It was light poured into one lamp so that the whole dark house could see.

## Why the frontier lives in the small verses

I love that the global mandate is hidden in the least likely place. I think there is a lesson in it that the church keeps missing.

We look for the mission of God in the grand chapters, the long prophecies, the thundering scenes. And it is there. But it is also tucked into two little verses that most people skim. It is as if Yahuah keeps writing the whole world into the margins, into the short songs, into the places we assume are too small to matter, so that no one can ever say the invitation was only for the important people in the important passages.

The peoples He is calling are often exactly like this psalm: small, overlooked, easy to turn past. The far-off village. The scattered household in a language no missionary has learned. The believer who thinks worship is a room she is not allowed to enter because she came from the wrong country, the wrong past, the wrong side of every wall. And to all of them, Scripture spends its very smallest chapter saying one thing, plainly, as a command and not a maybe: **you, too. Praise Him. Out loud. All of you.**

## Take your two verses and sing

So here is what I want you to do with the shortest song in the Bible.

Stop reading it as though it belongs to somebody else. If you have ever felt too far out, too new, too foreign, too unqualified to lift your voice with the people who seem to have been singing forever, this psalm was aimed directly at you. It did not spend its two precious verses on the insiders. It spent them on the outsiders. It spent them on the nations. It spent them, quite literally, on the ones who were not yet in the room.

And notice how it ends. After the command and the reason, the last word of the smallest chapter is simply *Halleluyah*, praise Yah, the very shout it was calling for, breaking out right there on the page, as if the psalm could not even finish giving the instruction before it started obeying itself.

That is the invitation. You do not have to wait until you feel like you belong. You do not have to earn a longer history first. His covenant love has already prevailed. His faithfulness already endures forever. The song is already yours, written into the smallest space on purpose so that no one, in any nation, could ever be missed.

So lift your voice. The shortest song in the whole book was written for you.
