"Bless the Lord, O my soul." You have sung it, prayed it, embroidered it on things. It is one of the warmest lines in the songbook. And it is also, in English, one of the vaguest, because *bless* has become a word we use for sneezes and meals and polite goodbyes. What could it possibly mean to *bless* the Almighty? He is not short of anything. He does not need our good wishes.

The Hebrew clears the fog instantly. And it does it not with an idea but with a body part.

## The knee in the word

The verb is *barak.* And *barak* is built on the same root as *berek*, the Hebrew word for *knee.*

That is not a poetic stretch on my part; it is right there in the consonants. The word for *bless* and the word for *knee* are the same three letters. Which means the Hebrew idea of blessing is not, at its root, a warm sentiment or a wish for someone's wellbeing. At its root it is a posture.

**To bless is to kneel.**

You can watch the picture behave in the plain narrative of the Scriptures. When Abraham's servant reaches the well at evening, the text says he made his camels *kneel* down outside the city, and the word doing that work belongs to this same family. The camels *barak.* They bend the great awkward hinges of their legs and lower themselves to the ground. The image underneath the whole word is a large body folding downward in submission.

So when the psalmist cries *barak Yahuah*, "Bless Yahuah, O my soul". He is not sending up a pleasant wish. He is telling his own soul to *bend the knee.* To lower itself. To take the posture of a creature before its Maker.

## Then how does a mortal bless God?

This is the puzzle that the buried knee finally solves.

We get tangled up because we hear *bless* and think of *conferring something*, a benefit handed down from someone greater to someone lesser. When a parent blesses a child, or Yahuah blesses His people, that direction makes sense: the greater gives to the lesser. But then the psalm turns it around and tells us to bless *Yahuah,* and the logic seems to break. What can we possibly confer on Him?

The knee answers it. When *barak* runs downward, from Yahuah to us. It means He bends toward us to enrich us, to give. But when *barak* runs upward, from us to Him. It does not mean we top up His supplies. It means **we kneel.** We adore. We take, deliberately and gladly, the low posture that says *You are worthy and I am Yours.*

> To bless the One who needs nothing is not to give Him something. It is to get on the knee He already deserves and to name Him worthy from the floor.

The direction of the word changes but its heart does not. Blessing, in both directions, is about honor moving between the great and the small. When it comes down, honor takes the form of gift. When it goes up, honor takes the form of a bent knee. Same word. Same reverence. Different posture for different stations.

## The posture we quietly deleted

Here is what troubles me a little, as a teacher who loves this word.

We have kept the *language* of blessing everywhere in our worship. We "bless the Lord" constantly, while quietly deleting the *posture* that the word is built out of. We bless Him standing, sitting, driving, half-distracted. And I am not laying down a law about knees; Scripture has worshipers on their feet with arms flung wide, and that is holy too. But I want us to notice what we lost when *bless* stopped carrying a knee.

We lost the reminder, sewn right into the vocabulary, that worship has a downward direction as well as an upward one. That there is a kind of honor you can only pay from below. That before the God who spoke the galaxies, the fitting response of a creature is not merely to feel warmly toward Him but to *lower* itself, to let the soul do what the camels did at the well, and fold.

There are things you can only say to Yahuah standing up, with your hands thrown open. And there are things you can only say kneeling. The word *barak* was carrying the second kind all along, and most of us have been reading it as if it only meant the first.

## What the knee knows

I have noticed that the body often understands theology before the mind agrees to it. There is a reason that grief, and awe, and overwhelming love all tend to buckle the legs. The knee knows how to say what the mouth cannot manage: *I am small, You are great, and I am glad it is so.*

That is *barak.* Not a duty. A relief. It is the deep exhale of a creature that has stopped pretending to be the center of things and has found, to its surprise, that bending low before the true Center is the safest and gladest place it has ever been.

## Bless Him, then

So take the line back up, "Bless Yahuah, O my soul", and read it now with the knee restored to it.

It is not a soft sentiment. It is the psalmist commanding his own inner life to bend. Telling his soul, which is forever trying to stand tall and manage its own affairs, to fold at last before the One who is worthy, and in the folding, to find that this was what it was made for.

You cannot add anything to Yahuah. That was never what blessing Him meant. To bless Him is to kneel, inwardly always, and sometimes, when the wonder is too much to hold standing, with the actual hinges of your actual body bending toward the actual ground.

The knee was hiding in the word the whole time. Perhaps it is time we let it out.
