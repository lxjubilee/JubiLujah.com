I want to put two crowds side by side, and I want you to notice that they are the same size.

The first crowd is at the foot of a mountain. There is fire on the summit, smoke rolling off the peak like a furnace, a sound so heavy the people beg it to stop. Yahuah is giving His law. And then, while the mountain is still smoking, the people at the bottom build a golden calf and dance in front of it, and something breaks. By the end of that day, the account tells us, about three thousand are gone.

The second crowd is in a city, packed into narrow streets for a festival. There is fire here too, but it has come down off the mountain and settled, gently, on individual heads. A sound fills the house. Men who cannot preach begin to preach, and a fisherman stands up and tells the story of Yeshua, and the crowd is cut to the heart. By the end of *that* day, the account tells us, about three thousand are added.

Same number. Opposite verb. And here is the thing almost nobody was ever taught: **it is the same feast.**

## The anniversary nobody circled

The festival in that crowded city was Shavuot, the Feast of Weeks, the day Greek-speaking Jews called Pentecost, meaning *the fiftieth*, because Leviticus commands the people to count fifty days from the barley firstfruits and then hold a sacred assembly.

Jewish tradition long held that this same day, in the third month after the exodus, was the day Yahuah descended on Sinai and gave the Torah. So Shavuot became, in the tradition, the birthday of the law, the anniversary of the fire on the mountain.

Hold that, because it changes everything about the second crowd. When the Spirit fell in that upper room, it did not fall on a random Tuesday. It fell on the exact festival that commemorated the giving of the law. Heaven chose the anniversary on purpose. The God who does nothing by accident circled a date on His own calendar and came back to it.

He came back to the scene of about three thousand deaths, and He undid the count.

## Fire that used to require distance

Look at what the fire does in each scene, because it is not doing the same thing.

At Sinai, the fire keeps people *out*. A boundary is drawn around the mountain. Do not touch it, do not push through, do not even let the animals wander up, or you will die. The holiness is real and it is lethal at close range. The fire says: *stay back.* The whole architecture of that day is distance, a holy God, a trembling people, and a line between them no one may cross.

Now watch the fire at Pentecost. It does not sit on a summit and hold the crowd at bay. It divides into little flames and comes to rest on *each one of them.* On the servant girl. On the fisherman who three weeks earlier had denied he even knew the Man. On heads that no priest would have selected. The same element that once meant *stay back* now means *this one is Mine, and this one, and this one.*

That is not a softening of holiness. It is holiness that finally found a way to come close without consuming, because in between the two mountains, a Lamb had gone up a third hill and made the nearness safe.

## Stone, and then the softer place

There is one more difference, and it is the deepest.

At Sinai, the law was written on stone. Beautiful, permanent, exact, and outside you. It stood over the people like the cliff it was carved from. You could read it, revere it, break it. What you could not do was keep it, because a word on a rock cannot get inside a heart.

The prophets kept promising a day when Yahuah would take that same law off the stone and write it somewhere else, on the inside, on flesh, on the one surface that could actually obey it. And when the fire came to rest on each head at Shavuot, that is the day arriving. The letter carved in rock kills, because it can only accuse. The Spirit gives life, because it can finally *fulfill* from within the very command the stone could only demand from without.

> The same law that fell on stone and left three thousand dead now falls on hearts and raises three thousand up. It was never the law that was the problem. It was the surface we kept trying to write it on.

## Count the crowd again

So put the two crowds back together one last time, and feel the reversal for what it is.

Sinai: about three thousand fall. Shavuot: about three thousand are added.

This is not a coincidence some preacher stretched. The numbers are sitting right there in two accounts separated by centuries, and they match, and the God who wrote both of them wanted you to do exactly what you are doing now, lay them side by side and gasp. He kept the number so you could not miss the reversal. He undid His own body count in front of witnesses.

And I need you to hear who this is for.

It is for the person who reads the Sinai story and quietly assumes they belong in the first crowd, the one that gets it wrong, dances in front of the wrong thing, ends the day on the ground. If you have spent your life braced for the fire because you are sure that when it falls it will fall *against* you, then this is the news the feast was built to carry across the centuries and hand to you personally.

The fire came back. It came back to the very date, the very number, the very kind of crowd. And this time it did not fall to subtract. It fell to *add*, one small flame per head, no one screened at the door, the denier and the servant girl and the outsider all lit at once and all counted *in.*

## The feast still keeps the number

Shavuot is not a museum. Yeshua is still holding out the same fire on the same terms, and the count is still running the same direction it turned that morning, always adding, never once subtracting a single one who comes.

You are not standing at the bottom of the smoking mountain, held back by a rope, hoping the holiness stays up top where it cannot reach you. You are standing in the crowded street where the flame is coming down head by head, looking for one more place to rest.

There is an empty spot in that number shaped exactly like you. The feast has been keeping it since the day it undid its own count.

Come and be added.
