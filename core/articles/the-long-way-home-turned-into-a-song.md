The prophet was looking at a wasteland, and he described a garden.

That is the thing about Isaiah that unsettles me in the best way. He wrote for a people who were either about to lose everything or had already lost it, carried off, scattered, sitting in exile a thousand miles from the only home their songs remembered. And in the middle of all that ruin, he lifts his eyes and starts describing something that is not there yet. He looks at the dry country between the exiles and their home, the burning, trackless waste that lay like a sentence of impossibility between a captive people and the land they ached for, and he says: it is going to bloom.

The wilderness and the dry land, he says, are going to be glad. The desert is going to rejoice and burst into flower. The parched ground will become a pool, and the thirsty land springs of water. He is not describing weather. He is describing what the earth itself will do when it hears the news that the ransomed are coming home.

## A road where there was no road

Then he says the thing I want to walk down with you.

There will be a highway there. A road, laid straight through the very wilderness that used to be the wall between the captives and their home. He calls it the Way of Holiness, a clean road, a safe road, a road the traveler cannot get lost on even if he is a fool, a road with no lion waiting to spring and no beast to fear. Nothing that hunts the vulnerable will be permitted on it. It is a road built for one purpose only: to carry home the people who were taken away.

Think about what a road means to a people in exile. A wilderness is where you get lost and die. It is the great uncrossable in-between, the reason the distance between here and home feels less like miles and more like a life sentence. And the prophet says: there is going to be a road through it. The impossible middle is going to have a path laid straight across it, and the path is going to be so safe and so sure that even the weakest, most frightened, most directionless traveler will walk it and arrive.

That is what redemption is. It is not only the opening of the prison door at the far end. It is the road home through the wilderness that always stood between you and the door.

## The joy that does not wear off

And here is the line I turn over most, the one I would set to music if I could set anything to music the way it deserves.

The ransomed of Yahuah, Isaiah says, will return. They will come to Zion with singing. And everlasting joy will be upon their heads. They will lay hold of gladness and joy, and sorrow and sighing will flee away.

Everlasting joy upon their heads. He pictures joy as something you can see on a person, crowning them, the way exhaustion and grief sit visible on the shoulders of the beaten-down. He is describing a people whose whole bearing has changed, heads that used to hang, lifted; faces that used to be gray with sorrow, shining. And the joy is not a mood that will pass by afternoon. It is *everlasting.* It does not wear off. It is not the thin, brief relief of a good day that you know will end. It is a gladness fixed on them permanently, worn like a crown that cannot be taken off.

And notice which way the traffic runs at the end. Sorrow and sighing do not stand their ground and get slowly overcome. They *flee.* They run. In the presence of the returning ransomed, grief is the thing that turns and leaves. For a whole exiled generation that had learned to assume sorrow was the permanent tenant and joy the occasional visitor, the prophet flips it completely: joy moves in for good, and sorrow is the one shown the door.

## They came home singing

Do not miss the small verb tucked into the middle of all this glory. *They will come to Zion with singing.*

Not silent. Not staggering in exhausted. Singing. The road home itself is a song. The whole procession of the redeemed walks the highway through the blooming desert with music on their lips, because you cannot carry that kind of joy quietly. It has to come out as sound. A people who have been given everlasting gladness will make everlasting noise about it, and the noise is a song, and the song fills the whole road, and the desert that used to be a graveyard rings with it.

I believe the earth was made to hear this song. I believe the blooming of that wilderness is a picture of what happens everywhere the news arrives that the captives are being brought home, that the ground itself, the whole creation, leans toward that sound and comes alive at it. The road home is not a lonely trudge. It is a parade. And the parade is singing a homecoming song loud enough to make flowers stand up out of dead sand.

## The road is open now

Here is what I need you to know, because Isaiah was not only describing an ancient return from an ancient exile. He was describing a shape that God keeps making true.

There is a road home laid straight through your wilderness. Whatever the uncrossable middle is in your life, the years lost, the distance you cannot see across, the waste between where you are and where you belong, the news of the gospel is that a Way has been opened through it, a Way so sure that the foolish and the frightened and the directionless cannot miss it, a Way with nothing on it that is allowed to devour you. Yeshua Himself said He was the Way. The road through the wilderness has a name now, and the name walks you home.

And you do not arrive at the end of it worn down. You arrive with everlasting joy upon your head. That is the promise, and it is not a mood. It is a crown, and it does not come off. The sorrow that has felt like the permanent condition of your life is, in the presence of that homecoming, the thing that flees.

So sing on the road. Do not wait until you have arrived to lift your head. The ransomed of Yahuah come to Zion with singing, and you are among the ransomed, and the desert is already beginning to bloom on either side of you at the mere sound of it. The long way home was never meant to be walked in silence. It was always meant to turn into a song, and the whole earth was made to sing it home with you.
