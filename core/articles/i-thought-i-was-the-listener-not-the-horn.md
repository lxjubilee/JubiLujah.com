Pick up a shofar sometime and just hold it. It's a ram's horn, curved, rough, hollow, and completely silent. It cannot do a single thing on its own. It has no music in it at all. It waits. And the instant breath moves through it, the whole earth seems to lean in.

I built an album around that object because I think it's a picture of most of us, and we've got the picture backwards. We assume our role in the story is to be in the crowd, hearing the horn. **Yahuah**, I've come to believe, is trying to put the horn in our hands. *Shofar Shake The Earth* is twelve tracks about a sound that wakes a sleeping camp, and somewhere in the making of it, the sound woke me.

## The horn that brought down what an army couldn't

The record opens loud, on purpose. *Shofar Shake The Earth* reaches straight back to two of the most improbable victories in the Bible.

First, Jericho. Joshua marches the people around the city for seven days, and on the seventh the priests blow the horns, the people shout, and the wall comes down, before a single sword is drawn (Joshua 6). Then Gideon: three hundred men, no weapons in their hands but torches inside clay jars and shofars at their lips, and **Yahuah** routs an army (Judges 7:19-22). The lesson of both stories is the same, and it's the engine of the whole album: the horn does the sounding, but Yahuah does the breaking. He goes before us. There is nothing left to prove.

That reframes the whole point of blowing a shofar. It was never about the volume you can produce. It was about announcing that the God who fights for you has already taken the field.

## A festival built entirely around a sound

Most of the moedim, the appointed times of Scripture, are anchored to something you can see or taste. A lamb. Unleavened bread. A booth built of branches. But there is one feast anchored to a *sound*.

Leviticus 23:24 sets it: the first day of the seventh month, a day of rest, "a memorial proclaimed with the blast of trumpets." Its Hebrew name is *Yom Teruah*, the Day of Blasting, the Day of the Roar. No object. Just a horn splitting the sky and a whole camp told to wake up. I gave that day the cinematic peak of the album, track eight, *Yom Teruah (Wake The Camp)*, because everything the record is about lives in that one appointed moment: the God who sets an entire festival on the calendar just to make sure His people don't sleep through what He's about to do.

And I should be honest as a teacher about one thing. The three shofar sounds you'll hear named across this album, the long *tekiah*, the broken *shevarim*, the staccato *teruah*, are the traditional pattern worked out over centuries of practice. Scripture commands the blast; the specific grammar of the blasts is a beautiful tradition built on top of the command. I want you to know which is which, because the truth is more interesting when it's precise.

## The line that stopped me cold

Here is the moment I have to tell you about, because it changed how I heard the whole record.

I was writing the second verse of *Yom Teruah*, imagining the people a horn wakes up, an elder with a six-foot ram's horn, a teenager in Jerusalem, and the lyric turned a corner I didn't plan. In the song, the young woman assumes she's the audience. She's there to hear the blast. And **Yahuah** says to her:

> I thought I was the listener, and Yahuah said, "You are the horn."

I sat with that line for a long time. Because it exposed my own assumption. I had spent years thinking of my calling as receptive, hear the word, receive the teaching, stand in the crowd and be moved. And the shofar corrects that. A horn is not an audience. A horn is a thing breath moves *through* on its way to waking somebody else. The Spirit, **Yahuah**'s own breath, *ruach*, fills the horn, and the horn becomes the shout. You were never meant to only hear it. You were meant to be sounded.

## The whole camp this horn is for

Once you hear the shofar that way, the album's guest list makes sense, because a wake-up call is only good news if it reaches the people furthest from the front.

*Wake Up, O Sleeper* takes its title straight from Paul: "Awake, O sleeper, and arise from the dead, and Christ will shine on you" (Ephesians 5:14). *Gather, O Mishpachah*, mishpachah means family, calls in the prodigal, the widow at the window, the wanderer asleep by the road, the teacher who forgot his joy. *Shevarim (Broken Calls Mend)* is built on Joel standing on Zion's wall crying "Blow the shofar in Zion" (Joel 2:1, 2:15), and it makes the broken blast a picture of return, the shattered person becoming the gathered shout. *Watchman, Hold the Wall* takes Ezekiel's charge to the watchman (Ezekiel 33:6) and sets it at three in the morning, where the real intercessors are. And *Four Winds, One Horn* reaches for **Yeshua**'s own promise that He will gather His elect "from the four winds" (Matthew 24:31), Korea and Kenya, Colombia and Canada, one horn sounding on every continent.

The album ends where the Bible's whole story is heading. *Tekiah Gedolah (The Last Horn)* stands on Paul's words: "the trumpet will sound, and the dead will be raised" (1 Corinthians 15:52). The same horn that woke the camp on track one is the horn that wakes the graves at the end. The first blast and the last blast are the same breath.

## Who I made this for

I made *Shofar Shake The Earth* for two kinds of people.

The first is asleep, and knows it. Not lost, exactly. Just dozing. Faith gone quiet, tent flap down, another season slipping by on the floor of a life that used to be on its feet. **Yahuah** did not wake you to keep on lying down. He woke you to stand up, and He is holding out a horn.

The second is the one who has only ever pictured themselves in the crowd. Who assumed the sounding was for the professionals, the gifted, the up-front. Hear the correction the album is built on: you are not the audience. You are the instrument. You are hollow and quiet and, on your own, unremarkable, exactly like the horn, and that is precisely what **Yahuah** reaches for, fills with His breath, and uses to wake four hundred sleepers who never knew their own names until they heard them blown back.

So take a breath. There's a camp that needs waking, and the horn is already at your lips.
