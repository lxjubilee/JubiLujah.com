Open the book of Psalms to the middle and you will find a little cluster of fifteen short songs sitting together, and every single one of them wears the same heading. Three words in Hebrew: *Shir Hamaalot.* Song of Ascents. Song of the goings-up. Song of the steps.

Fifteen of them in a row, from the hundred-and-twentieth psalm through the hundred-and-thirty-fourth. And they are unlike almost anything else in the songbook, because they were not written for a quiet room. They were written for a road.

## Songs made for the climb

Jerusalem sits high. It is built on hills, and no matter which direction you came from, you came *up* to get there. Three times a year the people were called to make the journey to the great feasts, and they came from everywhere, down in the valleys, out on the farms, over in distant villages a hard week's walk away. Old men came. Nursing mothers came. Children came on short legs. Whole families packed the road, and the road went up.

The word *maalot* means exactly that, steps, stairs, ascents, things you climb. And these fifteen psalms were the songs the pilgrims sang while they climbed. Not from a pew. On the move. Dusty, footsore, watching the city get slowly closer over days of walking.

There is an old tradition, and I want to name it as tradition, a beautiful one handed down rather than written in Scripture, that connects these fifteen songs to fifteen actual steps that led up into the temple courts, one psalm sung on each stair as the worshipers climbed. Whether or not it happened exactly that way, the picture it hands us is true to the songs themselves: worship taken one step at a time.

## Worship for the road-weary

Here is what I love, and what I think the weary among us most need to hear.

These songs are short. If you read them, you notice it immediately. Most of the big psalms sprawl. They go on for verse after verse, whole architectures of praise. But the Songs of Ascents are small. Brief. The kind of song you can actually sing when you are out of breath, when your legs ache, when you have been on the road for days and you have exactly enough air in your lungs for a few honest lines and no more.

That is not an accident. That is mercy built into the design.

Whoever assembled these songs understood something about tired people that we sometimes forget when we build our worship: **a person on a hard climb cannot sing an epic.** They can sing a line. And so the songbook of the pilgrimage is a book of short songs, one after another, each just long enough to get you up the next stretch of road, and then the next, and then the next.

> You were never asked to make the whole climb in one heroic leap. You were only ever asked to sing the next short song and take the next single step.

## They start in the hard place

Look at where the fifteen begin. The very first one, the hundred-and-twentieth, does not open on the mountaintop. It opens in trouble. A man cries out that he is far from home, living among people who hate peace, sick of the distance and the conflict. That is where the pilgrimage *starts*, not in triumph, but in a homesick, worn-out cry from a long way off.

And that is exactly right, because that is where you start too. Nobody sets out on a climb from the summit. You start at the bottom, tired before you have even begun, wondering if you have it in you. The songbook does not pretend otherwise. It meets the pilgrim in the low place and simply says: alright. Sing this. Now take a step.

And as the fifteen go on, something happens. Slowly the songs turn. The one about lifting your eyes to the hills and asking where your help comes from, that one is in here, a walking song about trusting the God who does not sleep to keep you on the road. The one about how they said "let us go to the house of Yahuah" and gladness rose up. The ones about the God who surrounds His people the way the mountains surround the city. Step by step, song by song, the homesick cry of the first psalm climbs toward the joy of arrival. Not in one leap. In fifteen small movements.

## One step at a time is not failure

I sit with a lot of tired believers, and I have noticed the thing that discourages them most is not the size of the climb. It is the belief that they are supposed to make it in a single bound, that real faith would have them at the summit already, that the slow ones and the footsore ones and the ones who have to stop and catch their breath are somehow behind.

The Songs of Ascents dismantle that quietly and completely. Because they are the songs of a whole nation walking uphill *together,* at the pace of the slowest child, singing short songs precisely because nobody could manage a long one on that road. The pilgrimage was never a race. It was a procession. And a procession moves at the speed of its most tired member and calls that holy.

So if your walk with God right now feels less like soaring and more like trudging, if you are getting there in small, unglamorous steps, one honest breath of worship at a time. I want you to hear that you are not doing it wrong. You are doing it exactly the way the songbook was built for. The fifteen songs are not for the people who leap. They are for the people who climb.

## The city is still up ahead

Here is the rest I want to hand you.

The whole point of the Ascents is that the road goes somewhere. The pilgrims were not walking in circles. Every dusty step was carrying them toward the city, toward the feast, toward the place where God had put His name and His people gathered to be glad in His presence. The tiredness was real, but it was tiredness *headed somewhere*, and that changes everything about how you carry it.

You are on that same road. The climb is real and I will not pretend it is not. But you are not wandering. You are ascending. Every small act of worship you manage on a weary day is another step up toward Him, and He counts them all, and not one of them is wasted.

So do not look at the whole mountain today and lose heart. Do not measure the distance still to go. Just sing the next short song. Take the next single step. Then rest, and catch your breath, and sing the one after that when you are ready. That is how the pilgrims did it, all of them, all the way home, and that is how you will get there too.

The city is up ahead, and He is in it, waiting. Take your time. The road is going the right way.
