I have been the desert. Not the poetry of the desert, the actual thing. A dry stretch with nothing green, nothing growing, nothing in the hand, the kind of inner wilderness where you stop even expecting rain because rain does not come to places like that. This album was born out of the memory of being that, and out of the astonishment of what happened next. Because rain came where rain never goes, and out of the dry riverbed something impossible bloomed.

*Desert Rose* opens with that exact confession: *Yasu', I was a thirsty stretch of sand, nothing green, nothing growing, nothing in my hand, then You came like rain where the rain never goes, and out of the wadi You raised me a rose.* The whole record grows out of that one image, a flower rising from a dry wadi, because that is the truest picture I have of what **Yeshua** did to me, and of what He is still doing across a whole region that the world too often writes off as barren.

## The Rose that grows in the dust

The title reaches for one of the oldest and most tender names devotion has ever given the Beloved. In the Song of Songs, the beloved says, *I am the rose of Sharon, the lily of the valleys* (Song of Songs 2:1), and for centuries believers have sung that line over Christ Himself, the fairest, the fragrant One, the beauty that outshines the whole field. That is a devotional reading, a song the church has long sung, and I sing it here with the same love.

But the desert bloom is not only tradition. It is direct prophecy. Isaiah promised that the wilderness and the dry land shall be glad, and the desert shall rejoice and blossom; it shall blossom abundantly and rejoice with joy and singing (Isaiah 35:1-2). A blooming desert is not a sentimental idea in Scripture. It is a promise with the weight of God behind it. So when I sing that Yasu' is the desert rose, I am holding both, the Beloved of the Song, and the fulfillment of the promise that the driest ground would one day break into flower.

> Blooming in the dust where the dry wind blows, deeper than the dune, sweeter than it knows, Yasu', in my desert You rose, You rose.

That doubled word at the end, *You rose, You rose*, is not an accident. It is the flower and the resurrection in one breath. The Rose that grows in the dust is the One who rose from the grave.

## The cross planted in the sand

The record does not stay in gentle imagery, because a real desert is a place of death, and the deepest song on the album knows it. "The Cross in the Sand" is the proclamation at the album's heart, and it refuses to be vague.

*The cross in the sand, the blood of Yasu', truly bled, truly died, truly rose, the cross in the sand, the Lamb of God, bearing the weight of every soul He chose.* I wrote it that way on purpose. Truly. Truly. Truly. Because the beauty of the desert rose is not a mood or a metaphor for feeling better. It grows out of an actual grave, an actual death, an actual body that an actual tomb could not keep. The whole record leans on the confession the Scriptures make of the Lamb who was slain and is worthy (Revelation 5). The bloom is real because the cross was real and the rising was real.

That is what separates this from a pretty desert song. The comfort is not that the sand is secretly nice. The comfort is that the King came down into the sand, bled in it, and rose out of it, and now He raises roses out of ground that had given up.

I have watched this happen in real lives, not only in my own. I have seen the driest people I know, hardened by loss, by exile, by years of being told nothing would ever grow in them again, kneel at that cross in the sand and come up changed, the green shoot pushing through where everyone had stopped looking. The desert does not bloom because it worked hard at blooming. It blooms because rain finally reached it. That is grace, and grace is the only thing that has ever made barren ground rejoice.

## The Shepherd who crosses the dunes

If the cross is the ground the rose grows in, the Shepherd is how it gets found. "Shepherd of the Dunes" is the most personal song on the record, because it is about being carried.

My people have herded flocks across hard country for longer than anyone can count. We know what a shepherd does when one is lost in the dunes. He does not shrug and count his losses; he goes out into the killing heat and he does not come back without it. That is exactly the picture Yasu' painted of Himself, the shepherd who leaves the flock to go after the one that wandered, and lays it on His shoulders and carries it home rejoicing (Luke 15). The chorus is that rescue: *Shepherd of the dunes, Yasu', calling the wanderer home, no more to wander and roam.* Every soul I have ever met in the diaspora, homesick in a foreign city, and every soul at home who feels far from God under a familiar sky, this song is for the wanderer in both.

## For the ones the desert wore down

The album carries on through the qahwa of welcome and the wedding joy of henna and the overflowing table, and it ends in the marketplace with hallelujah on every tongue. But its heart never leaves the wadi where the rose came up.

I made this for the ones the desert has worn down. For the heart that has been dry so long it has stopped expecting anything to grow. For the region so often described only by its hardness, its heat, its loss, and never by the astonishing thing God delights to do in exactly such ground. I want you to know that the driest place you carry is not disqualified from blooming. It is the very kind of ground the promise was made for. The wilderness shall rejoice and blossom. That is not wishful thinking. That is a word older than your dryness.

So bring Him the sand. Bring the empty hand and the riverbed that has not run in years. **Yeshua** is the rain that comes where rain never goes, the Rose that grows in the dust, the Shepherd who crosses the dunes to carry you home. Out of the driest wadi He can raise a rose. He raised one out of me, and He rose Himself out of a sealed tomb, and He is still in the business of making deserts bloom out loud.
