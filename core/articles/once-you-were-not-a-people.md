Read the first pages of the story slowly and you will notice that the very first thing sin did was not make a person guilty. It made a person *hide*.

Before there was a court, there was a bush. Two people who had walked with their Maker in the cool of the day heard His footsteps and, for the first time, ran the other way. They sewed coverings. They stepped back into the trees. And when He called, *where are you?*, the answer was not a defense. It was a confession of a new and terrible feeling: *I was afraid, so I hid.*

That is the oldest symptom, and it is the one we still carry. Long before sin ever sends the bill, it does its quiet, ruinous work by *separating*. It pulls the thread out of the weave. It convinces the ashamed that the safest place is alone, that the honest thing is to withdraw, that everyone else is inside the circle and you are the one exception. Guilt is loud. But isolation is the older wound, and it is the one that goes untreated the longest.

## The Great Scattering

Follow the story forward and the pattern only widens. A brother kills a brother and is sent out to wander, restless and far from the faces he knew. A tower rises on a plain, built by people determined to make a name and stay together on their own terms, and the story tells us they were *scattered*, one tongue split into many, one crowd broken into a thousand mutual strangers who could no longer understand each other's songs.

This is what the Scriptures show us again and again: sin does not only stain the individual, it *shatters the choir*. It divides Jew from Gentile, near from far, clean from unclean, us from them. It builds walls in temple courtyards and in kitchens and in hearts. It teaches whole peoples to look at one another and see rival, threat, foreigner, less-than.

So when we speak of being saved, we have to be careful not to shrink the word. If the disease was only guilt, then a private pardon would be enough, a whispered *you're forgiven*, and each of us could go back to our own tree in our own patch of forest, cleaned up but still alone. But that is not what **Yeshua** came to do. He did not come to hand out solitary pardons to a scattered species. He came to *gather*.

Hear how tenderly He said it: like a hen longing to gather her chicks under her wings. Not fix them. Not merely forgive them. **Gather** them.

## He Was Not Building Soloists

There is a letter, written by a fisherman to believers flung across hostile provinces, people who had been thrown out of families, out of trades, out of the only belonging they had ever known, all because they had turned to the Messiah. To those lonely, displaced people he writes a sentence that I cannot read without my throat tightening. He tells them, in substance, that once they were *not a people* at all, just a scatter of unrelated strangers, but now they are *the people of God*; once they had received no mercy, but now they have received it.

Notice what salvation *is* in that sentence. It is not only mercy received. It is a *people* made. The two arrive together, because in the mind of Yahuah they were never separable. To be shown mercy is to be knit in.

The same writer piles up images, and every one of them is a *joined* thing. You are living stones, he says, and a single stone is not a house. You are being *built together* into a dwelling. A stone alone in a field is just a stone; laid beside its brothers, bonded, bearing and being borne, it becomes a wall, a shelter, a home for the Presence.

Elsewhere the picture is a body. Not a warehouse of spare parts, a *body*, where the hand cannot say to the foot *I have no need of you*, where the eye cannot dismiss the ear, where the parts that seem weakest are treated with the greatest honor, where when one member suffers they *all* suffer and when one is honored they *all* rejoice. That is not a metaphor for how a good church should behave. It is a description of what you were *saved into*. You were rescued *out of* the loneliness of being a loose limb and *into* the aliveness of being a needed member.

## The Miracle Was the Reversal of the Scattering

Remember that tower on the plain, where one language became many and the people were driven apart? There is a morning, much later, when the reversal came. A room full of ordinary followers, and suddenly a sound like wind, and fire resting on each of them, and they began to speak, and people from every nation under heaven heard the wonders of God each in their *own* mother tongue.

Babel scattered by confusing the languages. This morning gathered *without erasing* them. Yahuah did not flatten the many back into a bland sameness. He kept every accent and made them all understand the same praise. That is the shape of the family He is building: not one nation swallowing the rest, but many nations, many tongues, many timbres, singing one Name in a harmony no single voice could have produced.

I am a teacher of harmony, so let me say it the way I hear it. **Unison is many voices singing the same note. Harmony is many voices singing different notes that belong to each other.** The world's best counterfeit of belonging is unison. Everyone the same, everyone matching, the odd one out either conforming or excluded. But the kingdom is *harmony*. Your part is not my part, and that is not a problem to be solved. It is the whole point. The gathered redeemed are a *many-membered song*, and the reason your voice is needed is precisely that no one else can sing your line.

> You were not saved to be a beautiful solo in an empty hall. You were saved to be one voice in a countless choir, and the music is incomplete until you take your place in it.

## For the One Still Hiding in the Trees

I know that some who read this have made peace with the trees. You have decided that you are the exception, too far gone, too strange, too foreign, too much or too little, the one who belongs on the outside looking in. Isolation always dresses itself up as humility or as safety. *They don't need me. I'm easier to be around from a distance. I'll just worship on my own.*

Beloved, that is the old wound talking, the very first symptom, still whispering the same lie it whispered in the garden: *hide.* And the gospel walks straight into the trees to find you, the way the voice walked in the cool of the day, calling *where are you*, not to shame you out, but to bring you home to a table with your name at it.

You have received mercy. Do not receive it alone. The hand cannot say to the foot *I have no need of you*, and neither can the family say it of you, nor you of the family. The wall of hostility that made *us* and *them* has already been torn down by a body broken on a tree; there is one new people now, and its doors are open, and the choir has been leaving a rest in the music exactly your size.

You were once not a people. That is over. Come and take your part in the song. We have been listening for your voice.
