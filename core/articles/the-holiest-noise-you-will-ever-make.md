There is one day on the calendar where the rule is: be as loud as you possibly can.

Not quiet. Not solemn. Not heads-bowed. *Loud.* On the feast called Purim, the most reverent people you will ever meet put on costumes and masks, load up baskets of pastries to carry to their neighbors, dig into their pockets for the poor, sit down to a feast that is meant to run long and joyful, and then, right in the middle of the sacred reading, when a certain villain's name is spoken aloud, the whole room *erupts*. Stamping. Shouting. Spinning noisemakers as hard as their arms can go. They blot the man's name out under a wall of sound.

I need to be careful and honest with you before I go one word further, because the house I write in does not let me fudge. Most of what I just described, the masks, the noisemakers, the wild carnival mood, is **tradition**, mi gente. Later custom. You will not find "wear a costume and scream" written into the story itself. What you *will* find written into the story is the reason for every bit of it. And the reason is so good that once you see it, you will want to grab a noisemaker too.

## The day everything flipped

The story is the book of Esther, and it is one of the great reversals in all of Scripture.

A man named Haman, powerful and poisoned with hate, cast lots to choose the day he would wipe out an entire people. That is where the name Purim comes from, *pur* is the word for the lot he cast, and *Purim* is just the plural, "lots." The feast is literally named after the dice of the man who wanted them all dead. Sit with that. They named their biggest party after his murder weapon.

Because the dice did not land the way he threw them. A young woman named Esther, hidden in a palace, and her cousin Mordecai, standing at the gate, ended up in exactly the right place at exactly the right hour. The scheme turned inside out. The very gallows the villain built got used on the villain. The day appointed for slaughter became the day of rescue. The book says it plainly: the thing was **turned around**. What was meant for their mourning became their feasting; what was meant for their end became their beginning.

And so the celebration was born, not as a party thrown *after* the danger, in some safe calm afterward, but as a party staged *on the very ground where the graves were supposed to be.* That is what makes the noise holy. They are not being loud because everything is easy. They are being loud on the exact spot where the enemy planned their silence.

## Joy as an act of war

This is the thing most people miss about Purim, and it is the thing I most want to put in your hands.

The riot of joy is not silliness. It is **defiance.** When that whole room stamps and shouts and drowns the villain's name in noise, they are doing something ancient and fierce: they are declaring, out loud and in public, that hate does not get the last word and its author does not even get to keep his name. The tradition of blotting out the name under a wall of sound is a people saying, *we will not let this word stand in the air unanswered.* It is worship shaped like a battle cry.

> The devil can survive your fear. He has trouble with your feasting. Nothing enrages the accuser like a rescued people throwing a party on the grave he dug for them.

And notice what the celebration is actually made of, because this is where it stops being a show and starts being the gospel. The heart of the day is not the costumes. The heart of the day, and this part is right there in the story itself, is **giving.** The rescued people are told to make it a day of sending portions of food to one another, *mishloach manot*, the baskets carried neighbor to neighbor, and of giving gifts to the poor. The joy is not allowed to curl up private and self-satisfied. It has to spill. It has to get up, put on shoes, and walk a basket of sweet things to somebody else's door, and it has to find the person with nothing and make sure they feast too.

Do you see the whole shape of it now? A people who were nearly erased spend their rescue day making *sure no one around them goes without joy.* That is not a party. That is a sermon you can eat.

## The King hidden behind the mask

Here is the part that undoes me, and it is the reason I put on the mask myself.

The book of Esther has a famous secret: the name of God is never written in it. Not once. In the whole story of the great rescue, Yahuah is never mentioned by name. He is *everywhere*, in the timing, in the sleepless night of a king, in the girl who "happened" to be queen, in the reversal no human could have arranged, but His name is hidden. He is working the entire miracle from behind a mask.

And I think that is why the tradition of the costumes is so beautiful, whether the ones who started it knew it or not. On the one day we celebrate the God who saved us without ever showing His face, we all put on faces too. We hide, because He hid. We mask up, in a holy joke, honoring the King who ran the whole rescue from behind the curtain and let a servant girl and an old man at the gate take the visible risk.

But mi gente, the mask is not the end of the story. Because the God who hid His name in Esther eventually stepped out from behind the curtain entirely and *put on flesh.* Yeshua is what happens when the hidden Deliverer finally shows His face, when the reversal we celebrate at Purim becomes a Man who takes the gallows meant for us, and turns the very instrument of death into the instrument of our rescue. Haman's gallows became his own; the cross meant to end Yeshua became the thing that ends *our* death. The pattern is the same pattern. What was built to destroy us got turned around and used to save us. The lots the enemy cast did not land the way he threw them.

## So make the noise

Here is what I want you to take from the loudest, most riotous day on the calendar.

There is a kind of joy that is a weapon, and you are allowed to pick it up. When the accuser whispers your name over the grave he has been digging for you, you do not have to answer him with fear. You can answer him with a *feast*. You can drown his voice under praise the way that room drowns the villain's name under noise. You can put on the garment of gladness on the exact ground where you were promised despair, and you can look the whole scheme in the eye and laugh, because the dice already landed and they did not land his way.

And you do not celebrate alone. You fill a basket. You find the one with nothing and you make sure they feast too, because joy that does not spill is not the joy of the redeemed. The redeemed always throw the party wide open.

So make the noise, familia. Make the holiest, gladdest, most defiant noise you have ever made. The name of the enemy is being blotted out. The King who hid His face has shown it. And the day the graveyard turned into a fiesta is a day worth screaming about.
