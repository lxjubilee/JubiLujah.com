The hardest thing I ever do with a song is stop owning it.

I can write it. That part, for all its difficulty, is mine, the melody arriving, the words being fitted to it, the long patient labor of getting the shape right. And when it is finished I love it in a particular, protective way, the way you love a thing you made with your own hands and understand completely. I know exactly how it should be sung. I know the tempo it wants, the breath it needs, the small ache in the third line that a careless singer would flatten right out. And so my instinct, always, is to hold it close. To keep it in the one place where it will be performed correctly, which is to say, in my own head, where no one can get it wrong.

But a song held that way never becomes anything. It stays a private possession, perfectly preserved and perfectly useless, exactly as good as I am and no better. And I have learned, slowly, and against everything in me, that the only way a song comes into its real life is the one thing I least want to do with it. I have to let it go. I have to hand it to voices I will never hear, in rooms I will never stand in, and let people who have never met me change it into something I did not write.

## A song is not finished until it leaves your hands

There is a strange truth here that took me years to accept. A song is not most itself when I sing it. It is most itself when a stranger does.

When I sing my own song, it can only ever mean what I meant. It is sealed inside my history, my losses, the specific griefs that gave it to me. But hand that same melody to a woman in another country who has never heard my name, and something happens that I could not have manufactured. She sings it into her own kitchen, over her own sorrow, and the song becomes true in a way it never could have been while I was guarding it. She bends a note I wrote straight. She slows a line I meant to rush. She finds a meaning in it I did not put there and did not know was possible, and she is not wrong. She has done what a song exists to have done to it. She has let it become hers.

I used to experience that as loss. Now I understand it as the whole point. The song was never a possession to be protected. It was a seed to be scattered, and a seed that is never let out of the hand does not stay safe. It stays sterile. It simply dies slowly in your fist, unplanted, having produced nothing at all, and the tragedy of it is that it looks like caution the entire time.

## The grain of wheat knew this before I did

Yeshua said this once, near the end, when His own hardest letting-go was almost on Him. He told the people around Him to consider a single grain of wheat. As long as it stays a grain, intact, whole, preserved, held back from the ground. It remains alone. Just the one seed, exactly what it was, going nowhere. But if it falls into the earth and dies, it bears much fruit.

I do not think He was only teaching agriculture. He was describing the deepest law of how life multiplies, and He was about to live it Himself in the hardest possible key. The grain that clings to being a grain stays one. The grain that lets go of being a grain, that goes down into the dark and gives up its own intact self, becomes a harvest. The dying is not the opposite of the fruitfulness. The dying is the mechanism of it. There is no way to the field except through the loosening of the hand.

And I realized that every instinct I have to protect my songs is the instinct of the grain that wants to stay a grain. I want to keep the seed whole. I want to keep it correct, uncorrupted, sung right, mine. And Yeshua looks at that impulse gently and tells me the truth about it: held that way, it will only ever be one seed. The song I refuse to let anyone else sing is a song I have quietly decided may as well not exist.

## Letting go is not the same as not caring

I want to be careful here, because there is a lazy version of this that I do not mean.

Letting a song go is not the same as not caring how it is sung. The scattering only matters because the seed is good, because I labored over it, got it right, made it as true as I possibly could before I opened my hand. You do not scatter garbage and call it generosity. The whole cost of the release is that I am giving away something I love and made well. If it were worthless, letting go would cost me nothing, and it would give the world nothing either.

So the discipline runs in both directions at once, and I have to hold them together. I write as if it will only ever be mine, with all the care of a person who will be judged for every line. And then I release it as if it were never mine at all, with the open hand of a farmer who knows that a fist full of seed feeds no one. The care is not for me to keep. The care is what makes the gift worth giving.

> The song you are guarding so it can never be sung wrong is a song you have decided may as well never be sung at all.

I suspect this is not really about music for you. I suspect there is something you have made, a gift, a work, a truth you carry, maybe a whole way of loving people, that you are holding too close because you know precisely how it ought to go and you cannot bear to watch someone else handle it clumsily. And I understand it. But I have to tell you what the grain of wheat told Yeshua and what Yeshua told me. Held in your hand, it stays one. It stays alone. Let into the ground, given away, allowed to be changed by hands you will never see, it becomes a harvest you were never meant to count.

The song does not live until you let it go. So open the hand. Scatter the good seed you worked so hard to grow. And trust that the God who made a single grain able to become a whole field is more than able to do with your gift what your careful, closed fist never could.
