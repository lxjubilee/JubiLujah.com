Every other song in the Bible arrives with words. The psalms have words. The blessing over the people has words. The song at the sea, the song in the upper room, the songs of the returning exiles, all of them carry language you can write down and hand to your children.

But there is one appointed day in the Hebrew year whose central act of worship has no words at all.

It is a *sound.* A single raw blast, forced through the hollowed horn of a ram until the note breaks and the air trembles and everyone in earshot feels it in the middle of the chest before they have managed to think a single thought. And I have come to believe that the wordlessness is not a limitation of this day. It is the entire point of it.

## The day named after a noise

In the Torah, this day has no romantic name. It is simply *Yom Teruah*, the Day of the Blast, or the Day of the Shout. On the first day of the seventh month, the people were to keep a day of rest and a holy gathering, marked by *teruah.* No lyrics were commanded. No melody was written down. The instruction was gloriously simple: on this day, make the sound.

Now sit with that Hebrew word, *teruah,* because it is doing something your English translations quietly flatten. *Teruah* is not a gentle word. Elsewhere in Scripture it is the alarm blast that told an entire camp in the wilderness to strike their tents and *move.* It is the war shout that armies loosed as they surged forward. It is the roar that went up from a whole nation when the ark came into the camp. *Teruah* is the sound of something about to happen, the noise that gets a sleeping, settled, stationary people up on their feet and pointed in a new direction.

So the day is not named after a pretty tune. It is named after an alarm.

## A sound the body hears before the mind

I have spent my life around instruments that can be trained to say almost anything. A harp can be sweet or sorrowful. A flute can flirt or mourn. A voice can be coached to *sound* humble while the heart behind it is proud. But the shofar refuses all of that. It has no keys, no frets, no reed, no polish. It is a bent piece of horn with a hole worn through it, and what comes out is not pretty by any ordinary standard. It cracks. It wavers. It sounds more like a cry than a song.

And here is the small piece of true science I cannot get over. That blast bypasses the part of you that analyzes and lands first on the part of you that *reacts.* A sudden, loud, unresolved sound reaches the oldest circuitry in the body, the ancient startle wiring that snaps you awake, floods you with alertness, turns your head toward the noise before your thinking mind has caught up. You do not decide to respond to a shofar. Your body responds, and your mind arrives a half-second later asking what happened.

That is not a flaw in the instrument. That is the instrument doing exactly what it was chosen to do.

Words have to be *understood* before they can move you. They go in through the front door of the mind, get sorted, weighed, agreed with or argued down. But the horn skips the queue. It does not ask your permission or your comprehension. It goes straight past the intellect and shakes the body first, which is precisely what you need when the danger, or the King, is close and there is no time for a paragraph.

## Awake, sleeper

Over the centuries a beautiful practice grew up around this day. The sages developed an ordered sequence of blasts, each with its own name and shape, a long unbroken note, a set of broken sighing tones, a burst of short staccato cries, and a final held blast drawn out until the breath gives way. (The single command in the Torah is simply *teruah,* to make the blast; the elaborate order of named blasts is the later tradition, built lovingly on top of the command.) And that tradition understood something deep about the sound. It heard the shofar not merely as noise but as a summons, a call flung out over a people to *stir,* to shake off the drowsiness that had crept over their souls in the long ordinary stretch of the year, and to come to attention because Someone was near.

> The horn was never trying to inform you of anything. It was trying to wake you.

And is that not exactly the condition it finds most of us in? Not rebellious, exactly. Not running. Just *asleep.* Dozed off at our posts. Grown so used to the settled, stationary rhythm of ordinary life that we have quietly stopped watching, stopped listening, stopped expecting anything to change. The great danger of the soul is rarely open defiance. It is drowsiness. It is the slow, comfortable dimming of a heart that has forgotten it was made to stay awake.

Into exactly that fog, the horn cracks.

## The alarm that points to a Person

Here is where I have to slow all the way down, because this is the part that undoes me.

The whole reason to wake is that Someone is coming. An alarm is not sounded over an empty field for no reason; it is sounded because a King is approaching, and the sleeping camp needs to be on its feet to meet Him. The shofar is not the point. It is the finger pointing. And every wordless blast pushed through that ragged horn is announcing the same thing across the centuries, that Yahuah is not far, that He draws near to His people, that history is not a flat and endless afternoon but a story rushing toward its King.

And the King the horn is waking us to meet has a name. Yeshua, the One who stood among a drowsy people and said, again and again, *stay awake.* Watch. Do not let the ordinary evening lull you to sleep, because the master is coming home and blessed are the servants he finds with their eyes open. He spoke about His own return in the language of trumpets and sudden arrivals and sleepers roused in the night. The Day of the Blast, it turns out, was a rehearsal, a yearly drill of the very startle the whole world will feel one day when the last horn sounds and the sleeping are raised on their feet in an instant.

So when I hear a shofar now, I do not hear an antique. I hear an alarm clock set at the foundation of the world, ringing across a dozing people, saying the one thing that has no words because it does not need any:

*Wake up. Get on your feet. He is nearer than you think.*

And I find I do not want to sleep through it. I want to be one of the ones already standing, already turned toward the noise, already awake, when the sound that has no words finally says everything at once.
