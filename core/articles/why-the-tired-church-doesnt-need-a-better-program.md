On the third Sunday of the month, a country pastor counts the cars in the lot before he counts anything else. He has learned not to say the number out loud.

He knows what the number used to be. He remembers when the overflow chairs came out, when the nursery ran two rooms, when the teenagers he baptized were teenagers. Now the good families drive forty minutes to the church three counties over, the one with the lights, the coffee bar, the app. He has seen their photos. He does not blame them. Some Sundays he almost wishes he could go too.

So he does the thing tired pastors do. He fixes the program.

New sermon series with a clever title. Fresh paint in the foyer. A better sound system the board could not really afford. A small-group push, a fresh curriculum, a men's breakfast with a guest speaker. And for about six weeks, it works. The room lifts. He lets himself hope.

Then it settles back to the number he does not say out loud.

Here is what no one tells him, and it is the only thing worth telling: **the program was never the problem, so a better program was never going to be the answer.**

There is a man in the old Scriptures who understood this, though nobody talks about him much. Twelve of them went to look at the land they had been promised. Ten came back terrified, and they were not lying. The cities really were walled. The giants really were there. "We looked like grasshoppers," they said, and it was an honest report of a real feeling about actual giants.

One of them saw the same walls, the same giants, the same everything, and said, "let us go up at once, for we are well able." His name was Caleb. The difference between him and the ten was not better information. They all had the same information. The book says he simply had another spirit in him.

And then he did the thing that made him who he was. He was right, he said so, and they did not listen, so he spent forty years in the wilderness for a fear that was never his, walking the whole way with the very people who had voted him down. He did not leave. He did not spend four decades being right at them. He just stayed, and stayed glad.

At eighty-five, when the ones who said no were finally all gone, he asked for one thing. Not the easy valley. The mountain, the one with the giants still on it. "Give me this mountain." The exact ground that had frightened everyone else out of the promise.

That is the whole secret, and your tired church is standing right on top of it.

> A dying church is not waiting for a strategy. It is waiting for someone who will not leave.

Think about the shepherd in the psalm everybody knows. Read it again and notice what he does not do. He does not optimize the valley. He does not rebrand the valley or add lighting to the valley or launch a six-week series on the valley. He walks through it. Yea, though I walk through the valley, with you, at your pace, all the way to the other side. His whole method is presence. He is simply there, and being there is the entire miracle.

This is the thing the church down the highway with the better everything cannot actually sell you, because it is not for sale. Presence is not a program. You cannot install it in six weeks. It is a pastor who buries the same families for thirty years and christens their grandchildren. It is the woman who has run the same nursery since before you were born. It is the empty chair on the third pew that everyone leaves empty on purpose, because that was Miss Ellen's, and grief is a form of faithfulness too.

None of that scales. None of it photographs well. And all of it is the actual thing the flashy room is desperately trying to manufacture and cannot.

So here is the honest word for the pastor counting cars, and I will not dress it up, because plainness is the only thing that has ever been believed in a room like his: **you have not failed because your church is small. You would only fail by leaving it, or by being there in body while your heart already drove off to the church you wish you had.**

The mountain is still good. The land was always good. What the wilderness generation lost was never the promise; it was the nerve to hold it. And every fold, in every thin season, is quietly kept alive by the one person who keeps holding it on everyone else's behalf until they can hold it again themselves. Not by arguing. By still being there. Still glad. Still going.

> A tired church does not need a better program. It needs someone who will not leave.

So this week, do not add a single thing to the calendar. Do the one thing instead. Find the name. You already know the name, of the person who stopped coming. Don't text. Go. Sit on their porch, or their hospital chair, or their kitchen table, and stay longer than is comfortable, and bring nothing but yourself.

That visit will do more for your church than the next three series combined. And if you are not sure you have the strength left in you to make it, that is exactly the kind of conversation this whole house was built for. The porch light is on, whatever hour you come.
