Every ordinary day of the year, the high priest of Israel was the most magnificent man in the nation. He served in gold. A breastplate set with twelve gemstones, one for each tribe, blazing over his heart. A robe of blue, its hem ringed with golden bells and woven pomegranates. A golden plate bound across his forehead, engraved with holiness to Yahuah. When he moved, he shone. He was glory made wearable, the splendor of a whole people gathered onto one man's shoulders.

And then came the holiest day of all, the Day of Atonement, the one day he would enter the deepest presence of God on behalf of the entire nation, and on *that* day, of all days, he took the glory off.

## The garments he set aside

The instruction is precise and, when you feel its weight, breathtaking. Before he could enter the Holy of Holies, the high priest bathed his body, and then he did not put on the golden garments. He dressed in a plain linen tunic, linen undergarments, a linen sash, and a linen turban, all of it simple, all of it white. The Torah calls these the *holy garments*, but there is not a thread of gold in them. No gemstones. No bells. No crown.

Think of what this means. The single most important act any human being performed all year, carrying blood into the presence of Yahuah to make atonement for a nation, was performed not in the regalia of his office but in the plainest clothing he owned. On the day he came nearest to the glory of God, he wore no glory of his own.

This was not an accident of wardrobe. It was a sermon stitched in linen. The message was unmistakable to anyone with eyes to see: **when you approach the holiness of Yahuah at the deepest level, you do not come clothed in your own splendor. You come stripped of it.** The gold was appropriate for representing the people to the world. But to go behind the veil, into the very presence, the man had to become, in a sense, nobody, a servant in white, his status left folded outside the door.

> The higher he was going, the lower he had to dress. The nearer the glory, the plainer the man. Heaven's arithmetic runs backwards from ours, and here it is written into a change of clothes.

## The humility hidden in the holiest service

We are people who instinctively believe the opposite. We assume that the most important occasions call for our most impressive selves, that when the stakes are highest, we ought to arrive most decorated, most qualified, most obviously worthy of the moment. We would have dressed the high priest in *more* gold for the great day, not less.

But Yahuah reversed it, and He reversed it on purpose, because He was teaching His people something they could not learn any other way: that the holiest ground is entered on the knees of humility, not on the stilts of achievement. The linen said that no ornament impresses God, no accumulated glory earns the nearness, no résumé of righteousness dresses a man well enough for that room. Before the true holiness, the only fitting garment is the plain one, the clothing of a servant who has laid his own importance down at the threshold.

There is something almost tender in it. The most exalted man in Israel spent the highest day of the year dressed like the lowest, precisely so that the whole nation, watching, would understand that the way to the Most High is *down*. And there is a hard grace here for those of us who carry things, who build things, who are used to being the impressive one in the room. The linen is a mercy to us too. It tells us we can set the gold down. We do not have to be magnificent to come near. We only have to be low enough to be let in.

## The One who took off a greater glory than gold

And then all of it, the folded gold, the plain white linen, the great man made small to go in, found its meaning in Yeshua, who did the thing the high priest's clothing had only ever pictured.

He wore a glory next to which gemstones and golden crowns are costume jewelry. He was the radiance of Yahuah Himself, clothed in light before the world began. And on the day He came to make the true atonement for a whole race of people, He took it off. He did not merely set aside golden garments; He set aside the splendor of heaven itself, and He came down, and He was found in the plain linen of a human life, a servant, a carpenter's son, unremarkable to the eyes that passed Him in the street. The One before whom the burning ones cover their faces went about in a body that turned no heads. He laid His glory down to enter the holiest service ever performed.

And the picture followed Him all the way to the end. When His atoning work was finished on the cross, they took His body and wrapped it, in linen. Plain white cloth, the servant's clothing, the very fabric the high priest wore to go behind the veil. He entered the deepest place any man has ever gone, the place of death itself on our behalf, dressed as the linen had always said the true Priest would be dressed: not in the gold of His own majesty, but in the humility that alone can carry a nation home.

## What the linen asks of you

So here is the quiet, searching question the folded gold puts to each of us.

What are you wearing when you come to Him? Most of us, if we are honest, keep trying to approach God in gold, arriving with our accomplishments held up like a breastplate, our spiritual performance polished, our worthiness on display, hoping the splendor will earn us the nearness we long for. And all the while the high priest is standing there in plain white, telling us we have it exactly backwards. The gold does not get you in. The gold has to come *off*.

You do not draw near to the holiness of Yahuah by becoming impressive. You draw near by laying the impressiveness down, by coming as a servant in linen, low and clean and unadorned, which, wonderfully, is the one thing every last one of us is actually able to do. You cannot manufacture glory worthy of that room. But you can take yours off. And the God who wrapped Himself in linen to reach you is not waiting for your gold.

Lay it down at the threshold. Come in low. That has always been the way home, and He walked it first, in the plainest clothing there is.
