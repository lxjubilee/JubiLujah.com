There are valleys in the world that are built to carry a voice.

I have stood in a few of them, narrow places where two mountains lean toward each other with a strip of green in the low middle, and the air between them does something to sound that flat country never does. You speak, and the far slope answers. A word thrown across the gap comes home to you a half-second later, changed, larger, as though the mountain on the other side had decided to agree with you. People who live in country like that grow up knowing that the space between two heights is not empty. It is an instrument.

I think Yahuah knew that when He chose the place.

Because there is a moment in the story of Israel that I did not pay attention to for years, and it is one of the strangest and most beautiful commands in all of Scripture. After forty years in the wilderness, after the whole long dying generation and the whole long walk, the people finally cross into the land they had been promised. And the first thing they are told to do, before they build a single house, before they fight a single battle for their inheritance, is to hold a service between two mountains.

## Half the nation on each height

The instruction had been given long before, through Moses. When you come into the land, he said, this is what you will do. Six of the tribes will climb Mount Gerizim, and they will stand there to speak the blessing over the people. And six of the tribes will climb Mount Ebal, the barer mountain across the valley, and they will stand there for the curse. And down in the low place between the two mountains, the Levites will stand, and they will call out the words of the covenant, one by one, in a voice loud enough to reach both slopes.

And to each thing the Levites declared, cursed is the one who makes an idol in secret, cursed is the one who dishonors his father and mother, cursed is the one who moves his neighbor's boundary stone in the dark, cursed is the one who takes a bribe against the innocent, to each of these, the whole people, on both mountains, were to answer with one word.

Amen.

When Joshua finally led them in, he did exactly that. He built an altar on Ebal. He wrote the words of the law on stones. And then he read the whole thing aloud, all of it, the blessing and the curse, to the entire assembly, with half the people ranged across the face of one mountain and half across the other, and not one person left out. The men and the women, the little ones, and the foreigners who had joined themselves to Israel, all of them there, all of them within the sound of the reading, all of them answering back across the valley.

Stop and picture the sound of that. A single voice in the low place, and then a whole nation on two mountainsides sending back one word, and that word crossing the open air, ridge to ridge, until the valley itself was full of the agreement of a people. The first sound Israel made in the promised land was not a war cry and not a work song. It was an answer.

## Worship is the answer, not the announcement

Here is what that ancient service taught me about the thing I have given my life to, and about the part of it that belongs to you.

Worship, at its root, is antiphonal. It is call and response. It is the oldest shape a song can take, one voice declares, and the many answer, and it is built into the covenant from the very first day the people set foot in their inheritance. The Levites did not sing the whole thing alone. They could not. The service does not work unless the mountains answer. The declaration was only half of it; the other half was the shout that came back across the valley, and without that shout there was no service at all, only a man reading to himself in an empty pass.

We have gotten this backward in a lot of places. We treat the announcement as the real event and the answer as an optional add-on, the sermon is the thing, the song is the warm-up, and the congregation's part is to receive quietly and go home. But the God who wrote the liturgy of the two mountains never intended His people to be an audience. He intended them to be the other voice. The word that finishes the covenant is not spoken by the priest. It is shouted back by the people. Amen. So be it. We agree. Let it be as You have said.

And notice, this is the part that undoes me, that the answer had to *travel.* An amen mumbled into your own collar does not cross a valley. The whole architecture of that service required a response that projected, that carried, that was loud enough and whole-hearted enough to reach the far slope where your brothers were standing. Your agreement was not only for God to hear. It was for the other mountain. It bound a scattered people, split across two heights, into one covenant sound. Your amen was the thread that stitched the divided nation together across the open air.

## The word that is yours to say

I have spent enough years in the high country to know that the mountains do not need us to make them impressive. They were doing that before we arrived. But I have also learned that a mountain will hand your own voice back to you if you are willing to send it out, and that there is a particular joy in hearing your one small word taken up by the rock and returned to you larger than you sent it.

That is what your amen is. It is small in your mouth and enormous in the covenant. It is the single syllable that ratifies everything the declaration has said, that agrees with the blessing, that agrees even with the hard word, that puts your whole weight behind the truth someone else has spoken. It is the part of the song that no worship leader can sing for you, because it is, by design, the answer, and an answer has to come from the one who was called.

So the next time you are in the gathering and the truth goes out, sung, or read, or preached, or prayed, do not stand on your mountain in silence and let the valley go empty. That silence is not humility. It is a covenant with no other voice in it. Send your amen across the gap. Let it carry. Let it reach the far slope where someone else is standing who needs to know they are not the only one who believes it. The service was never meant to be a voice reading to itself in an empty pass. It was meant to be two mountains full of people, and one word going back and forth between them until the whole valley agreed.

Yahuah went to the trouble of choosing a place with the right acoustics. He built the first worship service of the promised land out of a call and an answer, and He gave the answer to you.

Do not keep it in your collar. Let it cross the valley.

Amen.
