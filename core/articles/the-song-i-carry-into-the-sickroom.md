The first time I sang at a deathbed, it was because I ran out of words.

I was a young pastor and I had prepared, the way young pastors do, a small speech of comfort, something about the valley and the shepherd, something true and correct. But when I stood in that dim room and looked at the man in the bed, thin as paper, past speaking, his family gathered close and silent, every prepared sentence I had turned to ash in my mouth. There was nothing to explain. Explanation was not what the room needed. The truth was too large and too near for a speech. So I did the only thing left, and I am not sure I decided to do it. I began, very quietly, to sing.

An old hymn, the kind he would have known his whole life, the kind his mother probably sang him. And the strangest thing happened. His hand, which had not moved in an hour, moved. His fingers found the rhythm. He could not form the words, but somewhere underneath everything the disease had taken, the song was still there, intact, waiting, older and deeper than the parts of him that were failing. And his daughter, weeping, began to sing it with me, and then his wife, and for a few minutes that room was not a place of dying. It was a place of worship. He passed a day later. His wife told me at the funeral that the singing was the last thing he responded to on this earth.

I have carried a song into every sickroom since. I did not learn this in seminary. The dying taught it to me.

Here is what I have come to understand about why it works, and it goes back further than any of us. There is a story in the old Scriptures about a king named Saul, tormented in his mind, harassed by a darkness that no counsel could reach. And the remedy his servants found for him was not a speech and not a strategy. It was a boy with a harp. They sent for David, and whenever the darkness came upon Saul, David would take up the harp and play, and the Scripture says the king was refreshed, and the tormenting thing departed. Not because the music was magic. Because there is a door in the human soul that words cannot open, that argument cannot force, that only music seems to have the key to, and it stays a working door long after the others have shut.

I have watched that door open in rooms where every other door had closed. The person who no longer knows the names of their own children will sometimes weep at the first line of a hymn from their childhood. The one who has stopped responding to voices will turn their head toward a melody. It is one of the mercies God has built into us that music is stored somewhere so deep that dementia cannot fully reach it, that a stroke cannot fully take it, that even dying cannot altogether silence it. When the disease has stripped a person down to almost nothing, the song is often part of the almost-nothing that remains. It is nearly the last of us to go. I think that is not an accident. I think God laid the song down in the deepest vault of the soul on purpose, so that when everything else is gone He would still have a way in.

This is worship as pastoral care, and I want to insist on the phrase, because we have made worship into a thing that mostly happens on Sundays with a band. But the oldest work of a song is not to fill a full room on a Sunday. It is to go where words cannot, and sit with the one who is suffering, and carry the presence of God across the last threshold. When I bring a hymn into a hospital room, I am not performing and I am not there to fix the diagnosis. I am doing what David did for Saul. I am using the one key that still fits the lock, to let some light into a room the darkness had closed.

And there is a mystery underneath even this, that I hold gently because it is holy. The Scriptures say something about God that I did not believe the first time I read it, that the Lord your God is in your midst, and that He rejoices over you, and that He will quiet you with His love, and that He exults over you with singing. God singing. Over you. Not you singing to Him, Him singing over you, the way a mother sings over a crib in the dark. I used to think that was only a beautiful image. Now, after enough bedsides, I think it is the most literal thing in the world. When I sing over a dying man and the room fills with a peace no one carried in, I do not think I am the only one singing. I think I am joining a song that was already going on over that bed, that had been going on his whole life, that he is about to hear at last without any interference at all.

So let me say this to you, wherever this finds you. If you are the one sitting at a bedside right now, out of words, wrung dry, sure that you have nothing left to offer the person you love. You have more than you think. You do not need the right speech. You almost never did. Sing to them. Sing the old song, the childhood one, the one that lives below the reach of the disease. Do not worry that your voice is not good; the room does not need it to be good, it needs it to be theirs. You will be doing the most ancient pastoral work there is, older than sermons, older than steeples, the work of a boy with a harp in a dark king's chamber, carrying light through the one door still open.

And if you are the one in the bed, or on your way to it, afraid of the silence at the end, hear the thing I have staked my life on believing. The song you learned as a child is still in you, deeper than the fear, deeper than the failing, in a vault nothing in this world can break into and steal. It will be there when the words are gone. And the God who put it there did not do so to leave you alone with it. He has been singing over you since before you had a name, and when the last of your own strength to sing runs out, His does not.

The music does not end at the bedside. It only, finally, gets quiet enough for you to hear who has been singing over you the whole time.
