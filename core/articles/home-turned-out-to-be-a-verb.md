On my last night in that apartment I slept on the floor, because the bed was already gone.

The rooms were empty and enormous the way rooms only get when you're leaving them. The radiator clicked in the corner like a metronome keeping time for nothing. There was a suitcase by the door waiting for morning, and there was the doorframe with the faint height-marks still penciled into it from a season I could barely remember starting. And lying there on the bare hardwood at 11:47, I felt the whole place saying the years back to me. Every corner held a chapter I had never given a name. That night is the entire album. I wrote it as a thank-you note to a building, and by the end, I understood I had gotten the whole thing backwards.

## What "The Apartment" Actually Means

For most of the record I sing to the apartment like it is the hero of the story, the keeper of my memories, the witness to who I was becoming.

And it was. The walls don't talk, but the walls were listening the whole time. "The Kitchen Knew" is about the tile with the chip by the fridge and the knife that learned my grip, the place I burned a year of toast and slowly became someone who could host a table, *every breakfast made me a little more myself.* "The Window Seat" is the faded cushion where I read a thousand evenings and, living alone for the first time, discovered I had become someone who looked forward to her own company. There is "The Kitchen Knew" for the meals that made me, and "The Last Night" for the empty-room gratitude, and a whole song for the bathroom mirror that heard more of my true voice than any stage ever did. The apartment held all of it. It was a real witness to a real becoming, the plaster quietly keeping every chapter I never bothered to name.

But the title is a trick I set for myself, and the record springs it at the very end. Because the last song is not called "The Apartment." It is called "Home Was a Verb."

> The walls don't speak, but the walls remember. And what they remember is not a building. It's who you became inside them.

## The Album Is a Move-Out That Becomes a Revelation

The turn happens in the peak, a song called "The Last Night", the empty-room gratitude, the radiator, the suitcase, the whole place wrapping its arms around the years as I lay on the floor.

And what I realized on that floor is the thing the whole album had been walking toward: I was not losing home. I could not lose it, because it had never been the square footage. Home was everything that *happened* there, the hosting, the healing, the becoming, the being-known by my own four walls. And all of that was leaving with me. It was already packed, invisibly, in the suitcase by the door. So the closing songs stop grieving and start carrying. "Carry the Walls" is exactly what it sounds like, the discovery that the architecture I loved was interior all along. "Next Front Door" turns the whole ache into propulsion, toward a threshold I hadn't crossed yet. And "Home Was a Verb" lands the plane: home is not a place you live. It is a thing you *do*, a way of loving, tending, becoming, and being-with. You don't leave it behind. You take it with you and you build it again.

## The Verse Under the Floorboards

Here is the Scripture the whole album is quietly resting on, and it reframes every move any of us will ever make.

The people of faith in the Bible were, almost to a person, tent-dwellers. Scripture says Abraham lived in tents his whole life, in a land he'd been promised but never fully possessed, and it explains why: he was looking forward to *a city with foundations, whose designer and builder is God.* It goes on to say that the faithful confessed they were strangers and pilgrims on the earth, people seeking a homeland, longing for a better country, a heavenly one. That is the theology under this whole album. The reason no apartment ever feels permanent, the reason every home is a little bit temporary, is not a design flaw. It is a homing signal. We were built to carry home and to seek Home at the same time.

And there's a line **Yeshua** spoke on the night before He died, to friends about to lose everything familiar: *in my Father's house are many rooms, and I go to prepare a place for you.* He did not promise them a fixed address. He promised them a place being prepared, a belonging that no move-out could cancel. An old psalm says it plainly too, *unless the LORD builds the house, those who build it labor in vain.* The walls I loved were real, and they witnessed real things. But the One who was actually building me was **Yahuah**, and He does not leave His work in the drywall when the lease ends. He carries it, carries *you*, to the next front door, and the next, all the way to a Home with foundations that finally cannot be left.

> Every home is a little temporary on purpose. The ache to belong somewhere permanent is a homing signal pointing at a house being prepared for you.

## Who I Made This For

I made *The Apartment* for anyone standing in an empty room on a last night, afraid they're losing the place that made them.

For the person packing boxes with grief in their chest. For the one who thinks that when they close this door, the version of themselves that lived here disappears. For everyone who has confused an address with a home and is scared of the next threshold. And for the pilgrim in all of us who has always felt, underneath every move, a little bit like a stranger looking for a country.

You are not losing home. It was never the building. Everything that made those walls holy, the becoming, the hosting, the being-known, is already in the suitcase, already inside you, ready to be built again at the next front door. So thank the room, and then carry it out with you. Home turned out to be a verb the whole time. And the One who has been building you never once leaves His work behind. He's preparing a place with foundations, and He'll walk you all the way there.

Home was a verb. Carry the walls.
