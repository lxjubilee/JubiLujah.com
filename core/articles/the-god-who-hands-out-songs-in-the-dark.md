There is a line in the book of Job that almost no one quotes, and I have come to think it is one of the most tender things in the whole Bible.

It is not spoken by Job. It comes from one of the younger men circled around him in the ash-heap, trying to make sense of a suffering too big for any of them. And in the middle of his speech he lets slip a description of God so gentle it does not fit the argument he is making. He asks: where is *Eloah my Maker*, who **gives songs in the night**?

Read it slowly, because every word is carrying weight.

Not the God who *ends* the night. Not the God who *explains* the night, or shortens it, or tells you what it was for. The God who *gives you a song in it.* Who reaches into the black hour where you are still very much awake and very much afraid, and presses a melody into your hands that was not there a moment before.

I did not know God did that until I needed Him to. And then I found out that Israel had known it all along, and had a word for it.

## The word for a night-song

The Hebrew here is *zemirot*, songs. It comes from the root *zamar*, which is one of the great music-words of the Scriptures: to make melody, to pluck the strings, to sing praise with an instrument in your hands. When the Psalms say *I will sing praises*, over and over, in every mood, that is often this word. *Zamar.* Music made on purpose, plucked out into the air.

And Job's young friend attaches it to *laylah*, the night.

Put them together and you have something startling. The songs Yahuah gives are not only for the bright temple courts at noon, with the choirs assembled and the harvest in. He is, this line insists, a *giver of night-music*, a God who composes specifically for the dark, who has melodies that only work at all when the lights have gone out.

> He does not merely permit songs in the night. He *gives* them. The melody in your worst hour is a gift, handed down, not something you had to manufacture to prove your faith.

That word, *gives*, is the whole comfort. Because when the dark is genuinely dark, you cannot make a song. You know this if you have been there. In the deep of a grief, there is nothing in you to compose *with*. The well is dry. And a faith that told you *you must summon praise from your own strength at 3 a.m.* would be a cruelty, not a comfort.

But Job's friend does not say *sing a song in the night*. He says God *gives* one. It comes down. It arrives from outside your empty hands. All you have to do, all you *can* do, is receive it and let it be sung through you.

## By day His steadfast love, by night His song

There is another place this exact idea appears, and the two verses lean on each other like they were written to be read together.

In one of the Psalms, a man who is drowning in his own sorrow, the same song where he says his tears have been his food, where he asks God why He has forgotten him, stops and says something almost defiant. *By day Yahuah commands His steadfast love, and by night His song is with me.*

Look at the pairing. The daylight has the *chesed*, the covenant kindness you can see, the mercies you can point to when the sun is up. But the night gets its own provision, and it is a *song*. His song. *With* him. In the exact stretch of hours when the evidence goes quiet and the arguments fail and the enemy's questions get loudest, this man is not left with silence. He is left with a melody God put there for the watch.

That is not a man who felt like singing. That is a man being *carried* by a song he did not write, through a night he did not choose.

I love that the two places agree, one in the oldest book of the Bible, one in the middle of the songbook. Both of them are saying the same quiet, enormous thing: **the God of Scripture keeps different music for the dark.** He is not only the God of your celebrations. He is the God of your night-watch, and He does not send you into it empty-handed.

## What the night-song is for

Here is what took me years to understand, and I want to hand it to you so it does not take you as long.

The night-song is not given to *lift* the night. If it were, it would only ever be a failed sunrise, and you would judge it by whether the darkness left, and it usually does not leave on schedule. The night-song has a different job.

It is given to keep you *company* until the watch is over.

A song in the night does not argue with the dark. It does not pretend the dawn is closer than it is. It does something better. It accompanies you. It reminds your own body, from the inside, that you are not alone in here, that Someone is awake with you, that the God who could have sent an explanation sent a *melody* instead because He knew a melody could go where an explanation cannot. An explanation talks *at* your fear. A song sits *down inside* it and holds on.

And there is a strange grace in the fact that night-songs are usually quiet. You do not belt them. You barely sing them above a breath. But that is not weakness, that is the sound of worship made for a room where everyone else is asleep and the only audience is the One who gave you the tune. The quietest song you will ever sing may be the truest, because there is no one there to sing it *for* except Him.

## So keep your ear open on the watch

If you are in one of those nights right now, the kind that does not end when the clock says morning. I am not going to tell you it will be short. Job's friend did not lie to him about the dark, and I will not lie to you.

But I will tell you what he told a suffering man in the ashes: your Maker *gives* songs in the night. He has one for this one. You do not have to be strong enough to compose it. You do not have to feel like singing. You only have to keep the ear of your heart open, because a melody is coming to you from outside the dark, put there on purpose, by a God who stays up through every watch and never once leaves His people to face the black hours without a song.

Listen for it. It is already on its way down to you.

And in the morning, whenever the morning finally comes. You will realize you were being sung *through* the whole time.
