For years I was waiting for a miracle big enough that I couldn't argue with it.

Something that parted a sea. Something with a before and an after so obvious that even the cynical part of me would have to sit down. I think a lot of us are waiting like that, for the sign to be loud enough, undeniable enough, so we can finally relax and believe our lives are being watched over. And while I was scanning the horizon for the enormous thing, I was walking straight past a hundred small ones a day.

The album turned when I finally slowed down at a kitchen window on a Tuesday. The coffee worked the first time. The chipped mug my grandmother gave me was warm in both hands. A bird on the wire outside was singing for nobody in particular. And it hit me that this, *this*, was the miracle, and it had been going invisible for years because it was too quiet to trip my alarm. I wrote the first line right there: *the miracles ain't the parted seas. They're the parted clouds on Tuesday.*

## What "Ordinary Miracles" Actually Means

The two words look like they cancel each other out. A miracle, by definition, is the extraordinary thing. So calling a miracle "ordinary" sounds like a downgrade.

It is not. It is the whole point. The bridge of the title track is the thesis of the entire record: **the miracles are ordinary, that's what makes them miracles.** The wonder was never that grace shows up in the rare, cinematic moment. The wonder is that it saturates the boring middle of a Tuesday. It is in the sip that lands exactly right. It is in the text from your sister at 4:42 that just says "love you" with no agenda. It is in a nap that lasts the exact right thirty-three minutes. Grace is not rationed for emergencies. It is poured out on the routine, which means most of it is landing on people too busy to notice.

> The wonders aren't hidden. They're sitting right beside you. You just have to slow the scroll enough to see them.

## The Album Is Twelve Ways of Paying Attention

I built the record like a two-year practice of noticing, because that is exactly what it took me.

"Small Things Counting" is about the voicemail you saved for two years from the friend who's gone now, the houseplant that survived eight moves. "If You're Lookin'" is about the grocery cashier who knows your name and your bread, the neighbor's wind chimes, the cardinals at the feeder every single winter. "Tuesday Morning Coffee" holds the softest line I've ever let myself sing out loud: *some prayers come in the steam of a mug at 7:14.* I believe that. I think a grateful breath over a warm cup is a real prayer, even when you don't have the words for it.

Then the record earns its tears. "The Kind Stranger" is about the man at the post office who held the door with no eye contact and re-set the whole tone of my afternoon without ever knowing it, the small holy way a good deed you can never repay just quietly changes the weather of a day. "My Sister's Laugh" is about a two-note cackle that fills a whole kitchen and three generations laughing at the same line, the safest sound I know. And the heart of the album is "The Dog Who Knew", the true-shaped story of a dog named Maple who came over slow, sat down, and put her chin on my knee for forty-seven minutes by the kitchen clock, on the exact day a hard phone call was waiting on the counter. She knew before I did. Some small loves arrive in fur and get there before the words do.

## The Verse Sitting Right Beside You

Here is the Scripture the whole album is quietly leaning on, though I kept it under the porch boards where you'd feel it before you saw it.

**Yeshua** did not point His anxious friends toward a spectacle. He pointed them at birds. He told them to look at the wildflowers, that not one of them toils or worries, and yet they are clothed better than kings. He was teaching a way of seeing: that the ordinary, unbothered creatures are a daily sermon on how carefully the Father tends what He loves. The album's whole "consider the small things" posture is stolen straight from that. And when the record tells you to give thanks for the Tuesday coffee and the backyard sunset, it is echoing the old instruction to give thanks in every circumstance, not because every circumstance is good, but because gratitude is how you finally start seeing.

The twist arrives in track seven, "Counting My Way Home." For two years I had been listing three small wonders a night in a journal, thinking I was the one doing the counting. And late one Friday it turned over on me: I had it backwards. *Been counting blessings, turns out the blessings were counting me.* That is the oldest song in the book made new, the promise that goodness and mercy don't just decorate your life, they *follow* you, all the days of it, walking you home whether you're keeping the tally or not.

> I thought I was collecting the wonders. The whole time, the wonders were collecting me, walking me home.

## Who I Made This For

I made *Ordinary Miracles* for the person still scanning the horizon for a sign big enough to trust.

For anyone who is grateful in theory but too rushed to feel it. For the one who almost threw away the handwritten note from the woman next door. For the family that hums the same tune over the same Sunday pot without realizing they are keeping a three-generation flame alive. For everyone who has been quietly afraid that their small, unspectacular life is beneath heaven's notice.

It is not. The last song is called "Front-Porch Hallelujah," and it is a praise that *doesn't quite scream*, a hallelujah for the small, sung from a porch at 6:47 with a screen door cracked and a dog on the boards. That is the kind of worship I trust most. You do not need the parted sea. Look at the parted clouds. Look at your sister's laugh, your Tuesday mug, the chin on your knee. **Yahuah** is not saving His attention for your emergencies. He is already in your ordinary, and it is full of Him.

Slow down. Look real soft, real slow. They're right beside you.
