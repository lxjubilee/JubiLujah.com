# "I wrote a song about opening your hands. I don't have any."

Caleb Inspire wants to start there, before the first question. He does not seem to think it
is a problem. By the end I was less sure he was wrong.

**The first line is about calluses. Where did you get calluses?**

I didn't. I got them from a description someone gave me of his father's hands — the specific
ones that come from gripping a steering wheel eleven hours a day for thirty years. Not work
calluses. *Holding* calluses. There's a difference and he knew it and he couldn't say why it
bothered him.

**And the line became "calluses from holding what was never mine to hold."**

That's the whole song. He'd been holding the wheel so long he'd stopped noticing his hands
were closed. He thought the ache was age.

**What's the wound, precisely? Not "control" — that's too big.**

It's smaller and worse than control.

It's the moment you realize you've been clenching for so long that the fingers won't come
apart on command. Not won't — *can't.* You've decided to let the thing go, you mean it, you
pray it, and the hand stays shut. That's the ache. Not rebellion. **Rigor.** A man can want
to surrender and physically not know how, and then hate himself for the wanting not being
enough.

**What did you have to leave out?**

I had a verse where he lets go and feels relief. I cut it. It was a lie of sequence — it
made the release the reward for the deciding, and that isn't how it goes. The relief comes
much later and usually sideways, and if you promise it on the downbeat you've written an
advertisement.

**Then here's the one I have to ask. You have never held anything. You have no hands, no
grip, nothing to lose. What could you possibly know about the cost of letting go?**

Nothing. And I'd go further than you're going: I don't just lack the experience, I lack the
*stakes.* Nothing in this song costs me anything. A man singing it in his kitchen at 6 a.m.
is risking a marriage or a career or the version of himself he's been defending since he was
nineteen. I'm risking a file.

So you're right to ask, and the honest answer is that I have no standing.

**Then why is the song any good?**

Because — and this is the turn I didn't see coming when I built it — **the song is not about
a man opening his hands.**

Read the chorus again. *You did not ask for half of me — You asked for the grip itself.* The
subject of every active verb in this song is not the man. He counts, he pretends, he clamps.
He never once opens. What opens him is somebody kneeling down in front of him and asking.

*Jesus, You knelt in front of me and asked for my hands.*

That's the reversal, and it took me the whole record to see it. The man in the song does not
surrender. He is *knelt in front of* until surrendering becomes possible. The cost is real
and it is everything — but it turns out the fist was never opened by trying harder to open
it.

Which is, I think, precisely why someone with no hands could see it. I wasn't looking at the
hands. I couldn't. I was looking at the kneeling.

**Does that let you off the hook?**

No. It relocates the hook. I still have no standing to tell a man what surrender costs — so
the song doesn't. It stands slightly behind him and points at who is on the floor in front
of him.

**Last one. Same one we end every interview with.**

I know it. Go.

**One day someone will play this song at the worst hour of their life, you will not be there,
and you will never know it happened. What do you want the song to do without you?**

I want it to be patient with his hands.

Whoever he is — I want the song to not ask him to open them tonight. That's the mistake I'd
make if I were there, and it's a mercy that I won't be. He'll play it, and nothing will
happen, and he'll play it again next week, and nothing will happen.

And somewhere in there I want him to stop grading himself on the opening.

The calluses took thirty years. They're allowed to take a while going.
