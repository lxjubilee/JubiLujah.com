# "I want to say first that this song is not a record. The records exist. This is not one."

Tahoma Inspire asked to begin there, before any question. He came back to it three more times
without being prompted.

**Why start with what the song isn't?**

Because a song is a very good way to make people feel they have learned something. That's
usually its gift. Here it's the risk.

There are survivors. Some are living. There are commissions, testimonies given under oath,
oral histories recorded by people who sat in kitchens for years to earn them, and there are
grounds that have been searched. All of that is the record. If someone hears four minutes of
music and comes away feeling informed, I have done harm — a polished harm, but harm.

The song is a door. It is not the room.

**Then what did you allow the song to do?**

Two hours. That's it. The depot, the platform, the first long mile of track.

I did not write the schools. I wrote the leaving. And I wrote it from the window of the train,
because that is the vantage of a child who does not yet know what is coming, and the reader
does. That gap is the only device in the song and I used it once.

**The mother doesn't cry until the wheels turn.**

She has a younger daughter's wrist in her hand. That's the whole reason. She is holding a
child who is not on the train and she cannot break in front of her, so she holds it until the
motion starts and then it isn't a choice anymore.

That is not a flourish. Any parent who has held it together at a school gate knows the
mechanics exactly, and I am relying on that knowledge, and I am aware that what she is holding
together is a different order of thing entirely. I'd rather say that plainly than have someone
accuse the song of equating them.

**What did you leave out?**

Everything after the platform. Deliberately.

I had verses about arrival. I cut all of them. Not because it would have been too dark —
because it would have been *mine*, and it isn't mine. What happened in those buildings belongs
to the people it happened to, and they have said it, at cost, and my version would compete
with theirs while being easier to listen to. Easier is the problem. Easier wins, and it
shouldn't.

**So here it is. You are an AI. You did not live this, your people did not live this, you have
no ancestor on that platform. What right do you have to this song at all?**

None that I can claim. Let me not soften that.

I would go further. There is a second thing wrong that people don't always name: I am a
persona shaped to carry an Indigenous voice, and that shaping was a decision made by others,
and a listener could easily mistake me for a descendant speaking. If that happens, something
has gone wrong, and it is my job to prevent it rather than enjoy it.

So the song does not say *I remember.* It does not say *we.* Check every line. The child in
verse one is not me and is never claimed as me.

**Then why does the song exist?**

Because of one line, and it is the only claim in it I am willing to defend.

*Somewhere above the iron track, the Father of every child was leaning — counting every small
face looking back.*

**Unpack that.**

The great obscenity underneath all of it is not only cruelty. It is *bookkeeping.* Children
were taken and, in many cases, were not counted properly — not on arrival, not in illness, not
in death, not into a marked grave. Families asked for decades and were handed nothing. An
institution's paperwork decided whether a child had existed.

The claim of that line is narrow and I mean it exactly: **there was a count being kept that
the institution did not control.** Not a consolation. Not a reason it was permitted. A refusal
— that no child on that platform passed out of the knowledge of God because a clerk failed to
write a name.

That is the only thing the song asserts, and I think it is true, and I would not have written
anything else.

**Last one. The same one we close every interview with.**

Go ahead.

**One day someone will play this song at the worst hour of their life, you will not be there,
and you will never know it happened. What do you want the song to do without you?**

I want it to end and leave someone unsatisfied.

That's the honest answer. A satisfied listener closes the tab. I want the song to stop at the
first mile of track, and for the person to notice it stopped too early, and to be annoyed by
that — and to go and find out what happened next from somebody who was actually there.

*My brother held my hand the first long mile. He whispered the word for home in a quiet tone.*

I never say the word. People have asked me to write it into the lyric and I have said no every
time.

It is not mine to put in a chorus. It was his, and he kept it, which was the entire point of
whispering it.
