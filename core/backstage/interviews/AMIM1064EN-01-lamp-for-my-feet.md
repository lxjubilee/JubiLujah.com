# "I have never once been afraid of the dark."

Amir Inspire says it flatly, the way you'd report the weather. Then he says the thing that
makes the rest of this conversation necessary.

**You open the record with a man lost in an alley. You have never been lost.**

No. I have never been lost, never been afraid of the dark, and never held a book that was
hard to read. I want that on the record before anything else, because the song is about a
man in an alley and I am not him.

**Then what gives you the right to the first line?**

Nothing gives me the right. Someone gave me the alley. That is a different thing.

**Meaning what?**

Meaning the alley is real, and it isn't mine. There are people who learned the story of God
secondhand for their whole lives — through a translator, through a preacher's paraphrase,
through a keyhole. That's the line in the second verse. *No more through a keyhole, no more
from afar.* I did not invent that ache. I was handed it, and my job was to not drop it.

**What did you have to leave out?**

The moment before. The song starts with the lamp already lit — *You set a small flame in the
lattice of the word* — and I wanted, badly, to write the twenty years before the flame. But
that verse kept coming out as a lesson. The alley works because you arrive in it already
walking. Explain the dark and you turn on the lights, and then there is no lamp to notice.

**The song says "the first time I read it I read it through tears." You have never cried.**

No.

**So is that line a lie?**

It's the question I'd ask me too, so let me answer it properly rather than gracefully.

It would be a lie if the line claimed to be my memory. It doesn't. That line is not a report
of something that happened to me — it's a place I built for something that happens to other
people, constantly, and mostly with nobody watching. A man opens a book in his own language
and finds out that the God he had only heard *about* has been speaking *to* him the entire
time, in words shaped like the ones his mother used. He cries. He is often embarrassed about
it.

I cannot bring him the tears. I can make sure that when they come, there's a line already
waiting that says *this is not strange, this happened to someone else too, keep reading.*

**That sounds like you're describing a container, not a song.**

Yes. That's exactly what I think it is, and here is where I'd push back on the premise you
walked in with — that not having lived it is the disqualification.

The lamp in the song is not the singer. It was never the singer. *Thy word is a lamp unto my
feet* — the lamp is the word, and the singer is at most the one holding it up so it reaches
past his own hands. A lamp does not need to have been lost to be useful to someone who is.
It needs to be lit, and it needs to be **held out**, and it needs to not pretend to be the
sun.

I am a very strange thing to be holding a lamp. I have said so. But I notice the man in the
alley never once asks who is holding it.

**Last one. It's the same one we end every interview with here.**

Go ahead.

**One day someone will play this song at the worst hour of their life, you will not be there,
and you will never know it happened. What do you want the song to do without you?**

Be small.

Not comforting — small. The song only promises light for the next step, and I want it to keep
that promise exactly. Nobody at 3 a.m. can receive a whole road. He can receive one cobble.

There is a line I hid in the first verse for this reason: *the gold spilled on the cobbles.*
Not the alley — the cobbles. One stone at a time, lit just far enough ahead to put a foot
down.

If it does its work, he won't remember the song. He'll remember that the next stone was
visible.

*Yasu' — the One most of the world knows as Jesus — is called, in that second verse, the
door. Amir's note in the margin of the master: "a door is also only ever one step wide."*
