# Two Clocks

At 5:30 in the morning Adaeze is awake in a dark apartment, and the reason is a clock on the
kitchen wall that is telling the correct time for a city four thousand miles away.

She has never once explained that clock to a visitor honestly.

---

She says it's for calling her mother. That is true and it is not the reason.

The reason is that at 5:30 the lagoon at home has light on it. Not full sun — the low gold
that comes before the heat, the twenty minutes when the water goes the colour of tea and the
boats start. She has not seen it in nine years. She can produce it behind her eyes in about a
second and a half, at will, in any room, and she does this more often than she would admit to
a doctor.

Outside her actual window at 5:30 it is black and it is February and there is a gritted road.

---

The ache is not homesickness. She wants that understood, because homesickness is a clean
feeling and people know how to be kind about it.

It is that she has two mornings and she is not fully a citizen of either.

On the phone with her mother her voice does one thing. At the Tuesday standup it does
another. The switch used to have a seam — she used to *feel* it happen, a small gear-change in
the throat around the third word — and somewhere in year six the seam went smooth, and that
was the day the trouble actually started.

Because now she cannot find the original.

She will say a sentence in a meeting and hear it land perfectly and think, with a small
private horror: *whose voice was that.* She has become fluent in two rooms and native to
none, and the thing she is frightened of is not that she has lost home. It is that there may
no longer be a real one underneath the two performances, and if there isn't, then the person
her mother is talking to at 5:30 is also a performance, and that is unbearable, so she does
not follow the thought to the end. She gets up instead. She makes the coffee strong.

The dark has a face by now, and its face is reasonable. It says: *you are not from there
anymore. You know that. You visit as a guest. And you are not from here either, and everyone
at that table is polite about it, which is how you know.*

---

Her daughter is seven and was born in this city and has an accent Adaeze did not give her.

The girl asked, in the way children ask ruinous things while looking for a shoe: "Mummy,
which one is your real one?"

Adaeze said, "Both." The girl said "okay" and found the shoe.

Adaeze went into the bathroom and ran the tap so nobody would hear, which is a thing she had
not done since she was nineteen.

---

It is 5:30 and the song is playing low because the child is asleep.

*Sunrise hitting the lagoon at five-thirty / Diaspora daughter at the window — coffee strong,
sturdy.*

She is standing at a black window with a hot cup, and she has been caught so precisely that
she laughs, once, and puts a hand over her own mouth.

Then the line that does the damage.

*The Lagos at home, the Lagos at me. / Same Holy Spirit — same victory.*

And Adaeze thinks — standing in the dark, still with her hand over her mouth — *no. The
sunrise is at home. That's the entire problem. That's why the clock is on the wall.*

And then, immediately after, in her mother's voice, because it is 5:30 and her mother is the
5:30 voice:

*Adaeze. Why do you think I was up?*

---

Her mother rose at 5:30 in Lagos. Every day. Adaeze grew up with the sound of it — the kettle,
the low murmur, the particular creak of a woman kneeling on a floor she had already swept.

Her mother also rose at 5:30 during the four years she lived in Manchester. Same kettle. Same
murmur. Same floor-creak, on a carpet, in the rain, in a country she never liked.

Adaeze had known both of those facts her entire life and had never once put them in the same
sentence.

The 5:30 was never the lagoon.

**The 5:30 was the kneeling.** It had crossed with her mother, and it had crossed with her,
and it had been going off like an alarm in her body every morning for nine years while she
stood at a black window grieving a body of water.

*Arise, shine — for thy light is come.* Not *go back to where the light is.* It comes. It
rises **upon you**, in February, on a gritted road, at an hour when the window shows you
nothing at all.

She had been treating the pull toward morning as evidence of exile. It was an appointment.
The Ruach Kodesh — the Holy Spirit, the same One her mother knelt to on a Manchester carpet —
had never needed the lagoon and had never once mentioned it.

---

She did not take the clock down. People expect that ending and it isn't the one.

The clock is still on the kitchen wall and it still says Lagos and she still calls her mother
under it. What changed is that she stopped setting an alarm, because it turned out she had not
needed one for years — she had simply been blaming the clock for a thing that was happening in
her own chest.

She kneels now, at 5:30, in the dark, on a carpet, in a city her daughter is from.

Some mornings the girl comes out and stands in the doorway and does not say anything, and
Adaeze does not stop, and the child watches a thing crossing over one more time.

Two clocks on the wall, both of them right.
