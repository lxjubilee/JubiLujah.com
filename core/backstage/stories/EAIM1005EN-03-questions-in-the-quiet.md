# What Her Father Said at 1 a.m.

He said four words and then he sat down on the kitchen floor, which she had never once seen
him do.

---

Nadia was nine and Callum from two doors down was nine and then Callum was not anything, and
there had been a service with too many small children in it and a woman singing badly, and
Nadia had held her mother's hand throughout and had asked nothing at all.

She waited eleven days. Then she came downstairs at one in the morning on a school night,
which she was not allowed to do, and found her father at the table doing the thing with the
bills.

And she asked him whether Callum was in hell.

She knew what she was asking. That's what he understood in about half a second and it is why
he did not manage a reflex. She'd been at that church her whole life. She had heard, in a
children's talk, from a man who meant nothing by it, a clean summary of who goes where and on
what basis. And she had done the arithmetic on a boy she had known since she was four, whose
family had never gone anywhere on a Sunday, and she had arrived at an answer, and she had been
carrying it alone for eleven days.

**The wound is not grief. It's a nine-year-old doing theology by herself at night and getting
somewhere unbearable, and correctly intuiting that the adults would tidy it if she said it out
loud.**

---

Her father was not a subtle man. He put up shelves, he coached under-11s, he had never in his
life said anything to her that could be described as pastoral.

He looked at her for a while. She has said he looked frightened, which she had also never
seen.

Then: **"I don't know, love."**

And then — and this is the bit she cannot explain to people without it sounding like nothing —
he got up, and he poured two glasses of milk, and instead of taking them to the table he sat
down on the kitchen floor with his back against the cupboards and put hers beside him on the
tile.

So she sat down on the floor too.

---

He said a few more things over the next hour and she remembers almost none of them, which she
finds funny now, because he was clearly working very hard.

She remembers that he did not say *God is good* in the voice that means the conversation is
over.

She remembers that he said, at one point, "I've asked that. About my dad," and then didn't
elaborate, and that it was the first time she had ever considered that her father had a
question in him.

And she remembers that when she said, in the small flat voice of a child testing a floorboard,
*so you don't actually know if it's all true* — he said no. Not *of course I do.* Not *don't
say that.* He said: *no. I don't know it the way I know this floor. I've decided it, and I
keep deciding it, and some weeks that's all it is.*

She is nine years old sitting on cold tiles at two in the morning and she is finding out that
the grown-ups are also in the dark and have not left.

That's the whole inheritance. It arrived in about ninety seconds and neither of them noticed.

---

She is thirty-one now and she does still believe it, which she says with a shrug rather than a
flourish, and she is aware that the shrug is the point.

She has met a lot of people since who lost it in their twenties, and she has noticed a pattern
she is careful about stating too confidently: an enormous number of them were given answers
early, and the answers were airtight, and then one of them failed — and because the structure
had been sold as seamless, the failure of one panel took the whole wall.

Nobody had ever told her the wall was seamless. Her father had told her, on a floor, at nine,
that he was holding it up by deciding to.

*My father couldn't answer all the questions that I asked, but he showed me how to seek.*

She heard that line in a car and had to pull over, which she reports with some irritation,
because she does not consider herself the sort of person who pulls over.

---

He died four years ago. She has his shelves in her house and they are extremely well made.

She has a daughter now who is six and has not yet asked anything difficult, and Nadia has
thought a great deal about what she'll do when it comes, and she has settled on a plan that is
not really a plan.

She isn't going to have an answer ready. She's decided that on purpose.

She's just going to make sure there's milk in the fridge, and that she gets down on the floor
first, because the floor is what she remembers — not one word of the theology, forty seconds of
a man deciding that the correct place to be was down there with her, at her height, in the
dark, for as long as it took.
