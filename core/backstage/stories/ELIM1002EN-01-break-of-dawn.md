# The 0600

The rule on the tower was that you called in at six, and June had gone eleven weeks without
once wanting to.

---

Fire lookout is a job that mostly consists of nothing happening correctly.

You are alone on a mountain in a glass box on stilts. You sweep the ridge with binoculars.
You log the weather. Nothing burns, which is the desired outcome, and the days accumulate.
The isolation is not incidental to the work; it *is* the work, and this is precisely why she
had applied.

Because eleven months earlier she had been a person in a town where every single interaction
began with a face. The head-tilt. The soft landing on the vowel. *How are you.* Not the
question — the other one, the one with the weight in it, the one that arrived with a casserole
and an expectation.

**The ache is not the loss. It's the surveillance of the grieving.** Being known, in a small
place, by kind people, all of whom are monitoring your progress and none of whom will say so.
Being unable to have an ordinary Tuesday, or laugh too loud in a shop, without somebody
recalibrating their sense of how you're doing.

She had come up the mountain to be un-monitored. It had worked. And the thing nobody warns you
about is that it keeps working after you'd like it to stop.

---

The 0600 was the one exception. Every morning, radio in, confirm you are alive, report
conditions. Thirty seconds with a dispatcher named Ray who had done it for nineteen years and
whose entire personality on the air was *copy that.*

She had come to dread it, and could not have told you why, and the not-knowing-why had begun
to worry her more than the dreading.

---

That night there was no fire, which is important. Nothing happened. She simply did not sleep,
in the specific way where you do not decide not to, and at some point around three she stopped
pretending and sat in the chair facing the black window with the lamp going down and let the
thing come.

She has not described what came and this account is not going to invent it.

She sat in the dark for the rest of the night with her hands in her lap. That's the sum total
of the event. Anyone watching would have seen a woman in a chair.

---

The dark on a mountain has a face and its face is arithmetic. It says: *nothing you do up here
changes anything. The ridge does not need you. You are being paid to watch a thing that is not
happening. If you went off this platform tonight the sun would come up on schedule and the
trees would be exactly as unburnt.*

It's not wrong. That's the trouble with it. Every clause of it is true.

---

At about twenty past five the window changed.

Not dramatically. The black went to a very dark blue that was not quite black, along one edge
of one ridge, and then the ridge had an outline where a minute earlier it had been a guess.

June is sitting in a cold glass box with her hands in her lap and she is watching the day
begin and she is thinking, with total clarity and no comfort in it at all: *I did not do
anything to deserve this and it is happening anyway.*

And then, immediately after, before she can stop it: *that's the whole of it, isn't it.*

Because the arithmetic in the dark had been right about everything except the conclusion. It
was true that nothing she did changed the ridge. It was true that the sun did not consult her.
It was true that she was not, in any measurable sense, required.

And the morning came for her anyway. Not as a reward. Not as a result. It was not withheld
pending improvement and it did not check her file first.

*The mercies are new.* She'd sung that phrase in a church for thirty years with the emphasis
on *new,* and it turns out the load-bearing word is the one nobody sings: they are new *every*
morning. Every. Including the ones you spent in a chair. Including the ones where you did
nothing, produced nothing, healed by no measurable increment, and could not have told a single
person why you were dreading a thirty-second radio call.

---

At six she picked up the handset.

"Tower four, morning, Ray."

"Copy that, tower four. Conditions?"

And she said — and she reports being as surprised as he was — "Clear. Really clear, actually.
You should see this."

There was a pause on the line of about two seconds, which for Ray was practically a speech.

"...Copy that, tower four."

---

She did the season out and came down in October and she did go home, eventually, to the town
with the casseroles, and she says it is still difficult and that people still tilt their heads.

The handset thing stayed with her though. She has thought about it a lot.

For eleven weeks she had answered that call because the rule said to. On the last morning of
the eleventh week she answered it because she had something she wanted to tell somebody, and
the only somebody available was a man saying *copy that* forty miles away, and it was enough,
and she has since decided it was never really about Ray.

There is a version of that morning where she doesn't pick up. She knows exactly what it looks
like. She had rehearsed it.

The light came up on the ridge either way. That's the part she cannot get over — and the part
she says she'd want anybody still sitting in the chair to hear.

It was not coming because she reached for the handset.

She reached for the handset because it was coming.
