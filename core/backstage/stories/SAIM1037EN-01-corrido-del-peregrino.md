# The Corrido He Never Wrote

Don Aurelio knew three hundred and eleven corridos and had never written one about himself,
and on a Thursday in the plaza a boy of about nineteen asked him why.

---

He had an answer ready. He'd had it ready for years, which should have told him something.

"Un corrido is for a man who did something," he said. "I sit down."

The boy laughed and put a coin in the case and went off toward the church steps, and Don
Aurelio played *Corrido de Gabino Barrera* for a family from out of town, and did not think
about it again until roughly two in the morning, when he thought about very little else.

---

Here is what a corrido is for, in case you have only heard them as party music.

A corrido keeps a name. That is the whole function. Before there were records in the county
office there were men with instruments who could tell you who your great-grandfather was and
what he did at the river crossing and whether he was brave, and the melody was the filing
system. You sing a man so the town cannot misplace him.

Don Aurelio had kept three hundred and eleven names. Vaqueros, a schoolteacher, two men who
were probably not as heroic as the songs allowed, a girl who drowned in 1961 and whose mother
paid him in bread for eleven years. He had carried other people's grandfathers on his back
across six decades.

And the ache — the one that arrived at two in the morning and would not leave — was this:

**Nobody was going to sing him.**

Not out of cruelty. Simply because he had not *done* anything. He had stayed. He had married
Lucía and buried Lucía. He had played at the same three plazas. He had never crossed, never
gone north, never had the one night that becomes a verse. He was the instrument, not the
subject. The man who keeps the archive does not appear in the archive.

He was seventy-one years old and he had begun to understand that he would end as a gap.

---

There is a worn place on a bajo quinto where the forearm rests. On his it had gone through
the finish and into the wood, a pale patch about three fingers wide, the shape of nothing in
particular.

He had always looked at that patch as the proof. Thirty-one years in one chair. A man who
travels does not wear a hole in one spot.

---

The Thursday after the boy, an old woman he half knew stopped in front of him and asked for
something for her husband, who had died on the Tuesday.

He asked what the man had done.

"Nada," she said, and she said it without apology, the way you state a fact about weather.
"He worked at the yard. He came home. Forty-six years." Then: "He was never comfortable here.
Not once. He used to say to me — *Elena, ya casi.* Almost. Every year. Almost."

*Almost.* Almost what?

She shrugged. "He never said. I stopped asking. I thought it was the town he didn't like." A
pause. "It wasn't the town."

---

Don Aurelio is sitting in the plaza with his hands on the strings and he is not playing, and
he can feel the worn place under his forearm the way you feel a scar you've stopped noticing.

Forty-six years in a yard, and the man was never comfortable.

Not restless. Not ungrateful. Just — never once persuaded that this was the address.

And Don Aurelio thinks: *Ya casi.* He thinks of Abraham, who went out not knowing where he
was going, and it strikes him for the first time in seventy-one years that the sentence does
not say Abraham arrived. He thinks of the verse the padre reads at every funeral in that
town, the one about strangers and pilgrims on the earth, and how he had heard it four hundred
times as a consolation for the dead.

It is not a consolation. It is a job description.

A pilgrim is not a man who traveled far.

**A pilgrim is a man who never once agreed that where he was standing was home.**

---

He wrote it that night, for the husband, and it was not a good corrido — no river crossing,
no rifle, no brave last words. Four verses about a man who went to a yard for forty-six years
and said *almost* to his wife every year and never explained.

He played it at the funeral. Elena did not cry until the third verse, and then she did, hard,
and afterward she held his hands and said the thing he did not expect:

"You knew him."

He had not known him. He had met him twice.

---

He is still in the plaza. He is seventy-two now. He has begun, quietly, on the corridos of
people who did nothing — the yard man, a woman who cleaned the clinic, a boy who was gentle
with animals and died at twenty of something stupid. There are nine so far. Nobody has asked
for a single one of them.

He does not expect anyone to sing him either. He has stopped minding, and he cannot fully
explain why, except that he no longer thinks of the pale patch on the wood as proof that he
stayed.

Three fingers wide, worn through the finish and into the grain, from thirty-one years of the
same forearm in the same place.

It is not the mark of a man who never went anywhere.

It is the mark of a man who carried something the whole way.
