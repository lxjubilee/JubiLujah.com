# The Notch

The rope had a notch in it, and Tirzah cut the second one on the morning she stopped
believing.

---

You cut a notch so you know when to stop turning.

That was the whole of it. Forty-one turns of the windlass and the bucket kissed water; you
felt it more than heard it, a small give in the handle like a held breath let go. Her
grandmother had cut the first notch before Tirzah was born, worn smooth now, a pale eye in
the hemp that your thumb found without looking.

Forty-one turns. Her whole life, forty-one.

In the third summer without rain it went to forty-four. She told no one. She cut the second
notch herself, low and clean, and she did not look at it while she cut it.

---

Understand what a well is in a village like that. It is not water. Water you can carry in
from the wadi if you are willing to walk and willing to be seen walking.

A well is the answer to the question *can we stay.*

So nobody discussed the well. They discussed the goats and the roof and whether the widow's
boy would marry, and every single one of them knew the number of turns that morning, and not
one of them said it out loud, and that is the particular silence Tirzah could not stand — the
whole village listening for a sound none of them would name.

The drought had a face by then. It was not the sky. It was the merchant from the coast road
who came twice that summer and priced their land both times, kindly, and got a little kinder
each visit.

Forty-four. Then forty-six. Then, in the week of the first cold night, fifty-one, and the
sound at the bottom was not a kiss. It was a knock.

---

She went down at dark so no one would see her carry the second bucket, the small one, the one
you use when you have decided the big one is a lie.

Fifty-one turns. Fifty-four. Fifty-eight.

She is turning the handle and she is not counting anymore, she is just turning, and her arms
are burning and her face is wet and she is furious in a way she has no words for, because
her grandmother's notch is up there in the dark being a promise that nobody kept —

and the handle goes soft.

Not at the bottom. Higher. Higher than fifty-one, higher than forty-four. Her hands know it
before she does; the give comes early and wrong and she nearly lets go of the windlass.

She hauls up a full bucket from a well that had been failing for three summers.

---

The old woman who found her sitting there at moonrise did not ask why she was crying, which
was a kindness, and did not help her up, which was a greater one.

She only put two fingers on the rope, at the pale worn eye of the first notch, and said:

"Your mother's people had a cistern. Do you remember it? Under the house."

Tirzah remembered. A stone belly in the dark. You filled it in the wet months and you spent
it in the dry ones, and when it was empty it was empty, and that was arithmetic, and
arithmetic is honest and merciless and cannot be argued with.

"A cistern gives you back what you put in it," the old woman said. "You have been counting
this well like a cistern. Forty-one turns of your own strength and then the water, and when
the number rose you thought you were being charged more."

She let go of the rope.

"Child. This is not a cistern. It has never once been a cistern. There is a spring down
there, and the spring does not consult you, and the rain has nothing to do with it, and it
was rising toward you the entire time you were weeping about the number."

---

Tirzah did not cut a third notch.

She went down the next morning at the hour the others drew, in front of everyone, and she
took the big bucket, and she did not count. She turned the handle and let the depth be
whatever the depth was, and when the give came she laughed out loud — a short, undignified,
embarrassing sound in a courtyard full of people who had been listening for a knock.

They all looked at her. Then one of them started turning her own windlass without counting,
and the sound of it went around that courtyard like something catching.

The second notch is still in the rope, low down, where her thumb passes over it every single
morning.

She leaves it there on purpose. Not as a scar and not as a warning.

As the mark of exactly how far down she was willing to go before she found out the water had
been coming up to meet her the whole way.
