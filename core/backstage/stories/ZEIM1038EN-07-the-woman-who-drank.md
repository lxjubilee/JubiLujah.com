# The Pot

Yoela saw the clay pot sitting alone on the well rim and knew, before she knew anything else,
that something had gone wrong in the world.

Because that pot was never set down. Not in eleven years.

---

You have to understand how much work Yoela had put into never meeting that woman.

It was not hatred. Hatred would have been an event, and events end. This was **maintenance.**

She drew at dawn. That was the first thing, and it had been the first thing for so long that
her daughters believed it was piety — *mother rises early* — when in fact it was a schedule
built entirely around one other woman's shame. She knew which path the other one took. She
knew that on the days the sun was cruel she came later, and so on those days Yoela sent a
girl instead. She had, without ever once saying the woman's name aloud in her own house,
organized eleven years of mornings around her.

That was the cost, and Yoela did not see it until much later: she had made a stranger the
axis of her days. And she had gotten something for it, which is the part that is hard to
admit — she had gotten to be **the other kind of woman.** You cannot be the respectable one
in a village unless somebody is available to be the other thing. Yoela had never thanked her
for the service and never would.

So: noon. The hour nobody sane draws water. The pot on the rim with no hands near it.

And a man sitting in the shade on the far side of the well, alone, a stranger, tired.

---

Yoela did what any careful woman does. She stepped back behind the fig and she waited to see
what she was looking at.

She heard the woman before she saw her — coming up the path from the town side, and running,
which was wrong. Nobody runs at noon. And she was *talking* — not to anyone, to everyone, to
doorways, to the men at the oil press who had not spoken a civil word to her since before
Yoela's youngest was born.

"Come and see a man who told me everything I ever did."

She said it to Rechav, who had spat on the ground behind her once. She said it to the
brothers at the press. She said it, without stopping, to a girl of nine who did not know why
she was supposed to look away from this woman and only knew that she was.

*Come and see.*

---

Here is the thing Yoela could not get her mind around, standing behind that tree.

If a stranger had said to Yoela *I know everything you ever did* — she would have gone home.
She would have gone home and shut the door and been ill, and she would have spent the rest of
her life making certain that man never spoke to another soul in that town.

Everything you ever did is not good news. Everything you ever did is the worst sentence in
the language.

But that woman had heard it, out loud, from someone who had no business knowing, and she had
not gone home.

She had come *here.* To the oil press. To Rechav. To the exact people who were the reason she
drew water at noon.

Yoela is standing behind a fig tree at the hottest hour of the day and she is working out,
slowly, the way you work out something you have been avoiding for eleven years, that she has
never in her life been that unafraid of anyone.

And that she has never been fully known by anyone, either.

And she is beginning to suspect those are the same fact.

---

The stranger in the shade had not moved. He was not calling out. He was not gathering the
crowd — the crowd was gathering itself, badly, arguing, half of them coming only to see the
woman make a fool of herself.

He was just sitting where he had been sitting, near a well that belonged to Ya'aqov, at an
hour when no one comes.

Waiting, it seemed to Yoela, in a manner she found almost unbearable — like a man who had
picked the hour on purpose. Like the noon and the heat and the empty path had all been the
appointment, and the shame that drove that woman to draw water alone had been, the entire
time, the very thing that put her at the right place.

She thought: *He came at the hour she was hiding.*

---

Yoela did not go and see. Not that day. She wants that written plainly, because the story
people tell has everyone running down the path together and it was not like that; half the
town went and half the town found reasons, and she was in the second half, and she stood
behind a tree until her water jar sweated in her hands.

But she went back at noon the following day.

Alone, in the heat, at the wrong hour, on the path she had spent eleven years not walking —
which meant that anyone who saw her would draw exactly one conclusion about what kind of
woman she had become.

She found she did not mind. That was the strangest part. She had spent her whole life being
the other kind of woman and it came off her on that path like a coat in summer.

The clay pot was still on the rim where it had been left. Nobody had touched it. It had a
chip out of the lip, and Yoela had never been close enough to know that.

She set her own jar down beside it.

Two pots on a well rim at noon, both of them empty, neither of them going home.
