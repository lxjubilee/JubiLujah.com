# The Wick Was New

Marta stood at the top of her mother's cellar stairs for eleven minutes before she put a foot
on the first one, and the reason it took eleven minutes was not the dark.

---

The house had sold. That was the thing driving all of it. Contracts signed, completion in
nine days, and everything above ground already boxed and labelled and handled with the brisk
competence that had carried her through the funeral, the paperwork, the phone calls to
distant cousins, and the conversation with the solicitor about a will that had been drawn up
in 1994 and never once updated.

Everything above ground. She had been in that house four times since February and had not
once opened the cellar door.

She had a good reason and it was a lie. The reason was *there's nothing down there but jars.*

**The ache was this: she had managed the entire death and had not felt one single thing, and
she had begun to be frightened of herself.** Not grief — the absence where grief was supposed
to be. She had watched her brother come apart at the service and had stood beside him
producing tissues and correct sentences, and had gone home and slept eight hours, and had
thought, in the car, with total clarity: *something is wrong with me.*

The cellar was the last unopened room in the house, and she knew perfectly well, in the way
you know things you refuse to say, that it was also the last unopened room in herself.

---

The dark down there had a face and its face was reasonable, the way the worst ones are. It
said: *you didn't feel it because there wasn't anything to feel. You and she were not close.
You performed a daughter for thirty years and now the performance is over and the honest
answer is that you're relieved.*

That was the sentence waiting at the bottom of the stairs. Marta was not frightened of the
cellar. She was frightened of agreeing with it.

---

She'd found the lantern in the scullery — her mother's, kept for power cuts, the wick
replaced at some point and never burned. It lit badly. New wicks do; they haven't yet drawn
the oil up through themselves, and they gutter, and they need a while.

She had wanted a bright, decisive light and she had a flame the size of a thumbnail that
would not hold steady.

She went down anyway.

Cold stone. Bare foot, because she'd left her shoes at the door out of a habit she'd learned
in that house and had never broken in any house since. Second step. Third. The smell that
came up was earth and iron and, underneath, very faintly, vinegar.

---

The jars were there. Of course they were. Four shelves, thirty years deep, the oldest ones
gone dark and unlabelled and the newest ones from a summer her mother would have been
seventy-eight and should not have been doing this.

And on the bottom shelf, at the back, behind them: a shoebox.

Marta is crouched on a stone floor at the foot of the stairs with a lantern that will not
hold still and she is not opening the box. She is looking at the handwriting on the lid. It
says her name. It has said her name down here for an unknown number of years and nobody was
ever told.

And what arrives is not grief either. It's *later.* An overwhelming, physical certainty that
she cannot do this now, that whatever is in that box is going to require a version of herself
she does not currently have access to, and that if she opens it at nine forty on a Tuesday in
a cold cellar she will get it wrong and it will be spent.

She put the lid back. She had not even lifted it fully.

She sat on the bottom step for a long time and nothing came and nothing was resolved and the
lantern kept guttering.

---

Here's what she says about that night now, and it took her a while to find the words for it.

She had expected the dark to be an interrogation. Go down, face it, produce the feeling, come
back up changed — that was the shape she had braced for, and it is the shape most people are
sold.

What actually happened was that she went down, got as far as she could get, and was not
pressed.

Nothing in that cellar demanded she perform a daughter one last time. The box waited. It had
been waiting years already; nine more days was not going to trouble it. And she went back up
the stairs with her hands empty and did not feel like a failure, which was, when she examined
it later, the single strangest thing that had happened to her since February.

*Take your time. I'll wait.* She'd have called that a soft sentiment before. It is not soft.
It is the only kind of patience that is any use to someone who cannot yet feel anything,
because it is the only kind that does not add a second failure on top of the first.

---

She opened the box six weeks after completion, in her own kitchen, in the afternoon, with
nothing scheduled after.

That is a story for somebody else to tell and she does not tell it often.

But she kept the lantern. It sits on a shelf in a flat that has never once lost power, and it
is faintly ridiculous, and she is aware of that.

The wick has burned in now. It took several nights of use. It draws properly and it holds a
steady flame, and it would be a good light if she ever needed one.

It was never going to be a good light on the first night. Nobody told her that at the top of
the stairs, and it turned out not to matter, because the stairs were passable by a bad light
and she had not, in fact, needed to see all the way to the bottom.

Only as far as the next step. The flame was always big enough for that.
