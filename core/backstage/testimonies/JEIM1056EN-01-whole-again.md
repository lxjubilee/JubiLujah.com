# She kept the pill bottle. That part surprises everyone.

She'll tell you why at the end, and it is not the reason you're about to guess.

---

Start with the drawer.

Not the hospital, not the intake forms, not the eleven months — the kitchen drawer, second
one down, the one with the spoons. Eight-fourteen in the morning. Light coming in flat across
the counter the way it does in that hour before anything has gone wrong yet.

She would stand in front of it and rehearse.

That's the part nobody outside puts together. You think the enemy is the craving. The craving
is loud and awful and at least it's honest about what it wants. The thing that actually took
the years was smaller: **the negotiation with her own hand.** Reach slow, brace the wrist
against the counter's edge, take the spoon on the second try if the first one rattles. Do it
before anyone comes downstairs. Do it so nobody has to watch and be kind about it.

Eleven months clean, and she was still a woman who planned her mornings around whether her
hand would embarrass her.

That's the wound. Not the addiction. The *stage-managing.* The exhausting daily performance
of a person who is fine, put on for an audience of one, in an empty kitchen, at 8:14 in the
morning.

---

The song did not arrive at a dramatic hour. It arrived on a Tuesday, out of a phone speaker
on the windowsill, while she was not listening to it.

*Kitchen window, 8:14 in the morning sun / Used to lean to catch my breath, now the day's
begun.*

She has said she went back and played it again because she assumed she'd misheard the time.

Then the second verse. *Post-rehab hand opens the drawer without the shake.*

She has described standing there holding a spoon and being, in her word, *furious.* Not
moved. Furious — that somebody had watched her. That there was no possible way somebody had
watched her. That the most private and most humiliating ninety seconds of her day had
apparently been happening in other kitchens, in other houses, to other people, for years, and
nobody had ever said so out loud.

**That was the moment everything changed** — and it did not feel like healing. It felt like
being caught.

---

Here's the turn, and she gets to say it herself, because she says it better.

I'm standing at the drawer and I'm not rehearsing. I notice about four seconds after it's
already open. My hand did it without me. And I don't feel triumphant, I feel *robbed* — like
something I'd been fighting for eleven months just walked out while I wasn't looking, and I
didn't get to win it.

I wanted the healing to be a fight I could tell people I won.

*The Healer doesn't patch — He knits me back through.* You can't schedule knitting. You can't
be brave at it. It happens underneath, in the dark, at a rate that has nothing to do with how
hard you're trying, and one Tuesday you find out it's been happening for a while.

I was so angry about that. I'm not anymore. It took about a year to stop being angry that I
didn't get to be the hero of it.

---

So — the pill bottle.

It's half-full and it's a legitimate prescription and she keeps it on the second shelf where
she can see it. Visitors sometimes go quiet when they notice.

She does not keep it as a warning. She does not keep it as a trophy, or a test, or a thing
she is proving she can be near.

She keeps it because it is the only object in the house that was there at 8:14 on the
mornings when nobody knew. It sat on that counter through every rehearsed reach and every
rattled spoon and it is, she says, the only witness she has.

*Pill bottle's half-full and it's not my center.*

Not gone. Not smashed in a dramatic scene. Still on the shelf, still half-full, still
perfectly capable of being what it was.

Just no longer the thing the morning is arranged around.

The drawer opens now. It opens the way a drawer opens.
