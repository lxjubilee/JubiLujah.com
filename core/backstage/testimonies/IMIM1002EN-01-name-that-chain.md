# In her family, the word was "tired."

Her grandmother was tired for thirty years. Her uncle was tired the whole autumn he did not
leave the house. She was raised bilingual — English, and this.

---

Nobody in that family lied. That's what made it impregnable.

If you had asked, at any point, anyone would have told you the truth in the approved
vocabulary. *Your uncle is going through a hard time. Your grandmother has her nerves. Your
cousin is finding his feet.* Every sentence accurate. Every sentence a door with no handle on
the outside.

It was a house language, and like any language it was learned young, and by nine she was
fluent enough to translate in both directions without noticing she was doing it.

**The ache is not the secrecy. It's that she reached thirty-four with no words for her own
interior at all.** She could describe a spreadsheet. She could describe a film. Asked what was
wrong she produced, involuntarily, the family word — *just tired* — and then sat in the car
afterward with her hands on the wheel knowing that a true sentence had been available and she
had not been able to find it.

She has said the frightening part was not being unable to tell anyone. It was suspecting she'd
lost the ability to tell *herself*, and that if the word didn't exist the thing might be
unreachable forever.

---

She heard the song at a women's meeting she had gone to reluctantly, standing near the back
where you stand when you are keeping your options open.

*Mark chapter five Jesus asked the demon "Name?"*

She has described the small, cold shock of hearing that framed as the *first* move. Not the
last resort. Not the thing you do after you're strong enough. The opening act.

Then verse two, which is just a list of people naming things:

*Aunt named the shame she carried since the divorce / Brother named the bottle that had been
his course / Sister named the silence that had locked her tongue.*

**That was the moment everything changed** — and it changed because of the third one.

The first two she'd heard before; shame and a bottle are things people testify about. Nobody
had ever told her that *the silence itself* could be the chain. In her family the silence
wasn't the problem. The silence was the good manners you maintained *around* the problem.

She sat down in the back row and did not stand up again for the rest of the meeting.

---

The turn, in her words.

I'd always thought I had to get better first and then I'd be able to talk about it. That's the
order I'd been given. Sort yourself out, then you can say it.

It's backwards. The whole song is about it being backwards.

I'm in a car park at half nine at night and I'm saying it out loud to nobody, in my own car,
with the engine off, and it takes me four tries because the family word keeps coming up
first — *tired, tired, tired* — and then finally I say the actual word.

And nothing happens. No lightning. I want to be clear about that because I'd half expected
something.

What happened was smaller and stranger: the thing got a size. For twenty years it had been
weather — everywhere, no edges, just the climate I lived in. And the second it had a name it
had a shape, and a shape has an outside, and if there's an outside then I am not the same
thing as it.

---

She made two appointments that week. One with a GP and one with her sister.

She is insistent that both go in this account, in that order, and that nobody reading it
imagines the word did the work of the medicine. She takes something now. It helps. She does
not consider that a failure of faith and gets noticeably short with people who hint that it
is.

*Addiction by its address — fear by its size.* An address is not a cure. An address is how the
help knows where to come.

---

Her grandmother is ninety-one and still says *tired,* and will, and that's allowed. Some
people are not going to learn a second language at ninety-one and it is not a granddaughter's
job to force it.

But at the family table last spring somebody asked her how she'd been, in the ordinary way,
expecting the ordinary answer.

And she is aware, as she opens her mouth, that eleven people are about to hear a word this
family does not say at a table, and that her mother is going to look at her plate.

She said it anyway. Plainly. One sentence, no explanation.

Her mother did look at her plate. Her cousin, twenty-two, sitting three seats down, did not —
she looked straight up.

Two weeks later that cousin called her, late, and said *I don't know how to start.*

She told her: it isn't a speech. It's one word, and you'll get it wrong three times first, and
the fourth one is the real one.

Yeshua — the One most of that table would call Jesus — asked a question He already knew the
answer to. She thinks about that more than anything else in the song.

He didn't need the name.

The man did.
