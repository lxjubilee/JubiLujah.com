# There were nine plans in the notebook. She'd like to talk about the tenth.

The tenth one is four words long and it took her eleven days to write it.

---

The notebook was a real notebook, spiral, the kind with the perforated edge, and it lived in
the bag that lived under the chair in room 4-East.

Inside it: nine contingencies.

If the counts came back low. If the counts came back low twice. If they moved him to the
other floor. If the insurance letter meant what she thought it meant. Who to call at the
school. Who to call at the school if the first person didn't answer. What to say to his
mother, and — separately, two pages later, in different ink — what to say to his mother if it
was the bad version.

She is a competent woman. This is what competence does when it is frightened. It makes lists.

And here is the ache, and it is not the fear. Everyone expects the fear.

**It's that the arranging was the only thing holding her up, and it had stopped working
around week three, and she kept doing it anyway.**

She'd sit in the chair at 2 a.m. adding a sub-clause to plan six. Not because plan six needed
it. Because if she stopped writing she'd have to just be a person sitting in a room where
something was happening that she could not administrate. The notebook wasn't preparation
anymore. It was a place to put her hands.

She has described it exactly like that. *A place to put my hands.*

---

The song came out of a nurse's phone. Not hers. The night nurse played it low at the station
and she heard it through a door left open, and she was annoyed, because she was on plan
seven.

*The army I have been fighting against was the army of my own arrangements.*

She got up and stood in the doorway to hear the rest of the verse, and the nurse — who had no
idea any of this was happening — turned it up a little without being asked.

*Philippians 2 in the hospital room, in the boardroom, in the bedroom.*

**That was the moment everything changed**, and what changed was one word. Not *surrender.*
She'd heard surrender her whole life and it had always sounded like losing, and she had a
child on the fourth floor and she was not going to lose.

The word was *creed.*

*The white flag is not a defeat — the white flag is a creed.*

---

The turn, in her words.

I thought the flag meant I'd stopped fighting for him. That's why I wouldn't put the notebook
down — putting it down felt like agreeing to something.

But you don't raise a creed because you've given up. You raise it because you've decided what
is true regardless of how it goes. Those are opposite things and I had them fused together
for thirty-nine years.

I'm standing in a hospital doorway at one in the morning and I'm working out that I can put
the pen down and still be for him. That the arranging was never what was holding him. That
I'd been carrying something that was never mine, and I'd gotten calluses from it —

and I could not tell you the last time I had asked Yeshua — the One most of the world knows
as Jesus — for anything that wasn't a logistics request.

---

She did not throw the notebook away. She wants that on the record, because she says the
throwing-away version is a movie and this was a Tuesday.

Plans one through nine are still in it. Some of them she used. Plan four turned out to be
necessary and she is glad she wrote it, and she does not think God was insulted by plan four.

It's the tenth entry that changed the book.

Eleven days after the doorway, on the next clean page, under nine pages of contingency, in
the same handwriting, she wrote:

**Jesus is Lord. Regardless.**

That's it. That's the tenth plan. No sub-clauses.

He's home now, and it's complicated, and there are appointments. The notebook is in a drawer
and she doesn't consult it.

But she says she still knows which page it's on, and that on the bad afternoons she doesn't
open the book — she just puts her hand flat on the outside of the drawer, which is a strange
thing for a competent woman to do, and does not care.

*He did not ask her to win. He asked her to yield.* The rest of the floor was asleep, and the
flag went up in a doorway, and nobody saw it but a night nurse who never knew what she'd done.
