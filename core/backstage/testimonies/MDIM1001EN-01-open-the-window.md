# She opened it four inches.

Not all the way. Four. There's a reason it was four and it takes the whole story to get there.

---

The curtains had been shut since December, and here is the part people get wrong: it was not a
decision.

Nobody stands in a room and resolves to stop opening the curtains. What happens is that one
morning you don't, because of a headache or a Tuesday, and nothing bad results — in fact
something in your chest unclenches slightly — and so the next morning you don't either, and by
about week five the closed curtain has stopped being a thing you do and become a thing that is
simply true about the room.

The room got smaller by degrees and each degree was reasonable.

She could still work. That's the detail people find hardest to hold. She was on calls, she was
competent, she was funny on the Thursday standup. She had simply stopped having any commerce
with the outdoors that wasn't strictly required, and had accumulated a wardrobe in which every
single item had long sleeves, and had developed a way of standing at the sink that meant she
never once faced the glass.

**The ache is not sadness. It's the administration again — the enormous quiet effort of
keeping the world at the exact distance where it can't reach you, while looking, to everyone
including yourself, like a person who is basically fine.**

She has described lying awake doing sums about how many days it had been. Not to fix it. Just
to know the number. It was one hundred and nine.

---

She was not listening to the song on purpose. It was a playlist, in the kitchen, while she was
doing something else, which is how most of the important things arrive.

*Counted the cracks where the light tried to slip.*

She has said she put down what she was holding.

Because that is a real thing she did — an actual, embarrassing, never-mentioned habit. There
is a gap where the two curtain panels don't quite meet, and it makes a bright vertical line on
the floor, and she had spent one hundred and nine mornings knowing exactly where that line
was and how it moved and what time it reached the rug.

She had never told anyone. There was no way to say it that didn't sound unwell. And a song in
her kitchen said it out loud like it was a normal thing that happens to people.

Then: *the latch is right here under my thumb.*

**That was the moment everything changed**, and what it changed was the size of the problem.

---

Here's the turn, in her words.

I'd been thinking about going outside. That was the project. Get out, see people, be a person
again — and every version of it was enormous, so I never started, and then I'd have the extra
day of not having started.

The song doesn't say go outside. It doesn't say be brave. It says *one brave second.* It says
*one small click.*

And I'm standing in the kitchen realizing the smallest possible unit of this is not leaving
the house. It's a latch. It's a piece of metal about the size of my thumb and it is four feet
away from me and it takes one second.

I hated it, honestly. I wanted the problem to be big, because a big problem excuses a hundred
and nine days. A latch does not excuse anything.

---

She opened it four inches because that is how far it went before the frame stuck, and she did
not force it, because forcing it would have been a project and projects were the enemy.

Four inches. January air. The sound of a car two streets over and someone's bin being dragged.

She stood there for about ninety seconds and then she closed it, and she has been very firm
that this is not the part where she felt better. She did not feel better. She felt cold and
slightly foolish and the flat smelled briefly of outside, which she had not missed and now
suddenly, violently, did.

The curtains stayed shut for two more days. Then she did it again.

---

That was some time ago now. She still owns the sleeves. All of them. She would like people to
stop expecting a bonfire.

What changed is not the illness — the illness has its own weather and comes and goes on terms
she does not set. What changed is that the window is now a thing that *opens.* It went back
into the category of ordinary objects. Some weeks it's open every day. Some weeks it isn't,
and she doesn't count anymore, and not counting is most of the victory.

The bright vertical line is still on the floor when the curtains are shut.

She just doesn't watch it cross the rug.
