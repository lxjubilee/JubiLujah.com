# The twelfth year, she didn't set the place.

She'd like you to know she thought about it for three days first. And she'd like you to know
what she did in the thirteenth year, which is the part she still can't explain.

---

You have to understand what setting the place actually involves, because from outside it
sounds like one plate.

It's one plate. It's also: the folding chair from the garage, brought up before anyone
arrives so nobody watches you carry it. It's the cousin who asks. It's the sister who doesn't
ask, which is worse. It's the moment somebody's new boyfriend goes to sit there and four
people say *no* at once, too fast, too loud, and then everyone eats.

Twelve Novembers.

And here is the ache underneath, which is not what people assume. It isn't missing him. She
was well practiced at missing him; missing him was Tuesday, missing him was the sound of a
motorcycle two streets over.

It's that **the chair had started to feel like a performance she was failing at.**

Somewhere around year nine it stopped being hope and became a thing she did so that nobody
could accuse her of giving up. She was setting a place for an audience. And she knew it. And
she hated it. And she got the chair out anyway, every year, because the alternative was a
sentence she wasn't willing to say out loud in front of her family.

So in year twelve she left it in the garage. Told no one. Sat down to a table that fit.

She has said it was the loneliest meal of her life, and that she does not regret it.

---

She heard the song the following spring, in a car, in a parking lot, not going anywhere.

*Fifteen years of prayer in the waiting season / Twelve Thanksgivings with the chair empty
for a reason.*

Twelve. She has described pulling the phone off the mount to look at it, as though the number
might change if she checked.

Then the line the whole record is built on — *He may not come exactly when you want Him to,
but He's always right on time.*

And she got angry. Genuinely, hands-on-the-wheel angry, which is not what a gospel record
expects from you in a parking lot. Because she had heard that line her entire life, always
delivered warmly, always by someone whose chair was full, and it had always landed as *your
timing is wrong and your grief is impatience.*

**That was the moment everything changed**, and it changed by going the opposite direction
from comfort.

She stayed in the parking lot and worked out that she had been reading the promise
backwards for twelve years.

---

Here's the turn, in her words.

I'd always heard it as *He's coming, be patient.* Like the whole verse was a delivery
estimate.

It isn't. Habakkuk doesn't say the waiting is short and it doesn't say the waiting is easy
and it *does not say what the answer will be.* It says the vision has an appointed time. It
says wait for it. That's all it ever said.

I'd turned a Person into a schedule and then I'd gotten furious at the schedule for being
late. And the entire time nobody had promised me a date. They'd promised me He'd be there.

I'm sitting in a parking lot realizing I've been waiting for an *event* for twelve years, and
that's why every November felt like a failed exam.

---

He has not come home.

That's true as of this writing, and this account is not going to arrange a knock at the door
to make itself land better. Some chairs stay empty. Anybody who tells you a song fixes that
is selling something, and this one isn't for sale.

But in the thirteenth November she went out to the garage and got the folding chair.

Not because she had news. Not because anything had changed, and not — she is very clear on
this — because she'd decided to have more faith about it.

She got it because the chair had finally stopped being about whether he'd walk in.

She set it, and somebody's new boyfriend went to sit down, and she said *go ahead, sit there,*
and everybody at that table stopped chewing.

The place is still set. It just isn't a performance anymore. It's the thing she does to say
she hasn't closed the account, and she's the only one it has to convince.

*He may not come when you want Him to.* She'd sung along with that line for a year before she
noticed it doesn't actually specify who *He* is in that sentence.

She thinks now it was never the son.
